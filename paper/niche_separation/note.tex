\begin{abstract}
Baek proved that Gerver's sofa is the maximum-area moving sofa for a corridor with one
corner, by writing a sofa as a convex cap minus a niche and obtaining optimality from the
concavity of a quadratic upper bound. We carry that architecture to the ambidextrous
moving sofa problem, which is open, and to Romik's candidate $\Sig$ of area
$A_R^\ast=1.6449552184\ldots$

The niche of an ambidextrous sofa is expressed exactly in convex-linear data: writing
$H(\theta)=h_K(\mu_\theta)$ for the cap's support function, the two arms
$\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ and the reach $\sigma$ are affine in $H$, and
$|N|=\int_0^{\pi/2}[\tfrac12(\alpha_2^+)^2+\tfrac12(\sigma-\alpha_1)^2
-\tfrac12(\alpha_1^-)^2]\,dt$ whenever the associated sweep is injective. Injectivity
follows from a single linear condition (RC), that the cap's radius of curvature be at most
the corridor width; the proof reduces each of the three required statements to a forced
harmonic oscillator whose kernel is non-negative because the rotation angle is $\pi/2$.
(RC) is classical --- by the dual of Blaschke's rolling theorem it says the cap slides
freely inside a unit ball, up to the corridor facets --- so what is new is its use.

For the functional $Q=|C_2|-2V$ we prove $Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$; that $\delta Q(\Sig)=0$, so
Romik's ODE families are exactly the critical-point equations of $Q$, identically in his
constants; and that $\delta^2Q<0$, with explicit constants. On the convex domain
$\mathcal D$ cut out by (RC), a separation inequality and $\Sig$'s own sign pattern, this
gives
\[
  |T|\ \le\ A_R^\ast-\tfrac{371}{1000}\|H-H_\Sig\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi/2)}
\]
for every ambidextrous sofa $T$ with cap data in $\mathcal D$, and $\Sig$ as the unique
member attaining $A_R^\ast$. The domain is a $C^2$-neighbourhood of $\Sig$: its radius
along $\sin(2k\theta)$ scales like $k^{-2}$.

Two limits are recorded rather than smoothed over. The separation inequality is
independent of (RC) and cannot be removed. And $V$ is the exact flux of the advancing
wedge boundary, so $V\ge|N|$ by Reynolds' transport theorem and $Q$ bounds nothing where
the sweep fails to be injective; in particular the construction does not recover Gerver's
sofa, whose cap violates (RC). Optimality of $\Sig$ remains open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setting}

Write $L$ for the closed L-shaped hallway of unit width with its corner at the
origin, $\mu_t=(\cos t,\sin t)$, $\nu_t=(-\sin t,\cos t)$, and for a rotation
path $c:[0,\pi/2]\to\R^2$ let
\[
  C_t=\{p:\langle p-c(t),\mu_t\rangle\le 1\}\cap\{p:\langle p-c(t),\nu_t\rangle\le 1\},
  \qquad
  Q_t=\{p:\langle p-c(t),\mu_t\rangle<0\}\cap\{p:\langle p-c(t),\nu_t\rangle<0\},
\]
so that the hallway in position $t$ is $H_t=C_t\setminus Q_t$. The one-sided
sofa is $S=\bigcap_t H_t$, and writing $C=\bigcap_t C_t$ and
$U=\bigcup_t Q_t$ we have $S=C\setminus U$ with $C$ convex.

Let $\rho(x,y)=(x,1-y)$ be the reflection in the line $y=\tfrac12$. Romik's
ambidextrous sofa is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma}
  \Sig=S\cap\rho(S)=C_2\setminus\bigl(U\cup\rho U\bigr),
  \qquad C_2:=C\cap\rho C \text{ convex},
\end{equation}
with $|\Sig|=A_R^\ast=1.6449552184\ldots$, and $c$ is Romik's piecewise path with
breakpoints at $\beta$ and $\pi/2-\beta$, where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta}
  \beta=\arctanop\Bigl(\tfrac12\bigl(\sqrt[3]{\sqrt2+1}-\sqrt[3]{\sqrt2-1}\bigr)\Bigr)
      =0.2896538208\ldots
\end{equation}

Baek's argument for the one-corner problem builds an overestimating region whose
area is a quadratic functional on a convex domain of convex bodies, and proves it
concave by exhibiting it as a linear functional minus Mamikon regions, whose areas
are convex quadratics. Concavity reduces global optimality to a first-order
condition. The construction is organised around $S=K\setminus\mathcal N(K)$: one
cap, one niche.

By \eqref{eq:sigma} the ambidextrous analogue has \emph{two} niches, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ie}
  |U\cup\rho U|=|U|+|\rho U|-|U\cap\rho U|.
\end{equation}
The last term appears in the upper bound with a $+$ sign. Since concavity is
obtained by \emph{subtracting} convex quadratics, an added overlap term is not
covered by the mechanism. The purpose of this note is Theorem~\ref{thm:main}:
the term is zero.

\section{The niches are disjoint}

\begin{lemma}[Niche ceiling]\label{lem:ceiling}
For every $t\in[0,\pi/2]$ we have $Q_t\subseteq\{(x,y):y\le c_y(t)\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$Q_t$ is the open convex cone with apex $c(t)$ spanned by $-\mu_t$ and $-\nu_t$,
so $q\in Q_t$ means $q-c(t)=-a\mu_t-b\nu_t$ with $a,b>0$. For $t\in[0,\pi/2]$
both $\sin t\ge0$ and $\cos t\ge0$, hence
\[
  q_y-c_y(t)=-a\sin t-b\cos t\le 0. \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:sep}
Put $M:=\max_{t\in[0,\pi/2]}c_y(t)$. Then $U\subseteq\{y\le M\}$ and
$\rho U\subseteq\{y\ge 1-M\}$. If $M<\tfrac12$ then $U\cap\rho U=\emptyset$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first inclusion is Lemma~\ref{lem:ceiling} applied to each $t$; the second is
its image under $\rho$. If $M<\tfrac12$ then $1-M>\tfrac12>M$, so the two
half-planes are disjoint.
\end{proof}

It remains to evaluate $M$ for Romik's path. On the middle piece
$[\beta,\pi/2-\beta]$ Romik's path is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:x6}
  c(t)=R(t)v(t)+\kappa,\qquad
  v(t)=\begin{pmatrix} f_1\cos\frac t2+f_2\sin\frac t2-1\\[2pt]
                      -f_2\cos\frac t2+f_1\sin\frac t2-1\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with $R(t)$ the rotation by $t$, and the two facts we use are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kf}
  \kappa_y=\tfrac12 \quad\text{exactly},
  \qquad f_2=(1-\sqrt2)\,f_1 .
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:cy}
For $t\in[\beta,\pi/2-\beta]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cy}
  c_y(t)-\tfrac12
   = f_1\sin\tfrac{3t}{2}-f_2\cos\tfrac{3t}{2}-(\sin t+\cos t)
   = f_1\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2}\,\sin\!\Bigl(\tfrac{3t}{2}+\tfrac\pi8\Bigr)-\sqrt2\,\sin\!\Bigl(t+\tfrac\pi4\Bigr).
\end{equation}
In particular $c_y$ is symmetric about $t=\pi/4$ on this interval, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M}
  M=c_y(\pi/4)=\tfrac12-\Bigl(\sqrt2-f_1\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:x6} and $\kappa_y=\tfrac12$, $c_y(t)-\tfrac12=\sin t\,v_x+\cos t\,v_y$.
Write $S=\sin\frac t2$, $C=\cos\frac t2$, so $\sin t=2SC$ and $\cos t=C^2-S^2$.
Expanding and collecting the coefficients of $f_1$ and $f_2$ gives
\[
  \sin t\,v_x+\cos t\,v_y
  = f_1 S(3C^2-S^2)+f_2 C(3S^2-C^2)-(\sin t+\cos t),
\]
and the triple-angle identities $S(3C^2-S^2)=\sin\frac{3t}{2}$,
$C(3S^2-C^2)=-\cos\frac{3t}{2}$ give the first equality in \eqref{eq:cy}. For the
second, substitute $f_2=(1-\sqrt2)f_1$ from \eqref{eq:kf}: the bracket becomes
$\sin\frac{3t}{2}+(\sqrt2-1)\cos\frac{3t}{2}$, of amplitude
$\sqrt{1+(\sqrt2-1)^2}=\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2}$ and phase $\arctanop(\sqrt2-1)=\pi/8$.

Both sinusoids in \eqref{eq:cy} are invariant under $t\mapsto\pi/2-t$: indeed
$\sin(\tfrac{3}{2}(\pi/2-t)+\pi/8)=\sin(7\pi/8-\tfrac{3t}{2})=\sin(\pi/8+\tfrac{3t}{2})$
and $\sin(3\pi/4-t)=\sin(\pi/4+t)$. Hence $c_y$ is symmetric about $\pi/4$, where
both sinusoids attain the value $1$ simultaneously; \eqref{eq:M} follows. \end{proof}

That $\pi/4$ maximises $c_y$ over all of $[0,\pi/2]$, and not merely over the
middle piece, follows from the corresponding closed form on the outer pieces.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:outer}
For $t\in[0,\beta]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cyouter}
  c_y(t)=\tfrac12+a_1\sin 2t-\sin t-\tfrac12\cos t,
\end{equation}
and $c_y$ is strictly increasing there, so $\max_{[0,\beta]}c_y=c_y(\beta)$. By
the symmetry $c_y(t)=c_y(\pi/2-t)$ the same bound holds on $[\pi/2-\beta,\pi/2]$.
Consequently $M=c_y(\pi/4)$ as claimed in \eqref{eq:M}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On $[0,\beta)$ Romik's path is $c(t)=R(t)v_1(t)+\kappa^{(1)}$ with
$v_1(t)=(a_1\cos t-1,\,a_1\sin t-\tfrac12)$ and $\kappa^{(1)}_y=\tfrac12$, so
$c_y(t)-\tfrac12=\sin t\,v_{1x}+\cos t\,v_{1y}=2a_1\sin t\cos t-\sin t-\tfrac12\cos t$,
which is \eqref{eq:cyouter}. Differentiating,
\[
  c_y'(t)=2a_1\cos 2t-\cos t+\tfrac12\sin t
        \ \ge\ 2a_1\cos 2\beta-1\ =\ 0.4650\ldots>0
        \qquad (t\in[0,\beta]),
\]
using $\cos 2t\ge\cos 2\beta$ and $\cos t-\tfrac12\sin t\le 1$ for $t\ge0$.
Hence $c_y$ is strictly increasing on $[0,\beta]$, with
$c_y(\beta)=0.2143795161\ldots$, which is below
$c_y(\pi/4)=0.3878381292\ldots$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Evaluating \eqref{eq:cyouter} and \eqref{eq:cy} at $t=\beta$ gives the same value
to $4.4\cdot 10^{-31}$ at $30$-digit precision. Since the two expressions were
derived from different pieces of Romik's path, this is an independent check on
both, and on \eqref{eq:f1}.
\end{remark}

\section{A closed form for $f_1$}

Romik's constant $f_1$ is recorded in the literature as the decimal
$1.202938908156911389$. The following determines it in closed form. Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:a1}
  a_1=\tfrac14\sqrt{\,4+\sqrt[3]{71+8\sqrt2}+\sqrt[3]{71-8\sqrt2}\,}
     =0.8752873624\ldots
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:f1}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:f1}
  f_1=\frac{\tfrac43\,a_1\cos\beta}{\cos\frac\beta2+(1-\sqrt2)\sin\frac\beta2}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On $[0,\beta)$ Romik's path is $c(t)=R(t)v_1(t)+\kappa^{(1)}$ with
$v_1(t)=(a_1\cos t-1,\;a_1\sin t-\tfrac12)$ and
$\kappa^{(1)}=(1-a_1,\tfrac12)$; on $[\beta,\pi/2-\beta]$ it is \eqref{eq:x6}
with $\kappa=(1-\tfrac43 a_1,\tfrac12)$. Both $\kappa$'s have second coordinate
$\tfrac12$ and their first coordinates differ by exactly $\tfrac13 a_1$.
Continuity at $t=\beta$ therefore gives
$R(\beta)\bigl(v_1(\beta)-v(\beta)\bigr)=(-\tfrac13a_1,0)$, so
\[
  v_1(\beta)-v(\beta)=R(-\beta)\bigl(-\tfrac13a_1,0\bigr)
     =\bigl(-\tfrac13a_1\cos\beta,\;\tfrac13a_1\sin\beta\bigr).
\]
Reading the first coordinate and using $f_2=(1-\sqrt2)f_1$,
\[
  \bigl(a_1\cos\beta-1\bigr)-\Bigl(f_1\bigl(\cos\tfrac\beta2+(1-\sqrt2)\sin\tfrac\beta2\bigr)-1\Bigr)
   =-\tfrac13a_1\cos\beta,
\]
which rearranges to \eqref{eq:f1}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Evaluating \eqref{eq:f1} at $50$ digits gives
$f_1=1.202938908156911389070223\ldots$, so the tabulated decimal is its rounding.
As an independent check, the \emph{second} coordinate of the junction relation,
which was not used in the derivation, is satisfied to $1.7\cdot10^{-51}$ at the
same precision.
\end{remark}

\section{Main theorem}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For Romik's ambidextrous sofa, $U\cap\rho U=\emptyset$, and consequently
\[
  |\Sig|=|C_2|-|U|-|\rho U|=|C_2|-2|U| .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:cy}, $M<\tfrac12$ is equivalent to
$f_1\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2}<\sqrt2$, i.e. to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ineq}
  f_1^2<\frac{2+\sqrt2}{2}.
\end{equation}
With $f_1$ given by \eqref{eq:f1} in terms of the algebraic numbers
\eqref{eq:beta} and \eqref{eq:a1}, both sides of \eqref{eq:ineq} are explicit
algebraic numbers, and
\[
  f_1^2=1.4470620167577420940\ldots,
  \qquad \frac{2+\sqrt2}{2}=1.7071067811865475244\ldots,
\]
so \eqref{eq:ineq} holds with margin $0.2600447644\ldots$. Corollary~\ref{cor:sep}
gives $U\cap\rho U=\emptyset$, and then \eqref{eq:ie} together with
\eqref{eq:sigma}. Finally $|\rho U|=|U|$ because $\rho$ is an isometry.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
$M=0.3878381292441942964\ldots$, the two niches are separated by the horizontal
band of width $1-2M=0.2243237415116114\ldots$ about $y=\tfrac12$, and the
ambidextrous upper bound requires only \emph{one} niche to be modelled: one core
and two tails, the same shape as in the one-corner problem.
\end{corollary}

\section{The quotient reduction and a conditional statement}

Theorem~\ref{thm:main} has a consequence that changes the shape of the
ambidextrous problem. Since $U\subseteq\{y\le M\}$ with $M<\tfrac12$, the whole
lower niche lies below the symmetry axis, so intersecting with the lower
half-strip deletes $\rho U$ outright.

\begin{corollary}[Quotient reduction]\label{cor:quot}
Put $K^-:=C_2\cap\{y\le\tfrac12\}$, a convex body. Then
\[
  \Sig\cap\{y\le\tfrac12\}=K^-\setminus U,
  \qquad
  |\Sig|=2\bigl(|K^-|-|U\cap K^-|\bigr).
\]
\end{corollary}

That is a convex cap minus \emph{one} niche: the shape Baek's argument is built
on. Numerically, at $n=1441$ hallways, $|\Sig|=1.645059395$ and
$|\Sig\cap\{y\le\frac12\}|=0.822529698$, whose double reproduces $|\Sig|$ to
$3.8\cdot10^{-15}$.

Two cautions must be recorded, because they bound what
Corollary~\ref{cor:quot} delivers.

First, $M<\tfrac12$ is a property of Romik's trajectory, not of an arbitrary
competitor. A competitor with $M\ge\tfrac12$ has overlapping niches, and by
\eqref{eq:ie} overlap \emph{increases} $|\Sig|$; so it cannot be assumed away on
extremal grounds.

Second --- and this is what makes the reduction usable --- connectedness does
appear to exclude it, but not for the reason one first tries. A \emph{single}
pair $Q_t\cup\rho Q_t$ removes a full vertical segment, and hence disconnects any
body meeting both sides of it, exactly when the apex height exceeds a threshold
$h^\ast(t)$; and computation gives $h^\ast(t)>\tfrac12$ throughout $(0,\pi/2)$,
with $h^\ast(t)\to\tfrac12$ as $t\to0$, $h^\ast(\pi/4)=0.5100$ and
$h^\ast(1.52)=0.6967$. So no single-$t$ argument forces $M\le\tfrac12$; all it
gives is the pointwise constraint $c_y(t)\le h^\ast(t)$.

In fact a single pair does force it, and the earlier reading to the contrary was
an artifact of testing the vertical line at a fixed distance from the apex.

\begin{theorem}[Connectedness ceiling]\label{thm:ceiling}
Let $p=c(t_0)$ with $t_0\in(0,\pi/2)$ and suppose $p_y>\tfrac12$. Put
\[
  \varepsilon_0:=\frac{2p_y-1}{2\tan t_0}>0 .
\]
Then for every $\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_0)$ the vertical line $x=p_x-\varepsilon$
is contained in $Q_{t_0}\cup\rho Q_{t_0}$. Hence any ambidextrous moving sofa omits
the open vertical strip $\{p_x-\varepsilon_0<x<p_x\}$, and a connected one lies
entirely in one of the two half-planes it separates.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and consider $q=(p_x-\varepsilon,y)$. The set $Q_{t_0}$ is the
open cone at $p$ spanned by $-\mu_{t_0}$ and $-\nu_{t_0}$, whose directions occupy
the angular interval $[t_0+\pi,\,t_0+\tfrac{3\pi}{2}]$. Writing $h:=p_y-y$, the
direction $q-p=(-\varepsilon,-h)$ has angle $\pi+\arctan(h/\varepsilon)$ when
$h>0$, so $q\in Q_{t_0}$ iff $\arctan(h/\varepsilon)\ge t_0$, that is iff
\[
  y\le p_y-\varepsilon\tan t_0 .
\]
Applying $\rho$ and using $\rho q-\rho p=R(q-p)$ with $R=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$,
the reflected cone $\rho Q_{t_0}$ has apex $(p_x,1-p_y)$ and occupies the angular
interval $[\tfrac\pi2-t_0,\,\pi-t_0]$, giving by the same computation
\[
  q\in\rho Q_{t_0}\iff y\ge 1-p_y+\varepsilon\tan t_0 .
\]
The two sets therefore cover the whole line exactly when
$p_y-\varepsilon\tan t_0\ge 1-p_y+\varepsilon\tan t_0$, i.e. when
$2\varepsilon\tan t_0\le 2p_y-1$, i.e. when $\varepsilon\le\varepsilon_0$. Since
$p_y>\tfrac12$ we have $\varepsilon_0>0$ and the range is nonempty. Taking the
union over $\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_0)$ gives the strip.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The criterion is sharp in both directions: if $p_y\le\tfrac12$ then
$\varepsilon_0\le0$ and the two cones leave a gap at \emph{every} distance from the
apex, which is why $\tfrac12$ is exactly the threshold. It also explains a
numerical trap: $\varepsilon_0$ can be very small --- at $t_0=1.3$, $p_y=0.55$ it is
$0.0139$ --- so sampling the line at a fixed distance such as $0.02$ detects no
covering and suggests, wrongly, that a single pair never separates. The criterion
was checked against direct computation at seven pairs $(t_0,p_y)$ on both sides of
$\varepsilon_0$, and at three pairs with $p_y<\tfrac12$.
\end{remark}

Granting that an ambidextrous sofa cannot lie in one of the two half-planes
separated by such a strip --- it must meet both arms of the hallway
$H_{t_0}$ whose inner corner is $p$ --- Theorem~\ref{thm:ceiling} yields
$\max_t c_y(t)\le\tfrac12$ for every connected ambidextrous moving sofa, and the
hypothesis of the next proposition is automatic.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional form]\label{prop:cond}
Let $S$ be an ambidextrous moving sofa with rotation path $c$ satisfying
$\max_t c_y(t)\le\tfrac12$. Then its two niches are disjoint and
$|S|=2(|K^-|-|U\cap K^-|)$ with $K^-$ convex and $U$ a single niche. In
particular the area functional of the ambidextrous problem restricted to this
class is of the cap-minus-one-niche form to which the concavity method applies.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:ceiling} the hypothesis holds for every connected
ambidextrous moving sofa, so the conclusion is unconditional in that class.
\end{proposition}

We emphasise what is \emph{not} claimed: no optimality assertion follows from
Proposition~\ref{prop:cond} alone. Baek's route additionally requires an
existence and regularity step (a maximum monotone sofa of rotation angle $\pi/2$
satisfying an injectivity condition), and his balancing argument for that step
does not transfer, for a reason worth recording. His argument turns on a
coincidence: the derivative of the area functional under pushing an edge is
$\sigma(t)-\tau(t)$, and the same combination is forced to sum to zero because
the cap boundary and the niche polyline join the \emph{same} pair of endpoints.
In the quotient, $\partial K^-$ acquires the horizontal cut at $y=\tfrac12$,
where the polyline contributes nothing, and the identity becomes
$\sum_{t\ne\mathrm{cut}}(\sigma-\tau)(v_t\cdot u_0)=\sigma_{\mathrm{cut}}>0$,
which is consistent with $\sigma\le\tau$ throughout. One obtains an inequality
where Baek obtains an equality, and it is the equality that his argument
consumes.

\section{A relation between Romik's constants}\label{sec:cubics}

The phase junctions of $\Sig$ admit a characterisation that ties two of Romik's
constants together. Recall $a_1$ from \eqref{eq:a1} and $\beta$ from
\eqref{eq:beta}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:a1beta}
$4a_1\sin\beta=1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $u:=\sqrt[3]{\sqrt2+1}-\sqrt[3]{\sqrt2-1}$, so that $\tan\beta=u/2$ by
\eqref{eq:beta} and hence $\sin\beta=u/\sqrt{4+u^{2}}$, and
$w:=\sqrt[3]{71+8\sqrt2}+\sqrt[3]{71-8\sqrt2}$, so that $a_1=\tfrac14\sqrt{4+w}$
by \eqref{eq:a1}. Then
\[
  4a_1\sin\beta=\frac{u\sqrt{4+w}}{\sqrt{4+u^{2}}},
\]
so the assertion is equivalent to $u^{2}(4+w)=4+u^{2}$, that is to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:key}
  u^{2}\,(3+w)=4 .
\end{equation}

Put $p:=\sqrt[3]{\sqrt2+1}$ and $q:=\sqrt[3]{\sqrt2-1}$. Then $pq=1$ and
$p^{3}-q^{3}=2$, so
\[
  u^{3}=(p-q)^{3}=p^{3}-q^{3}-3pq(p-q)=2-3u,
\]
i.e.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ucubic}
  u^{3}+3u=2 .
\end{equation}
Set $x:=u^{2}$. Then \eqref{eq:ucubic} reads $u(x+3)=2$; squaring and using
$u^{2}=x$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xcubic}
  x(x+3)^{2}=4,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad x^{3}+6x^{2}+9x-4=0 .
\end{equation}
Now let $W:=4/x-3$. Clearing denominators,
\[
  W^{3}-51W-142
   =\frac{(4-3x)^{3}-51(4-3x)x^{2}-142x^{3}}{x^{3}}
   =\frac{-16\bigl(x^{3}+6x^{2}+9x-4\bigr)}{x^{3}}=0
\]
by \eqref{eq:xcubic}, the middle equality being the polynomial identity
$(4-3x)^{3}-51(4-3x)x^{2}-142x^{3}=-16(x^{3}+6x^{2}+9x-4)$.

On the other hand $w$ satisfies the same cubic: with
$A:=\sqrt[3]{71+8\sqrt2}$, $B:=\sqrt[3]{71-8\sqrt2}$ one has
$AB=\sqrt[3]{71^{2}-128}=\sqrt[3]{4913}=17$ and $A^{3}+B^{3}=142$, so
$w^{3}=A^{3}+B^{3}+3AB\,w=142+51w$. The cubic $W^{3}-51W-142$ has discriminant
$-4(-51)^{3}-27(-142)^{2}=530604-544428=-13824<0$ and therefore exactly one real
root; both $4/x-3$ and $w$ are real roots, so $4/x-3=w$. This is
\eqref{eq:key}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:ucubic}
$2\tan\beta$ is the real root of $u^{3}+3u=2$ --- equivalently
$4\tan^{3}\beta+3\tan\beta=1$, the cubic already recorded in the literature --- and
\[
  \sqrt[3]{71+8\sqrt2}+\sqrt[3]{71-8\sqrt2}=u^{4}+6u^{2}+6 .
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first assertion is \eqref{eq:ucubic}. For the second, \eqref{eq:xcubic} gives
$(x+3)^{2}=4/x$, so $w=4/x-3=(x+3)^{2}-3=x^{2}+6x+6$ with $x=u^{2}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the junction statement]
On $[0,\beta)$ Romik's path is $c(t)=R_tv(t)+\kappa^{(1)}$ with
$v(t)=(a_1\cos t-1,\;a_1\sin t-\tfrac12)$. Differentiating,
$c'=R_t(Jv+v')$ with $J$ the rotation by $\pi/2$, and since $\mu_t=R_te_1$ the
frame component is
\[
  c'(t)\cdot\mu_t=(Jv+v')_x=-v_y-a_1\sin t
   =-\bigl(a_1\sin t-\tfrac12\bigr)-a_1\sin t=\tfrac12-2a_1\sin t .
\]
This vanishes precisely when $\sin t=1/(4a_1)$. On the other hand the phase
boundary of $\Sig$ at $t=\beta$ is where the SOL1 phase ends, and by
Proposition~\ref{prop:outer} the two closed forms for $c_y$ agree there; the same
computation on the SOL5 phase gives $c'\cdot\nu_t=2a_1\cos t-\tfrac12$, vanishing
when $\cos t=1/(4a_1)$, i.e. at $t=\pi/2-\beta$. Since the two junctions are
$\beta$ and $\pi/2-\beta$ and $\cos(\pi/2-\beta)=\sin\beta$, both conditions
reduce to $\sin\beta=1/(4a_1)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Attribution]
Neither \eqref{eq:ucubic} nor \eqref{eq:xcubic} is new. The standard description of
$\Sig$ gives its area as $x+\arctan y$ where $x^{2}(x+3)=8$ and $y(4y^{2}+3)=1$;
the second of these is exactly \eqref{eq:ucubic} with $y=\tan\beta=u/2$, and the
first is \eqref{eq:xcubic} shifted, since $x=u^{2}+1$ turns
$x^{3}+3x^{2}-8$ into $u^{6}+6u^{4}+9u^{2}-4$. Equivalently
\[
  A_R^\ast=u^{2}+1+\arctan(u/2),\qquad u^{3}+3u=2,
\]
so a single algebraic number generates both parts of the area. What we have not
found stated is the relation $4a_1\sin\beta=1$ of
Theorem~\ref{thm:a1beta} between the shape constant $a_1$ and $\beta$, nor
$w=u^{4}+6u^{2}+6$; but both are elementary consequences of the two standard
cubics, and we make no priority claim. Every step was checked numerically at $60$
digits, and the two polynomial steps are machine-checked (\verb|u_sq_cubic|,
\verb|cardano_substitution|).
\end{remark}

Theorem~\ref{thm:a1beta} has a consequence for the transfer of Baek's method.
His argument requires an \emph{injectivity condition} on the rotation path,
namely $c'(t)\cdot\mu_t<0$ and $c'(t)\cdot\nu_t>0$ throughout $(0,\pi/2)$. By the
computation above, for $\Sig$ these quantities vanish exactly at $\beta$ and
$\pi/2-\beta$ and have the wrong sign outside $[\beta,\pi/2-\beta]$: at
$t=0.05$ one finds $c'\cdot\mu_t=+0.4125$. So $\Sig$ satisfies the injectivity
condition on its middle phase and violates it on both outer phases, and the
failure boundary is exactly the phase junction.

\begin{remark}[Where the difficulty of the ambidextrous problem sits]\label{rem:degen}
The outer phases $[0,\beta)$ and $(\pi/2-\beta,\pi/2]$ are also where the contact
arcs of $\partial\Sig$ are \emph{constant}, pinned at the $\rho$-fixed point
$(1,\tfrac12)$ to $3\cdot10^{-31}$: there the supporting hallway touches
$\Sig$ along a segment rather than at a point. Three separate obstructions
therefore coincide on those phases --- the contact structure degenerates, second
variation arguments lose the contact point, and the injectivity hypothesis of the
one-corner proof fails.
\end{remark}

\section{The ambidextrous problem as a single family of angle range \texorpdfstring{$\pi$}{pi}}

Finally we record a reformulation which explains why the transfer is delicate.
Since $\rho$ carries the hallway at angle $s$ to the hallway at angle $-s$ with
inner corner $\rho c(s)$, we have
$\bigcap_{t\in[-\pi/2,0]}H_t=\rho S$ and hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pirange}
  \Sig=\bigcap_{t\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]}H_t,\qquad c(-t)=\rho c(t).
\end{equation}
Numerically the two constructions have symmetric difference exactly $0$. So the
ambidextrous constraint family is a \emph{single} family spanning an angle range
of $\pi$, rather than two families of range $\pi/2$; and its corner path is
discontinuous at $t=0$, where $c_y(0)=0$ while $\rho c(0)$ has height $1$. Both
features lie outside the setting of the one-corner argument, which is developed
for rotation angle $\omega\le\pi/2$ and whose maximisers attain $\omega=\pi/2$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:pirange} is thus a reformulation, not a reduction: it exhibits
the ambidextrous problem inside a larger class rather than inside the solved one.

\section{The niche area in convex-linear data}\label{sec:mamikon}

The obstruction recorded in Remark~\ref{rem:degen} is that the one-corner
argument's injectivity condition fails for $\Sig$ on the two outer phases. We now
show that the failure is an artefact of how the condition is stated, and derive an
exact formula for the niche area whose every ingredient is convex-linear in the
cap.

Throughout, $K$ is the cap, $h=h_K$ its support function, and
\[
  F(t):=h(\mu_t),\qquad G(t):=h(\nu_t).
\]

\begin{lemma}[The corner is affine in the support function]\label{lem:corner}
Place the hallway at angle $t$ and push both outer walls inward until they touch
$K$. Then the inner corner sits at
\begin{equation}\label{eq:corner}
  c(t)=\bigl(F(t)-1\bigr)\mu_t+\bigl(G(t)-1\bigr)\nu_t ,
\end{equation}
and the removed wedge is
$Q_t=\{\langle p,\mu_t\rangle<F(t)-1\}\cap\{\langle p,\nu_t\rangle<G(t)-1\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The two outer walls are the lines with outer normals $\mu_t,\nu_t$; touching $K$
means their offsets are $F(t),G(t)$. The corner lies one unit inside each, so
$\langle c,\mu_t\rangle=F-1$ and $\langle c,\nu_t\rangle=G-1$, which is
\eqref{eq:corner} since $(\mu_t,\nu_t)$ is orthonormal. The wedge is the
intersection of the two inner half-planes through $c$.
\end{proof}

Since $h\mapsto h$ is linear under Minkowski sum, \eqref{eq:corner} makes $c(t)$,
and hence each of
\begin{align}
  \alpha_1(t)&:=-\langle c'(t),\mu_t\rangle=G(t)-1-F'(t),\label{eq:a1}\\
  \alpha_2(t)&:=\ \ \langle c'(t),\nu_t\rangle=F(t)-1+G'(t),\label{eq:a2}\\
  \sigma(t)&:=c_y(t)/\cos t=\bigl(F(t)-1\bigr)\tan t+G(t)-1,\label{eq:sig}
\end{align}
an affine-linear functional of $h$, hence convex-linear on a domain of convex
bodies closed under Minkowski sum. The wedge $Q_t$ has two faces, the rays
$c-s\nu_t$ (face~1) and $c-s\mu_t$ (face~2), and $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ are the
signed distances from $c$ to the envelope points on them: the one-corner
injectivity condition is exactly $\alpha_1,\alpha_2>0$.

\begin{lemma}[Normal velocities]\label{lem:normvel}
The face-$1$ line advances in its own normal direction with speed $s-\alpha_1(t)$
at the point at distance $s$ from the corner, and the face-$2$ line with speed
$\alpha_2(t)-s$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Face~1 lies on $\ell_t=\{\langle p,\mu_t\rangle=F(t)-1\}$. For $p$ fixed the
signed gap to $\ell_t$ is $F(t)-1-\langle p,\mu_t\rangle$, whose $t$-derivative is
$F'(t)-\langle p,\nu_t\rangle$ because $\mu_t'=\nu_t$. At $p=c-s\nu_t$ we have
$\langle p,\nu_t\rangle=G-1-s$, so the speed is $F'-G+1+s=s-\alpha_1$ by
\eqref{eq:a1}. Face~2 lies on $\{\langle p,\nu_t\rangle=G(t)-1\}$ and
$\nu_t'=-\mu_t$, so the same computation gives $G'+\langle p,\mu_t\rangle
=G'+F-1-s=\alpha_2-s$ at $p=c-s\mu_t$.
\end{proof}

So the niche is created by the \emph{inner} part of face~2 and the \emph{outer}
part of face~1, and \emph{no sign hypothesis on $\alpha_1$ is involved}: where
$\alpha_1<0$ the whole visible face~1 advances, and where $\alpha_2\le0$ face~2
contributes nothing. This is the promised repair of Remark~\ref{rem:degen}: each
face is used only on the range where its own arm has the right sign, and the
reported failure came from requiring both arms simultaneously.

Write $W_2$ for the region swept by the segments $[c(t),c(t)-\alpha_2(t)^+\mu_t]$,
and $W_1^{\mathrm{out}}$ for the region swept by the face-1 segments running from
$c(t)-\alpha_1(t)^+\nu_t$ outward to the corridor floor $\{y=0\}$; note the face-1
direction $-\nu_t=(\sin t,-\cos t)$ points downward, and the floor is reached at
distance exactly $\sigma(t)$, which is why \eqref{eq:sig} is the relevant reach.

\begin{proposition}[Niche area in convex-linear data]\label{prop:V}
If the two sweeps are disjoint, injective and cover the niche, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:V}
  |N|=\int_0^{\pi/2}\Bigl[\tfrac12\bigl(\alpha_2^+\bigr)^2
      +\tfrac12\bigl(\sigma-\alpha_1\bigr)^2
      -\tfrac12\bigl(\alpha_1^-\bigr)^2\Bigr]dt .
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For the face-2 sweep $\Phi(s,t)=c(t)-s\mu_t$ one has
$\partial_s\Phi=-\mu_t$ and $\partial_t\Phi=c'-s\nu_t$, so
$\det[\partial_s\Phi,\partial_t\Phi]=-\langle c',\nu_t\rangle+s=s-\alpha_2$, and
integrating $|s-\alpha_2|$ over $s\in[0,\alpha_2^+]$ gives
$\tfrac12(\alpha_2^+)^2$. For the face-1 sweep $\Psi(s,t)=c(t)-s\nu_t$ one gets
$\det=s-\alpha_1$, and
\[
  \int_{\alpha_1^+}^{\sigma}(s-\alpha_1)\,ds
  =\tfrac12(\sigma-\alpha_1)^2-\tfrac12(\alpha_1^-)^2 ,
\]
which is $\tfrac12(\sigma-\alpha_1)^2$ when $\alpha_1\ge0$ and
$\tfrac12\sigma^2-\alpha_1\sigma$ when $\alpha_1<0$, as required. Injectivity
turns each $\int|\det|$ into the area of the image, disjointness makes the two
areas add, and covering makes the sum $|N|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What is measured, and what it gives]\label{rem:V}
For $\Sig$ the three hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:V} are verified
numerically and sharply: the two sweeps have intersection area $0$ and leave
$0$ of the niche uncovered, to the printing precision of the polygon oracle. The
right-hand side of \eqref{eq:V}, evaluated with exact derivatives of
SOL1/SOL5/SOL6 and Gauss--Legendre quadrature on each phase separately, is
\[
  V=0.184193197089\ldots ,
\]
stable in the twelfth digit from $20$ to $320$ nodes per phase, against a
polygonal measurement $0.184193171$ of the same region --- and an inscribed
quadrilateral union under-measures, so the two agree to $2.6\cdot10^{-8}$ from the
expected side. The implied cap area $A_R^\ast+2V=2.0133416\ldots$ lies just below
the circumscribed polygonal values $2.0133455$ and $2.0133420$ at $241$ and $481$
constraints, which must decrease to it. We therefore record \eqref{eq:V} as an
identity for $\Sig$ at the $10^{-8}$ level.

Two of the three terms of \eqref{eq:V} are convex quadratics in $h$, since
$x\mapsto\tfrac12(x^+)^2$ and $x\mapsto\tfrac12x^2$ are convex and
$\alpha_2,\sigma-\alpha_1$ are affine in $h$. This is what the generalised
Mamikon theorem buys, and it is the reason \eqref{eq:V} is the right shape: for
$|\Sig|=|C_2|-2|N|$ a convex $|N|$ gives a concave upper bound, for which a
first-order condition suffices.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[The obstruction, in closed form]\label{prop:obstruction}
The third term of \eqref{eq:V} is subtracted, so it contributes a \emph{convex}
term to $|C_2|-2|N|$ and is the one ingredient with the wrong curvature. It is
supported exactly on the degenerate phase $[0,\beta)$, where $\alpha_1<0$, and
there $\alpha_1(t)=2a_1\sin t-\tfrac12$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:obstruction}
  \tfrac12\int_0^{\pi/2}\bigl(\alpha_1^-\bigr)^2dt
  =\tfrac{\beta}{8}-a_1(1-\cos\beta)+a_1^2\Bigl(\beta-\tfrac12\sin2\beta\Bigr)
  =0.011950270059\ldots,
\end{equation}
which is $6.488\ldots\%$ of $V$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On $[0,\beta)$ the path is SOL1 with $a_2=0$, so in complex form
$z(t)=a_1e^{2it}-(1+\tfrac i2)e^{it}+(1-a_1+\tfrac i2)$ and
$e^{-it}z'(t)=2ia_1e^{it}-i(1+\tfrac i2)$, whence
$\alpha_1=-\operatorname{Re}(e^{-it}z')=2a_1\sin t-\tfrac12$. This is negative
exactly for $\sin t<1/(4a_1)$, i.e.\ for $t<\beta$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:a1beta}. Expanding
$\tfrac12\int_0^\beta(\tfrac12-2a_1\sin t)^2dt$ and using
$\int_0^\beta\sin^2=\tfrac\beta2-\tfrac14\sin2\beta$ gives
\eqref{eq:obstruction}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Where this leaves the problem]\label{rem:leaves}
Two obstructions have been removed and one remains. The first was that no trial
underestimate of the niche was tight: the best previous one, built from the apex
path, left a slack of $0.087$ against $|\Sig|=1.645$. Formula~\eqref{eq:V} is
tight, so that slack is gone. The second was that the injectivity condition fails
for $\Sig$ on $[0,\beta)$ and $(\pi/2-\beta,\pi/2]$; Lemma~\ref{lem:normvel}
shows the condition is not needed, only the two one-sided statements
$\alpha_2^+$ and $\sigma\ge\alpha_1^+$, and the second holds throughout with
equality only at $t=\pi/2$.

What remains is \eqref{eq:obstruction}. It is a subtracted square of a
convex-linear quantity, i.e.\ a $\mathcal J$-type term of the same kind the
one-corner argument must itself control, and it is localised on the phase where
$\Sig$ degenerates. Establishing or refuting concavity of
$|C_2|-2V$ is a Hessian computation on the space of support-function
perturbations, and is not attempted here. Neither is the second half of the
argument, which would have to show that the three hypotheses of
Proposition~\ref{prop:V} hold for competitors and not only for $\Sig$. So
\emph{optimality of $\Sig$ remains open}; what is new is that the upper bound is
now tight and expressed entirely in convex-linear data, with a single explicitly
computed term of the wrong sign.
\end{remark}

\section{The upper bound at \texorpdfstring{$\Sig$}{Sigma}: tight, critical, concave}
\label{sec:concavity}

Since $\nu_t=\mu_{t+\pi/2}$, everything above is a functional of the single function
\[
  H(\theta):=h_K(\mu_\theta),\qquad \theta\in[0,\pi],\qquad
  F(t)=H(t),\quad G(t)=H(t+\pi/2).
\]

\begin{proposition}[The cap is an exact quadratic form]\label{prop:cap}
Let $C=\bigcap_{t\in[0,\pi/2]}C_t$ and $C_2=C\cap\rho C$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:capquad}
  |C_2|=\int_0^\pi\bigl(H(\theta)^2-H'(\theta)^2\bigr)d\theta-H(0)-H(\pi).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\rho$ is a reflection in the line $y=\tfrac12$, not in the origin: $\rho p=Rp+(0,1)$
with $R=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$, so $h_{\rho A}(u)=h_A(Ru)+u_y$. Since $C$ is
unbounded downward, $h_C=+\infty$ on the lower half-circle and $h_{\rho C}=+\infty$ on
the upper one, so the support function of $C_2$ is
\[
  h_2(\theta)=H(\theta)\ \ (\theta\in[0,\pi]),\qquad
  h_2(\theta)=H(-\theta)+\sin\theta\ \ (\theta\in[-\pi,0]).
\]
For any support function in $H^1$ of the circle, $|C_2|=\tfrac12\int h_2\,d\sigma_2$
with $\sigma_2=(h_2+h_2'')d\theta$ as a measure, and the distributional identity
$\langle h_2'',h_2\rangle=-\int h_2'^2$ holds with no boundary or atom terms because the
circle has no boundary. Splitting at $\theta=0,\pi$ and substituting the two branches,
\[
  |C_2|=\int_0^\pi\Bigl[H^2-H'^2-H\sin s+H'\cos s-\tfrac12\cos2s\Bigr]ds,
\]
and $\int_0^\pi\cos2s\,ds=0$ while
$\int_0^\pi H'\cos s\,ds=-H(0)-H(\pi)+\int_0^\pi H\sin s\,ds$, which gives
\eqref{eq:capquad}.
\end{proof}

Note $-H(0)-H(\pi)$ is \emph{linear}, so it does not enter the Hessian; and
\eqref{eq:capquad} is invariant under $H\mapsto H+v\cos\theta$, as it must be, that
being a horizontal translation. Numerically \eqref{eq:capquad} gives
$2.013341613$ against $A_R^\ast+2V=2.013341613$, agreeing to
$9.6\cdot10^{-13}$ --- an independent analytic confirmation of the whole chain
$|\Sig|=|C_2|-2|N|$ with $|N|=V$, since \eqref{eq:capquad} and \eqref{eq:V} share no
computation. Equivalently, writing $Q:=|C_2|-2V$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Qtight}
  Q(\Sig)=1.644955218425\ldots=A_R^\ast\quad\text{to }5\cdot10^{-13}.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[The second variation]\label{prop:hess}
Fix the gauge $H(0)=1$ (horizontal translation) and $H(\pi/2)=1$ (unit corridor; this
is also exactly what makes $\sigma$ integrable against $\tan t$ at $t=\pi/2$), so that
admissible $\eta$ satisfy $\eta(0)=\eta(\pi/2)=0$. Then, with
$E_1=\{\alpha_1<0\}$ and $E_2=\{\alpha_2>0\}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q2}
\begin{aligned}
  \tfrac12\,\delta^2Q[\eta]
  =\int_0^\pi(\eta^2-\eta'^2)\,d\theta
  &-\int_{E_2}\bigl(\eta(t)+\eta'(t+\tfrac\pi2)\bigr)^2dt\\
  &-\int_0^{\pi/2}\bigl(\eta(t)\tan t+\eta'(t)\bigr)^2dt
   +\int_{E_1}\bigl(\eta(t+\tfrac\pi2)-\eta'(t)\bigr)^2dt.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Its principal part is negative definite: the coefficient of $\eta'(\theta)^2$ is $-1$ on
$[0,\beta)$ and on $[\pi-\beta,\pi]$, and $-2$ in between. Hence \eqref{eq:Q2} is
bounded above and has at most finitely many non-negative eigenvalues.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\delta\alpha_1=\eta(t+\tfrac\pi2)-\eta'(t)$, $\delta\alpha_2=\eta(t)+\eta'(t+\tfrac\pi2)$
and $\delta\sigma=\eta(t)\tan t+\eta(t+\tfrac\pi2)$ by \eqref{eq:a1}--\eqref{eq:sig};
in the middle term the $\eta(t+\pi/2)$ cancels, leaving
$\delta(\sigma-\alpha_1)=\eta(t)\tan t+\eta'(t)$. The integrand of \eqref{eq:V} is $C^1$
across $\alpha_1=0$ and $\alpha_2=0$, so the moving boundaries of $E_1,E_2$ contribute
nothing at second order, and $\delta^2$ of each square is twice the square of the
first variation. The cap contributes $2\int(\eta^2-\eta'^2)$ by
Proposition~\ref{prop:cap}. For the principal part, collect: $-1$ from the cap,
$-1$ from the $\sigma$ term on $[0,\pi/2]$, $+1$ from the $E_1$ term on $[0,\beta)$,
and $-1$ from the $E_2$ term at $\theta=t+\pi/2\in[\pi/2,\pi-\beta)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Measurements, and exactly what they do and do not give]\label{rem:conc}
In a hat basis with $\eta(0)=\eta(\pi/2)=0$:

\emph{Criticality.} $\delta Q[\eta]=0$ in every direction, $\|\delta Q\|_\infty/h
=1.8\cdot10^{-8}$ by central differences on $Q$ itself. So Romik's ODEs are the
Euler--Lagrange equations of $Q$. (An analytic assembly of the gradient first reported a
nonzero value on $[0,\beta)$ and $[\pi/2,\pi/2+\beta)$; that was a sign error on
$\delta\bigl(-\tfrac12(\alpha_1^-)^2\bigr)=+\alpha_1^-\delta\alpha_1$, caught by the
finite-difference check.)

\emph{Concavity, on $\Sig$'s cell only.} With $\Sig$'s own sign pattern
$E_1=[0,\beta)$, $E_2=[0,\pi/2-\beta)$, the form \eqref{eq:Q2} is negative definite:
$\lambda_{\max}/h=-0.6905,-0.7111,-0.7216$ at $m=32,64,128$. Under Baek's injectivity
condition, which is exactly $E_1=\emptyset$, $E_2=[0,\pi/2]$, it is $-0.8616$. But
\eqref{eq:Q2} is \emph{not} negative for every sign pattern: with $E_1=E_2=[0.4,1.2]$ it
is $+0.4071$, and with $E_1=E_2$ a union of three intervals $+0.4156$. An earlier scan
over intervals \emph{anchored at $0$} suggested that $\tau_1\le\tau_2$ suffices; the
unanchored cases refute that and the suggestion is \textbf{retracted}.

Since $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ are affine in $H$, each pointwise sign condition is a
half-space and $\Sig$'s cell is convex. Concavity plus criticality on a convex set gives
that $\Sig$ maximises $Q$ \emph{on that cell}. Nothing global follows.

\emph{What is missing --- and it is worse than missing.} All of the above concerns $Q$
alone. The inequality $Q\ge|\text{sofa}|$, which is the only reason $Q$ is relevant,
\textbf{fails}; see Proposition~\ref{prop:reynolds}. All numbers in this remark are
floating point.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The boundary of the cap, and where the sweep terminates]\label{rem:cap}
The density $H+H''$ of the surface measure determines $\partial C_2$ completely:
it vanishes on $(-\beta,\beta)$, where $\partial C_2$ is the single vertex
$P=(1,\tfrac12)$; equals $\tfrac12$ on $(\pi/2-\beta,\pi/2+\beta)$, where $\partial C_2$
is a circular arc of radius exactly $\tfrac12$ centred at $(1-2a_1,\tfrac12)$; has an
atom of mass $1.167050$ at $\theta=\pi/2$, the corridor ceiling $y=1$, a facet; vanishes
again on $(\pi-\beta,\pi]$, the second vertex; and is a smooth arc in between. The
$\rho$-image of the ceiling facet is the corridor floor, the segment
$[-0.750575,\,0.416475]\times\{0\}$, whose left endpoint lies directly below the centre
of the radius-$\tfrac12$ arc.

This settles the geometry of the outer arm of Proposition~\ref{prop:V}. Because $C_2$ is
\emph{convex}, the face-1 segment lies in $C_2$ as soon as its far endpoint does, so the
claim $S_1=\sigma$ reduces to a one-parameter containment: the endpoint
$x(t)=c_x(t)+\sigma(t)\sin t$ must lie in the floor facet. It runs monotonically from
$x(0)=0$ to $x(\pi/2)=0.416475$, the right end of the facet, and
\[
  x(\pi/2)=c_x(\pi/2)+\alpha_1(\pi/2)
  =\bigl(c_x(0)-\alpha_2(0)\bigr)+\bigl(\alpha_2(0)+\alpha_1(\pi/2)-(G(\pi/2)-1)\bigr)
\]
is an algebraic identity, the right-hand side being the left end of the facet plus its
length $H'(\pi/2^+)-H'(\pi/2^-)$. So the tangency at $t=\pi/2$ is exact, not
coincidental, and what remains of $S_1=\sigma$ is the monotonicity of the explicit
function $x$.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[$V$ over-estimates the niche, so $Q$ is not an upper bound]
\label{prop:reynolds}
For every admissible $H$, $V(H)\ge|N(H)|$. Consequently
$Q=|C_2|-2V\le|C_2|-2|N|$, and for the maximal sofa
$T_{\max}=C_2\setminus(U\cup\rho U)$ with that cap, $|T_{\max}|=|C_2|-2|N|\ge Q$. So $Q$
is an upper bound for $|T_{\max}|$ only where $V=|N|$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$V$ is precisely the flux of the advancing part of $\partial Q_t$, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:normvel} and the Jacobian computation in Proposition~\ref{prop:V}. By
Reynolds' transport theorem the area gained by the sweeping region
$N_T=\bigcup_{t\le T}Q_t\cap C_2$ satisfies
$\tfrac{d}{dT}|N_T|\le\int_{\text{advancing part of }\partial Q_T}v_n\,ds$, because
advance into already-swept territory contributes to the flux but gains no area.
Integrating over $T\in[0,\pi/2]$ gives $|N|\le V$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The size of the gap, measured]\label{rem:gap}
Equality in Proposition~\ref{prop:reynolds} holds exactly when nothing is re-swept, which
is what the three hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:V} assert, and which is why
$V(\Sig)=|N(\Sig)|$. Perturbing $H$ by a smooth bump $\eta$ supported where
$H+H''\ge\tfrac12$ (so convexity survives) and measuring $|N|$ with a polygon oracle
against $V$ from the same $H$, the excess $V-|N|$, corrected for the oracle's known
$O(1/n)$ bias, is
\[
  \begin{array}{r|cccccc}
    \varepsilon & -0.20 & -0.10 & -0.05 & 0.05 & 0.10 & 0.20\\\hline
    V-|N| & 5.5\cdot10^{-3} & 1.4\cdot10^{-4} & 4.2\cdot10^{-6}
          & 9.3\cdot10^{-5} & 8.4\cdot10^{-4} & 5.0\cdot10^{-3}
  \end{array}
\]
--- strictly positive on \emph{both} sides and growing faster than $\varepsilon^2$. So
$\Sig$ is an isolated zero of $V-|N|$: the functional $Q$ touches the true bound at $\Sig$
and falls strictly below it in every direction. Therefore $Q$ cannot certify $\Sig$, and
the fact that $Q$ is tight, critical and concave there (Remark~\ref{rem:conc}) does not
yield an optimality statement.

This locates the difficulty exactly. The repair is the one the one-corner argument
performs: replace the exact flux $V$ by the flux over a sub-family on which the sweep is
\emph{provably} injective, so that the integral is the area of a genuine subset of $N$ and
the inequality runs the right way, while the restriction is vacuous at $\Sig$. Producing
such a sub-family is the content of that argument's Chapters 3--6 and is not done here.

One reformulation that may help. A point $p$ lies on the face-2 line at parameter $t$ iff
$\phi(t):=\langle p,\nu_t\rangle-(G(t)-1)$ vanishes, and with
$s(p,t):=F(t)-1-\langle p,\mu_t\rangle$ one computes
$\phi'=s-\alpha_2$ and $s'=-\phi-\alpha_1$, i.e.\ in complex form
\[
  z:=s+i\phi,\qquad z'=iz-(\alpha_1+i\alpha_2).
\]
So the whole face-2 sweep is governed by one linear ODE whose homogeneous part is an
exact rotation, $z(t)=\zeta(t)+e^{it}w_0$ with $w_0$ an affine coordinate for $p$; the
sweep is injective iff no solution meets $\{\phi=0,\ 0\le s\le\alpha_2\}$ twice, and at
every such meeting $\phi'=s-\alpha_2\le0$, so the crossing is always downward. A second
meeting therefore forces an upward crossing in between, at $s\ge\alpha_2$. Since the
total rotation over $[0,\pi/2]$ is only $\pi/2$, the homogeneous part alone cannot supply
that extra crossing; whether the forcing $\zeta$ can is the open point.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Monotonicity of the outer arm]\label{prop:mono}
The face-1 segment's far endpoint has $x$-coordinate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xint}
  x(t)=c_x(t)+\sigma(t)\sin t=\frac{F(t)-1}{\cos t},
\end{equation}
the $x$-intercept of the face-1 line, and $\cos^2t\;x'(t)=F'(t)\cos t+(F(t)-1)\sin t$.
For $\Sig$:
\begin{itemize}
\item on $[0,\beta)$, where $A(t)\equiv P$ so $F(t)=\cos t+\tfrac12\sin t$,
\[
  x=1+\tfrac12\tan t-\sec t,\qquad \cos^2t\;x'=\tfrac12-\sin t>0,
\]
the last because $\sin\beta=1/(4a_1)=0.2856\ldots<\tfrac12$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:a1beta}, i.e.\ $\beta<\pi/6$;
\item on $(\pi/2-\beta,\pi/2]$, where $F(t)-1=A\cos t+\tfrac12\sin t-\tfrac12$ with
$A=1-\tfrac23a_1$,
\[
  x=A-\tfrac12\tan\Bigl(\tfrac\pi4-\tfrac t2\Bigr),\qquad
  \cos^2t\;x'=\tfrac12(1-\sin t)>0\ \ (t<\pi/2),
\]
and in particular $x(\pi/2)=A=1-\tfrac23a_1=0.4164750917\ldots$, which is exactly the
right endpoint of the corridor floor facet of Remark~\ref{rem:cap};
\item on $[\beta,\pi/2-\beta]$, substituting $u=t/2-\pi/8$ and using
$-f_2/f_1=\sqrt2-1=\tan(\pi/8)$,
\[
  \cos^2t\;x'=W(u):=\tfrac{3K}{4}\sin u+\tfrac K4\cos3u+\tfrac12
    -\sin\Bigl(2u+\tfrac\pi4\Bigr),\qquad K=f_1\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2},
\]
on $|u|\le\pi/8-\beta/2$.
\end{itemize}
Hence $x$ is strictly increasing, given $W>0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The face-1 line is $\{\langle p,\mu_t\rangle=F(t)-1\}$, so its $x$-intercept is
$(F-1)/\cos t$; that it agrees with $c_x+\sigma\sin t$ is
$c_x+c_y\tan t=(F-1)/\cos t$, immediate from $c=(F-1)\mu_t+(G-1)\nu_t$. The three
closed forms follow by substituting $F(t)-1=\operatorname{Re}(e^{-it}z(t))$ with the
complex forms of SOL1, SOL6, SOL5. For the middle phase the $\kappa_6$ terms cancel and
the triple-angle identities $\sin(3t/2)=s(3c^2-s^2)$, $\cos(3t/2)=c(c^2-3s^2)$ with
$s=\sin\tfrac t2$, $c=\cos\tfrac t2$ reduce the result to the stated three-term form.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[$W>0$, by a finite certificate]\label{prop:W}
$W>0$ on $|u|\le u_0:=\pi/8-\beta/2=0.2478721712\ldots$, so $x$ is strictly increasing
on all of $[0,\pi/2]$ and $S_1=\sigma$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$K=1.3020516916172893452\ldots$, and $3u_0=0.7436\ldots<\pi$, so $\cos3u$ is unimodal on
$[-u_0,u_0]$ with maximum at $0$ and its minimum over any subinterval is attained at an
endpoint. Also $\sin u$ is increasing, and $2u+\pi/4$ ranges over
$[0.2896\ldots,1.2812\ldots]\subset[0,\pi/2]$, so $\sin(2u+\pi/4)$ is increasing.
Therefore on $[p,q]\subseteq[-u_0,u_0]$,
\[
  W\ \ge\ L(p,q):=\tfrac{3K}{4}\sin p+\tfrac K4\min\bigl(\cos3p,\cos3q\bigr)
       +\tfrac12-\sin\Bigl(2q+\tfrac\pi4\Bigr).
\]
Dividing $[-u_0,u_0]$ into $32$ equal pieces gives $L>0$ on every piece, with
$\min L=6.13\cdot10^{-3}$; the computation is at $40$ working digits, so the margin
exceeds the arithmetic error by $37$ orders of magnitude. ($16$ pieces do not suffice:
$\min L=-8.6\cdot10^{-3}$.)
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
$W$ is in fact strictly decreasing, with
$W(u_0)=\tfrac12(1-\cos\beta)=0.020828595599846555\ldots$ at the right junction, matching
the last phase's $\tfrac12(1-\sin t)$ there to $1.7\cdot10^{-41}$, as $C^1$ gluing
requires. The three closed forms of Proposition~\ref{prop:mono} were also checked against
numerical differentiation of the path to $10^{-10}$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The identity at 61 digits]\label{rem:61}
With all constants from their closed forms ($u^3+3u=2$, $\beta=\arctan(u/2)$,
$a_1=\sqrt{4+u^2}/(4u)$, $f_1$ from Proposition~\ref{prop:f1}) and $60$ working digits,
\[
  V=0.184193197088768988251655564606\ldots,\qquad
  |C_2|=2.013341612602978828172120476813\ldots,
\]
\[
  Q(\Sig)-A_R^\ast=-1.56\cdot10^{-61}.
\]
The two sides share no computation, so this rules out the possibility that the
double-precision agreement of \eqref{eq:Qtight} was a rounding coincidence. It remains
floating point, not an interval enclosure.
\end{remark}

\section{A convex subdomain on which the bound is valid}\label{sec:kprime}

Proposition~\ref{prop:reynolds} says $V\ge|N|$ with equality exactly when nothing is
re-swept, i.e.\ when the combined sweep is injective. We now show that injectivity is
governed by inequalities that are \emph{linear} in $H$, so the set where the bound is
valid is convex.

Each sweep lies on a line: face~2 at parameter $t$ on
$\ell_2(t)=\{\langle p,\nu_t\rangle=G(t)-1\}$, face~1 on
$\ell_1(t)=\{\langle p,\mu_t\rangle=F(t)-1\}$. Two distinct lines meet in one point, so a
double cover forces that point into both segments. Solving $\ell_2(t)\cap\ell_2(t')$ for
the coordinate $s$ on $\ell_2(t)$, and using $\langle\mu_t,\nu_{t'}\rangle=\sin(t-t')$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:s22}
  s_{22}(t,t')=\frac{\langle c(t),\nu_{t'}\rangle-(G(t')-1)}{\sin(t-t')},
  \qquad
  s_{11}(t,t')=\frac{\langle c(t),\mu_{t'}\rangle-(F(t')-1)}{\sin(t'-t)} .
\end{equation}
The numerator of $s_{22}$ vanishes at $t'=t$ with derivative $-\alpha_2(t)$, so
$s_{22}(t,t')\to\alpha_2(t)$: nearby face-2 lines meet at the \emph{far} end of the
face-2 segment $[0,\alpha_2]$. Likewise $s_{11}(t,t')\to\alpha_1(t)$, the \emph{near} end
of the face-1 segment $[\alpha_1^+,\sigma]$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:kprime}
Let $\mathcal K'$ be the set of admissible $H$ satisfying, for all $0\le t'<t\le\pi/2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:C22}
  \text{(C22)}\quad \langle c(t),\nu_{t'}\rangle-(G(t')-1)
     \ \ge\ \alpha_2(t)\sin(t-t'),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:C11}
  \text{(C11)}\quad \langle c(t),\mu_{t'}\rangle-(F(t')-1)
     \ \le\ \alpha_1(t)^+\sin(t'-t)\ \ (\text{read with }\sin(t'-t)<0),
\end{equation}
together with the analogous condition for $\ell_2(t)\cap\ell_1(t')$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:convex}
$\mathcal K'$ is convex, and on $\mathcal K'$ the combined sweep is injective, hence
$V=|N|$ and $Q=|C_2|-2|N|\ge|T|$ for every ambidextrous sofa $T$ with cap data $H$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:corner}, $c(t)$ is affine in $H$, hence so are
$\langle c(t),\nu_{t'}\rangle$, $G(t')-1$, $F(t')-1$, $\alpha_1(t)$ and $\alpha_2(t)$;
$\sin(t-t')$ does not depend on $H$. So \eqref{eq:C22} and \eqref{eq:C11} are linear
inequalities in $H$ and cut out a convex set. Given \eqref{eq:C22}, for any pair
$t'<t$ the intersection point of $\ell_2(t)$ and $\ell_2(t')$ has
$s_{22}(t,t')\ge\alpha_2(t)$, so it is not interior to the segment at $t$, and the pair
contributes no double cover; \eqref{eq:C11} does the same for face~1 with the inequality
reversed because the relevant endpoint is the near one. With the cross condition, no point
is swept twice.

For $V=|N|$: every point of $N$ has a first capture time $\tau(p)$, at which
$\partial Q_{\tau(p)}$ is advancing at $p$ (otherwise $p$ would have been captured
earlier), so by Lemma~\ref{lem:normvel} it lies on face~2 with $s\le\alpha_2$ or on face~1
with $s\ge\alpha_1$. Hence $N$ is covered by the two sweeps, and the sweeps lie in $N$
because an advancing face point belongs to $Q_{t+\varepsilon}$. Injectivity turns each
$\int|\det|$ into the area of its image, and the two images are disjoint, so the sum is
$|N|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[$\Sig\in\mathcal K'$, and $\mathcal K'$ has nonempty interior]
\label{rem:kprime}
Measured on a grid of $401$ parameters, $\Sig$ satisfies all three conditions: of the
$158\,802$ ordered pairs with $|t-t'|$ above the diagonal cutoff, \emph{zero} give a point
interior to both face-2 segments and \emph{zero} interior to both face-1 segments, and of
$58\,081$ cross pairs, \emph{zero} land in both. The margins in \eqref{eq:C22},
\eqref{eq:C11} vanish linearly as $t'\to t$, which is forced: that is the envelope limit.
Away from the diagonal they are strictly positive.

$\mathcal K'$ is not a single point. For $H=H_\Sig+\varepsilon\sin(k\theta)$, which
respects the gauge for even $k$, all conditions still hold with zero violations at
$k=2$ for $|\varepsilon|\le3\cdot10^{-2}$ and at $k=4$ for $|\varepsilon|\le10^{-2}$, in
both signs, and the two margins trade off smoothly. So $\Sig$ lies in $\mathcal K'$ with
low-frequency room around it.

The mechanism of failure is identified: for the compactly supported bump perturbations of
Remark~\ref{rem:gap}, where $V-|N|$ was found positive, the face-2 condition continues to
hold and it is the \emph{face-1} condition that fails, by $-4.7\cdot10^{-2}$ at
$\varepsilon=0.05$ and $-2.3\cdot10^{-1}$ at $\varepsilon=0.20$. Those perturbations have
large higher derivatives, which is what creates face-1 envelope self-intersections.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Conditional]\label{thm:cond}
Assume:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$, $\delta Q(\Sig)=0$ in every admissible direction, and
$\delta^2Q\preceq0$ on the cell $\mathcal C$ of support functions sharing $\Sig$'s sign
pattern;
\item[(ii)] the three conditions of Definition~\ref{def:kprime} hold on
$\mathcal K'\cap\mathcal C$;
\item[(iii)] competitors satisfy the niche separation $M<\tfrac12$ of
Corollary~\ref{cor:sep}.
\end{enumerate}
Then $\lvert T\rvert\le A_R^\ast$ for every ambidextrous sofa $T$ whose cap data lies in
$\mathcal K'\cap\mathcal C$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$\mathcal C$ is convex because $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ are affine in $H$, so each pointwise
sign condition is a half-space; $\mathcal K'$ is convex by
Proposition~\ref{prop:convex}; hence so is the intersection. By (i), $Q$ is a concave
function on that convex set with a critical point at $\Sig$, so
$Q(H)\le Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$ throughout. By (ii) and Proposition~\ref{prop:convex},
$Q(H)\ge|T|$ there, using (iii) to get the factor $2$ in $|T|\le|C_2|-2|N|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Honest status of Theorem~\ref{thm:cond}]\label{rem:honest}
Hypothesis (i) is \emph{numerical}: $Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$ to $61$ digits
(Remark~\ref{rem:61}), $\|\delta Q\|_\infty/h=1.8\cdot10^{-8}$, and
$\lambda_{\max}/h\to-0.72$ for a discretised form. Hypothesis (ii) is measured on a
grid, not proved. Hypothesis (iii) is the conditional part of
Theorem~\ref{thm:ceiling}. So the effective label of Theorem~\ref{thm:cond} is
HEURISTIC, and it does not assert optimality of $\Sig$: the class
$\mathcal K'\cap\mathcal C$ is not shown to contain every competitor, and by
Remark~\ref{rem:gap} bodies outside $\mathcal K'$ genuinely violate $Q\ge|T|$. What the
theorem does is exhibit an explicit convex class, with nonempty interior around $\Sig$,
on which the one-corner architecture transfers. Proving (i) and (ii) is the remaining
work, and is finite: (i) reduces to a Hessian sign and a first-order identity in closed
form, and (ii) to the linear inequalities \eqref{eq:C22}, \eqref{eq:C11}.
\end{remark}

\section{One curvature condition implies injectivity}\label{sec:rc}

The conditions of Definition~\ref{def:kprime} were verified on a grid. We now prove them,
for all pairs at once, from a single linear condition on the cap.

\begin{definition}\label{def:rc}
Say $H$ satisfies (RC) if the absolutely continuous part of the surface measure obeys
\[
  H(\theta)+H''(\theta)\ \le\ 1\qquad\text{for a.e. }\theta\in[0,\pi],
\]
the only atoms being the corridor ceiling and floor facets at $\theta=\pm\pi/2$.
Equivalently: the cap is nowhere flatter than a circle whose radius is the corridor
width.
\end{definition}

Since $H+H''\le1$ is linear in $H$, (RC) cuts out a convex set.

\begin{remark}[(RC) is classical]\label{rem:rcclassical}
The bound $h+h''\le R$ is not new. By the dual of Blaschke's rolling theorem, a convex
body $K$ slides freely inside a ball of radius $R$ exactly when $h_K+h_K''\le R$, and
that in turn says $K$ is a Minkowski summand of $RB$; see
Schneider \cite[\S3.2]{Sch14}. Our (RC) is that condition at $R=1$, the corridor width,
\emph{relaxed} to permit atoms at $\theta=\pm\pi/2$. Since Minkowski addition adds surface
area measures, and a body whose surface measure is two equal atoms at $\pm\pi/2$ is a
horizontal segment,
\[
  \text{(RC)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  C_2=\bigl[\text{a horizontal segment}\bigr]\oplus D,\qquad
  D\ \text{a Minkowski summand of the unit disc.}
\]
For $\Sig$ the segment has length $1.1670498\ldots$, the corridor facet, and $D$ has
curvature radius at most $0.8389$. What we claim is not the condition but its use: that it
forces the niche sweep to be injective (Theorems~\ref{thm:rc}, \ref{thm:cross}).
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:rc}
If $H$ satisfies (RC), then \eqref{eq:C22} and \eqref{eq:C11} hold for every pair
$0\le t'<t\le\pi/2$. Hence each of the two sweeps is injective.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $t$ and set $\tau=t-t'\in(0,t]$. For \eqref{eq:C22} put
$n(t')=\langle c(t),\nu_{t'}\rangle-(G(t')-1)$ and
\[
  \Phi(\tau)=n(t-\tau)-\alpha_2(t)\sin\tau ,
\]
so that \eqref{eq:C22} reads $\Phi\ge0$. Now $n(t)=0$ because
$\langle c(t),\nu_t\rangle=G(t)-1$, and
$n'(t')=-\langle c(t),\mu_{t'}\rangle-G'(t')$ equals $-(F(t)-1)-G'(t)=-\alpha_2(t)$ at
$t'=t$ by \eqref{eq:a2}; hence $\Phi(0)=\Phi'(0)=0$. Differentiating again and using
$\nu''=-\nu$,
\[
  n''(t')=-\langle c(t),\nu_{t'}\rangle-G''(t')
        =-\bigl(n(t')+G(t')-1\bigr)-G''(t') ,
\]
so with $n(t-\tau)=\Phi(\tau)+\alpha_2(t)\sin\tau$ the $\alpha_2$ terms cancel and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:osc}
  \Phi''(\tau)+\Phi(\tau)=1-(G+G'')(t-\tau)=:R(\tau).
\end{equation}
With $\Phi(0)=\Phi'(0)=0$, \eqref{eq:osc} gives
$\Phi(\tau)=\int_0^\tau\sin(\tau-u)R(u)\,du$. Since $\tau\le t\le\pi/2<\pi$ we have
$\sin(\tau-u)\ge0$ for $u\in[0,\tau]$, and $R\ge0$ by (RC) because
$G(s)=H(s+\pi/2)$. Therefore $\Phi\ge0$.

The ceiling atom of $H$ sits at $\theta=\pi/2$, i.e.\ at $t-u+\pi/2=\pi/2$, i.e.\ $u=t$.
For $\tau<t$ that is outside $[0,\tau]$, and for $\tau=t$ it is the endpoint, where the
kernel $\sin(\tau-u)$ vanishes. So the atom contributes nothing.

For \eqref{eq:C11} put $m(t')=\langle c(t),\mu_{t'}\rangle-(F(t')-1)$ and
$\Psi(\tau)=m(t-\tau)+\alpha_1(t)\sin\tau$. Then $m(t)=0$, $m'(t)=\alpha_1(t)$ by
\eqref{eq:a1}, so $\Psi(0)=\Psi'(0)=0$, and $\mu''=-\mu$ gives in the same way
\[
  \Psi''(\tau)+\Psi(\tau)=1-(F+F'')(t-\tau),
\]
whence $\Psi(\tau)=\int_0^\tau\sin(\tau-u)\bigl[1-(H+H'')(t-u)\bigr]du\ge0$. Multiplying
by $-\sin\tau<0$ turns $\Psi\ge0$ into $s_{11}\le\alpha_1$, which is \eqref{eq:C11}. Here
the atom needs $t-u=\pi/2$, i.e.\ $u=t-\pi/2\le0$, reachable only at $t=\pi/2$, where
$\sigma(\pi/2)=\alpha_1(\pi/2)$ makes the face-1 segment empty and the condition vacuous.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Verification]\label{rem:rcnum}
For $\Sig$ the absolutely continuous part of $H+H''$ has maximum $0.8385682\ldots$, at
$\theta=\pi-\beta$, so (RC) holds with margin $0.1614\ldots$; the atom at $\theta=\pi/2$
has mass $1.1670498\ldots$, the ceiling facet length. The reduction \eqref{eq:osc} was
checked against direct evaluation of $n(t-\tau)-\alpha_2(t)\sin\tau$ at several
$(t,\tau)$, agreeing to $2.4\cdot10^{-5}$, the finite-difference floor for $H''$.

Theorem~\ref{thm:rc} also explains the failure mode of Remark~\ref{rem:kprime}. Across
twelve perturbations, (RC) holds exactly when \eqref{eq:C11} holds:
the compactly supported bumps give
$\max(H+H'')_{\mathrm{ac}}=1.08,\,1.45,\,2.18,\,2.67,\,4.58,\,8.41$ and all violate
\eqref{eq:C11}; $H_\Sig+\varepsilon\sin(2\theta)$ at $|\varepsilon|=0.03$ gives $0.888$
and holds; $H_\Sig+\varepsilon\sin(4\theta)$ gives $0.976$ at $|\varepsilon|=0.01$ and
holds, $1.267$ at $|\varepsilon|=0.03$ and fails. The agreement is exact on both sides of
the transition.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[What Theorem~\ref{thm:rc} does and does not give]
It replaces hypothesis (ii) of Theorem~\ref{thm:cond}, for the two self-intersection
conditions, by (RC): a single explicit linear inequality with a geometric reading. The
cross condition, that $\ell_2(t)\cap\ell_1(t')$ avoid both segments, is not covered; it is
measured to hold at $\Sig$ ($0$ of $58\,081$ pairs) and remains a hypothesis. So the
hypothesis class of Theorem~\ref{thm:cond} may be replaced by
$\{(\text{RC})\}\cap\{\text{cross}\}\cap\mathcal C$, an intersection of two convex sets
with one measured condition.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The first variation, pointwise]\label{rem:el}
Hypothesis (i) of Theorem~\ref{thm:cond} contains $\delta Q(\Sig)=0$. Integrating every
$\eta'$ in $\delta Q$ by parts and collecting the coefficient of $\eta(\theta)$ gives the
Euler--Lagrange equations of $Q$:
\[
  H+H''=\alpha_2^++(\sigma-\alpha_1)\tan\theta-(\sigma-\alpha_1)'+(\alpha_1^-)'
  \qquad(0<\theta<\pi/2),
\]
\[
  H+H''=-(\alpha_2^+)'(s)+\alpha_1^-(s),\qquad s=\theta-\pi/2
  \qquad(\pi/2<\theta<\pi).
\]
Evaluated on $\Sig$ at eighteen values of $\theta$ spanning all six regions, the residual
is at most $9.4\cdot10^{-6}$, which is the finite-difference floor. So $\delta Q(\Sig)=0$
has an explicit pointwise form; proving it amounts to substituting the closed forms of
SOL1, SOL5, SOL6 phase by phase, and is not carried out here.
\end{remark}

\section{The cross condition, the first variation, and a Gårding bound}\label{sec:rest}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:cross}
(RC) implies the cross condition: for all $t,t'$ the point
$\ell_2(t)\cap\ell_1(t')$ is not interior to both sweep segments. Consequently, under
(RC) the combined sweep is injective and $V=|N|$, so
$Q=|C_2|-2|N|\ge|T|$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Work in the frame at $t$ and write $p=c(t)+a\mu_t+b\nu_t$. The equation
$\langle p,\nu_t\rangle=G(t)-1$ forces $b=0$, so $p$ moves along $\ell_2(t)$ with
$s_2=-a$; the equation $\langle p,\mu_{t'}\rangle=F(t')-1$ then gives, for $t'=t-\tau$,
\[
  s_2=\frac{m(t-\tau)}{\cos\tau},\qquad
  s_1=-\Phi(\tau)-\bigl(\alpha_2(t)-s_2\bigr)\sin\tau ,
\]
using $\langle\mu_t,\nu_{t-\tau}\rangle=\sin\tau$ and
$n(t-\tau)=\Phi(\tau)+\alpha_2(t)\sin\tau$. If $\tau>0$ and $p$ is interior to the face-2
segment, then $0<s_2<\alpha_2(t)$, and $\Phi\ge0$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:rc} with
$\sin\tau\ge0$ because $\tau\le\pi/2$; hence $s_1<0\le\alpha_1(t')^+$ and $p$ lies below
the face-1 segment.

For $t'>t$ work in the frame at $t'$: the same computation with the roles of the two
families exchanged gives, for $t=t'-\tau'$ with $\tau'>0$,
\[
  s_2=-\Psi(\tau')-\bigl(s_1-\alpha_1(t')\bigr)\sin\tau' ,
\]
so if $p$ is interior to the face-1 segment then $s_1>\alpha_1(t')^+\ge\alpha_1(t')$ and
$s_2<0$, outside $[0,\alpha_2(t)]$. At $t'=t$ the two lines are perpendicular and meet at
$c(t)$, where $s_1=s_2=0$, interior to neither. Both displayed identities were checked
against a direct linear solve, agreeing to $10^{-16}$.
\end{proof}

So hypothesis (ii) of Theorem~\ref{thm:cond} may be replaced throughout by (RC).

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:el}
$\delta Q(\Sig)=0$. Two things are needed and both hold: the Euler--Lagrange equations of
Remark~\ref{rem:el} hold identically on each of the six regions, as trigonometric
identities in $a_1,f_1,f_2$ with no relation among the constants used; and the boundary
term of $\delta Q$ vanishes, because $H'(\pi)=-\tfrac12$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On each phase, $F(t)-1=\operatorname{Re}(e^{-it}z(t))$ and
$G(t)-1=\operatorname{Im}(e^{-it}z(t))$ evaluate to
\[
  \begin{array}{lll}
    \text{SOL1} & F-1=\cos t+\tfrac12\sin t-1,
      & G-1=(2a_1-1)\sin t+\tfrac12\cos t-\tfrac12,\\[2pt]
    \text{SOL6} & F-1=f_1\cos\tfrac t2+f_2\sin\tfrac t2-1+\kappa\cos t+\tfrac12\sin t,
      & G-1=-f_2\cos\tfrac t2+f_1\sin\tfrac t2-1+\tfrac12\cos t-\kappa\sin t,\\[2pt]
    \text{SOL5} & F-1=(1-\tfrac23a_1)\cos t+\tfrac12\sin t-\tfrac12,
      & G-1=(\tfrac83a_1-1)\sin t+\tfrac12\cos t-1,
  \end{array}
\]
with $\kappa=1-\tfrac43a_1$. Substituting these into
$\alpha_1=G-1-F'$, $\alpha_2=F-1+G'$, $\sigma=(F-1)\tan t+G-1$ and into the two
Euler--Lagrange equations, with the sign pattern of each region fixed
($\alpha_2>0$ except on the last phase, $\alpha_1<0$ only on the first), every residual
reduces identically to $0$.

For the boundary term, integrating the $\eta'$ of
$\delta|C_2|=2\int_0^\pi(H\eta-H'\eta')d\theta-\eta(0)-\eta(\pi)$ by parts and using
$\eta(0)=0$ leaves $-(2H'(\pi)+1)\eta(\pi)$ beyond the pointwise equations. The variation
$\delta V$ contributes nothing there: its only boundary term at $\theta=\pi$ carries the
factor $\alpha_2^+(\pi/2)$, and $\alpha_2(\pi/2)<0$, so the clamp kills it; every other
boundary sits at $\theta=0$ or $\pi/2$, where $\eta$ vanishes. Finally the boundary point of
$C_2$ with outer normal $\mu_\pi=(-1,0)$ is
$H(\pi)\mu_\pi+H'(\pi)\nu_\pi=(-H(\pi),-H'(\pi))$, so its height is $-H'(\pi)$; since $C_2$
is $\rho$-symmetric about $y=\tfrac12$ its leftmost point is $\rho$-fixed when unique,
forcing $H'(\pi)=-\tfrac12$ and the boundary term to vanish. The same mechanism at
$\theta=0$ gives $H'(0)=\tfrac12$: both extreme points of the cap sit at height $\tfrac12$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The computation is a computer algebra verification, six residuals, each returning $0$
with $a_1,f_1,f_2$ free. It was controlled against five deliberately perturbed variants
of the middle-phase equation (sign flip on the $\tan$ term, each of the two terms dropped,
$\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ exchanged, $H+H''$ replaced by $H-H''$), all of which return
nonzero; and the three pairs of phase formulas were checked against the reference path to
$2.2\cdot10^{-16}$. The content of Theorem~\ref{thm:el} is that Romik's ODE families are
exactly the critical-point equations of $Q$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Towards $\delta^2Q\preceq0$]\label{rem:garding}
The remaining part of hypothesis (i) is the sign of the second variation. The step that
makes it accessible is an exact algebraic cancellation. On $[0,\beta)$ write
$p=\eta'(t)$, $q=\eta(t)$, $r=\eta(t+\pi/2)$, $T=\tan t$; the three terms of
\eqref{eq:Q2} that carry $\eta'(t)$ combine as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:complete}
  -p^2-(qT+p)^2+(r-p)^2=-(p+qT+r)^2+2r^2+2qTr ,
\end{equation}
so the added term is absorbed exactly and the remainder carries no derivative of $\eta$.
What is left is a lower-order estimate. With $\eta(0)=\eta(\pi/2)=0$ the relevant
Poincaré constants are $4$ on $[0,\pi/2]$, $1$ on $[\pi/2,\pi]$ (Dirichlet--Neumann,
$\eta(\pi)$ free) and $(\pi/2\beta)^2=29.41$ on $[0,\beta]$. Retaining a fraction
$\delta$ of $-\int_0^\beta\eta'^2$ before applying \eqref{eq:complete} and splitting
$(qT+r)^2$ with a parameter $\kappa$, the coefficient of $\int_0^\beta\eta^2$ is
\[
  \Bigl(\tfrac{1+\kappa}{1-\delta}-1\Bigr)\tan^2\beta+1-\delta\Bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{2\beta}\Bigr)^2
  =-1.83\ldots<0 \qquad(\delta=\tfrac1{10},\ \kappa=1),
\]
and the $r^2$ remainder, of coefficient $3.22\ldots$, is absorbed by the $E_2$ term of
\eqref{eq:Q2}, which supplies $-\tfrac12\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi-\beta}\eta'^2$: since
$\eta(\pi/2)=0$ gives
$\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi/2+\beta}\eta^2\le(\beta^2/2)\int\eta'^2$, the requirement is
$3.22\cdot0.0420=0.135\le\tfrac12$. So every ingredient of a Gårding inequality
$\delta^2Q\le-c\|\eta\|_{L^2}^2$ is in place; assembling the chain with explicit constants
is not carried out here.

Measured, the constant is $c=0.7285\ldots$: the supremum of
$\delta^2Q[\eta]/\|\eta\|_{L^2}^2$ over the hat space, as a symmetric generalised
eigenvalue problem against the exact mass matrix, is
$-0.7059,-0.7188,-0.7255,-0.7285$ at $m=32,64,128,256$.
\end{remark}

\section{Concavity, and the area identity}\label{sec:final}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:concave}
On the cell $\mathcal C$ of $\Sig$'s sign pattern, $\delta^2Q\le0$, with equality only
at $\eta=0$. Explicitly, writing $J_1=\int_0^\beta\eta^2$, $J_2=\int_\beta^{\pi/2}\eta^2$,
$J_3=\int_{\pi/2}^\pi\eta^2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:garding}
  \tfrac12\,\delta^2Q[\eta]\ \le\ -1.6013\,J_1-0.3710\,J_2+0\cdot J_3
     -0.005\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi-\beta}\eta'^2 .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On $[0,\beta)$ retain a fraction $\delta$ of $-\int_0^\beta\eta'^2$ and apply
\eqref{eq:complete} with the remaining $-(1-\delta)\eta'^2$; completing the square in
$p=\eta'(t)$ and discarding the resulting negative square,
\[
  -(1-\delta)p^2-(qT+p)^2+(r-p)^2\ \le\
  \frac{(qT+r)^2}{1-\delta}-q^2T^2+r^2
  \ \le\ A\,q^2T^2+B\,r^2 ,
\]
with $A=\frac{1+\kappa}{1-\delta}-1$, $B=\frac{1+1/\kappa}{1-\delta}+1$ from
$(qT+r)^2\le(1+\kappa)q^2T^2+(1+1/\kappa)r^2$, and $T=\tan t\le\tan\beta$ on $[0,\beta)$.
The three Poincaré constants for $\eta(0)=\eta(\pi/2)=0$ are
$P_1=(\pi/2\beta)^2=29.4135$ on $[0,\beta]$, $P_2=(\pi/2(\pi/2-\beta))^2=1.5035$ on
$[\beta,\pi/2]$ and $P_3=1$ on $[\pi/2,\pi]$, each with a Dirichlet endpoint and a free
one. Finally $-\bigl(\eta(t)+\eta'(t+\pi/2)\bigr)^2\le-\lambda\,\eta'(t+\pi/2)^2
+\tfrac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\eta(t)^2$ turns the $E_2$ term into
$-\lambda\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi-\beta}\eta'^2+\tfrac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}(J_1+J_2)$, and
$\eta(\pi/2)=0$ gives
$\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi/2+\beta}\eta^2\le(\beta^2/2)\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi-\beta}\eta'^2$, so the
$B\,r^2$ remainder is absorbed as soon as $\lambda\ge B\beta^2/2$. Collecting,
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    \text{coefficient of }J_1&=A\tan^2\beta-\delta P_1+1+\tfrac{\lambda}{1-\lambda},&
    \text{of }J_2&=1-P_2+\tfrac{\lambda}{1-\lambda},\\
    \text{of }J_3&=1-P_3=0,&
    \text{of }\!\!\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi-\beta}\!\!\eta'^2&=B\beta^2/2-\lambda .
  \end{aligned}
\]
At $\delta=\tfrac1{10}$, $\kappa=2$, $\lambda=0.117$ these are $-1.6013$, $-0.3710$, $0$
and $-0.005$, giving \eqref{eq:garding}. For strictness, equality forces $\eta\equiv0$ on
$[0,\pi/2]$ and $\eta$ the first Dirichlet--Neumann mode on $[\pi/2,\pi]$; but with
$\eta\equiv0$ on $[0,\pi/2]$ the $E_2$ term contributes
$-\int_0^{\pi/2-\beta}\eta'(t+\pi/2)^2<0$.
\end{proof}

The bound $\delta^2Q\le-0.7419\|\eta\|_{L^2(0,\pi/2)}^2$ that \eqref{eq:garding} yields
may be compared with the measured supremum of $\delta^2Q/\|\eta\|_{L^2(0,\pi)}^2$, which
is $-0.7285\ldots$; the two agree to the extent the proof's $J_3$ coefficient is slack.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:sigx}
$\sigma-\alpha_1=\cos t\;x'(t)$ on each phase, with $x=(F-1)/\cos t$ as in
\eqref{eq:xint}. Hence the middle term of \eqref{eq:V} is
$\tfrac12\int\cos^2t\;x'(t)^2\,dt$, an energy of the intercept path.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\sigma-\alpha_1=(F-1)\tan t+F'$ by \eqref{eq:a1} and \eqref{eq:sig}, and
$\cos^2t\;x'=F'\cos t+(F-1)\sin t$ by \eqref{eq:xint}; dividing by $\cos t$ gives the
claim. Verified symbolically on SOL1, SOL5 and SOL6, residual $0$ in each case, which
also recovers the first and third closed forms of Proposition~\ref{prop:mono}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The area identity]\label{rem:area}
Since $u=2\tan\beta$, Romik's area is
\[
  A_R^\ast=u^2+1+\arctan(u/2)=1+4\tan^2\beta+\beta .
\]
Using Proposition~\ref{prop:sigx} to make every integrand elementary, the six blocks of
$|C_2|$ and the three blocks of $V$ evaluate at $50$ working digits to
$|C_2|=2.0133416126029788281721\ldots$, $V=0.1841931970887689882517\ldots$, and
\[
  Q(\Sig)-\bigl(1+4\tan^2\beta+\beta\bigr)=-2.7\cdot10^{-51}.
\]
A symbolic evaluation of the same integrals was attempted and did not terminate; the
identity therefore remains HEURISTIC, now against a closed-form target and with a
phase-by-phase decomposition.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Status of Theorem~\ref{thm:cond}]\label{rem:status}
Hypothesis (ii) is Theorems~\ref{thm:rc} and \ref{thm:cross}, proved from (RC).
Hypothesis (i) splits: $\delta Q(\Sig)=0$ is Theorem~\ref{thm:el}, $\delta^2Q\preceq0$ is
Theorem~\ref{thm:concave}, and $Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$ is Remark~\ref{rem:area}, still
numerical. Hypothesis (iii), the separation $M<\tfrac12$ for competitors, is untouched;
across a family of low-frequency perturbations no competitor satisfying (RC) was found
with $M\ge\tfrac12$, but no implication is proved. So the conditional theorem now rests
on one $51$-digit numerical identity and one geometric hypothesis, rather than on four
measured conditions. Optimality of $\Sig$ remains open.
\end{remark}

\section{\texorpdfstring{$\Sig$}{Sigma} satisfies (RC), and \texorpdfstring{$Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$}{Q = A_R}}
\label{sec:rcsigma}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:rcsigma}
$\Sig$ satisfies (RC). The surface-measure density is, block by block,
\[
  H+H''=
  \begin{cases}
    0 & \theta\in[0,\beta)\ \text{and}\ \theta\in(\pi-\beta,\pi],\\
    \tfrac12 & \theta\in(\pi/2-\beta,\pi/2)\ \text{and}\ \theta\in(\pi/2,\pi/2+\beta),\\
    \tfrac{3K}{4}\cos\bigl(\tfrac\theta2+\tfrac\pi8\bigr) & \theta\in[\beta,\pi/2-\beta],\\
    \tfrac{3K}{4}\sin\bigl(\tfrac{\theta-\pi/2}{2}+\tfrac\pi8\bigr)
      & \theta\in[\pi/2+\beta,\pi-\beta],
  \end{cases}
  \qquad K=f_1\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2},
\]
plus the ceiling atom at $\theta=\pi/2$. Hence
\[
  \max(H+H'')_{\mathrm{ac}}
  =\tfrac{3K}{4}\cos\bigl(\tfrac\beta2+\tfrac\pi8\bigr)=0.8388253494\ldots<1 ,
\]
and already the amplitude satisfies $\tfrac34K=0.9765387687\ldots<1$, so (RC) holds as
soon as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rccrit}
  f_1^2\ \le\ \frac{16}{9\,(4-2\sqrt2)}=1.5174\ldots,
  \qquad\text{against } f_1^2=1.4470\ldots
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each block of $H$ is read off the three closed forms of Theorem~\ref{thm:el}. On the
first phase $H=\cos\theta+\tfrac12\sin\theta$, so $H+H''=0$; the shifted last phase is
the same. On the last phase $H=(1-\tfrac23a_1)\cos\theta+\tfrac12\sin\theta+\tfrac12$ and
on the shifted first phase $H=(2a_1-1)\sin\theta+\tfrac12\cos\theta+\tfrac12$, each giving
$H+H''=\tfrac12$. On the middle phase only the half-angle terms survive
differentiation twice, contributing a factor $1-\tfrac14$, so
$H+H''=\tfrac34(f_1\cos\tfrac\theta2+f_2\sin\tfrac\theta2)$ and, on the shifted middle
phase, $\tfrac34(f_1\sin\tfrac\theta2-f_2\cos\tfrac\theta2)$. Both have amplitude
$\tfrac34\sqrt{f_1^2+f_2^2}=\tfrac34f_1\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2}$ by
$f_2=(1-\sqrt2)f_1$, and phase $\pi/8$ because $-f_2/f_1=\sqrt2-1=\tan(\pi/8)$. On
$[\beta,\pi/2-\beta]$ the argument $\tfrac\theta2+\tfrac\pi8$ runs over
$[0.5375,1.0333]\subset[0,\pi/2]$, where cosine decreases, so the maximum is at
$\theta=\beta$.
\end{proof}

Criterion~\eqref{eq:rccrit} is strictly stronger than the separation criterion
$f_1^2<(2+\sqrt2)/2=1.7071\ldots$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}: within the family in which
$f_1$ is the free parameter, (RC) implies $M<\tfrac12$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:QA}
$Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$\Sig$ satisfies (RC) by Proposition~\ref{prop:rcsigma}, so $V(\Sig)=|N(\Sig)|$ by
Theorems~\ref{thm:rc} and~\ref{thm:cross}. The niches are disjoint by
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, and $C_2$ is $\rho$-invariant, so
$|\Sig|=|C_2|-|N|-|\rho N|=|C_2|-2|N|$. Hence
$Q(\Sig)=|C_2|-2V(\Sig)=|C_2|-2|N|=|\Sig|=A_R^\ast$.
\end{proof}

No integral is evaluated. Independently, with $A_R^\ast=1+4\tan^2\beta+\beta$ and the
integrands made elementary by Proposition~\ref{prop:sigx}, the two sides agree to
$2.7\cdot10^{-51}$ at $50$ working digits, which is a consistency check on the whole
chain rather than a proof of it.

\begin{remark}[Regression against the one-corner problem]\label{rem:gerver}
The construction does \emph{not} recover Gerver's sofa, and this should be stated
plainly. Applying the same machinery to Gerver's rotation path:
\begin{itemize}
\item Gerver satisfies the one-corner injectivity condition everywhere:
$\alpha_1>0$ and $\alpha_2>0$ on all of $[0,\pi/2]$, as expected.
\item (RC) \emph{fails} for Gerver's cap: $\max(H+H'')_{\mathrm{ac}}=1.3960$ near
$\theta=\pi$.
\item Gerver's face-2 sweep is genuinely not injective in this decomposition:
$33\,002$ of $158\,802$ tested pairs meet interior to both segments, with
$\min(s_{22}-\alpha_2)=-0.0944$. The face-1 sweep is injective.
\item Consistently, $V=0.6426146$ against $|N|=|C|-|S|=0.6406$ to $0.6412$ at $481$ and
$721$ constraints, i.e.\ $V>|N|$ as Proposition~\ref{prop:reynolds} requires.
\end{itemize}
So (RC) and the one-corner injectivity condition are independent: $\Sig$ satisfies the
first and not the second, Gerver the second and not the first. By
Remark~\ref{rem:rcclassical} the difference has a classical reading: $\Sig$'s cap is a
corridor facet plus a body that slides freely inside the unit disc, whereas part of
Gerver's cap boundary is \emph{flatter than the corridor is wide}, its curvature radius
reaching $1.3986$. The decomposition
$N=W_2\sqcup W_1^{\mathrm{out}}$ is therefore not the one the one-corner argument uses,
and the fact that it fails on the solved case is a standing caution about the framework,
recorded rather than explained.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The separation hypothesis for competitors]\label{rem:Mnum}
Hypothesis (iii) remains open. Over $250$ random perturbations
$H_\Sig+\sum_{k=1}^3c_k\sin(2k\theta)$ with $|c_k|\le a/k^2$ for
$a=0.02,0.05,0.10$, every $H$ satisfying (RC) had $M\le0.3976$, against the required
$M<\tfrac12$; the $(RC)$-satisfying fraction fell from $250/250$ to $34/250$ as $a$ grew.
No counterexample and no implication.
\end{remark}

\section{Two negative findings}\label{sec:neg}

\begin{remark}[The mirror decomposition does not rescue Gerver]\label{rem:mirror}
The decomposition of Proposition~\ref{prop:V} has a mirror image under
$t\mapsto\pi/2-t$, $\mu\leftrightarrow\nu$: sweep the inner part of face~1 and the outer
part of face~2, the latter reaching the floor at distance $\sigma_2=c_y/\sin t$. For
Gerver both give the same value, $0.6426144\ldots$, because Gerver's rotation path is
itself symmetric under $t\mapsto\pi/2-t$; both exceed $|N|\approx0.641$. So the failure of
Remark~\ref{rem:gerver} is not an artefact of orientation.

The structural reason is visible in Proposition~\ref{prop:rcsigma}. $\Sig$'s cap has
$H+H''=0$ on $[0,\beta)$ and $(\pi-\beta,\pi]$, i.e.\ \emph{vertices}, because $\Sig$'s
outer phases have constant contact arcs pinned at $(1,\tfrac12)$. Gerver's cap has no such
vertices: there $H+H''$ reaches $1.3960$ near $\theta=\pi$. The degeneracy that makes
$\Sig$ hard for local analysis is exactly what makes (RC) hold for it.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[A false bound, retracted]\label{rem:retract}
The following was attempted for hypothesis (iii) and is \textbf{false}. Solving
$H''+H=W$ with $H(0)=1$ gives
$H(\theta)=\cos\theta+H'(0)\sin\theta+\int_0^\theta\sin(\theta-s)W(s)\,ds$, and
substituting into $c_y(t)=(H(t)-1)\sin t+(H(t+\pi/2)-1)\cos t$ yields
\[
  c_y(t)=H'(0)-\sin t-\cos t+\sin t\!\int_0^t\!\sin(t-s)W\,ds
        +\cos t\!\int_0^{t+\pi/2}\!\cos(t-s)W\,ds .
\]
Both kernels are non-negative on their ranges, so ``$W\le1$'' would give $c_y\le H'(0)$,
and for a $\rho$-symmetric cap $H'(0)=\tfrac12+\tfrac12(\text{vertical right edge})$,
apparently yielding $M\le\tfrac12$.

The error is that $W$ is \emph{not} $\le1$ as a measure: it carries the ceiling atom of
mass $1.1670\ldots$ at $s=\pi/2$, and here that atom meets the kernel
$\cos(t-\pi/2)=\sin t$, which does not vanish. The corrected bound is
$M\le H'(0)+\tfrac12\cdot1.1670=1.083\ldots$, useless against the required $\tfrac12$.

This does \emph{not} affect Theorem~\ref{thm:rc}: there the same atom lands at $u=t$,
which is outside the integration range when $\tau<t$ and is the endpoint where the kernel
$\sin(\tau-u)$ vanishes when $\tau=t$; and the face-1 atom needs $u=t-\pi/2\le0$. The
placement of the atom relative to the kernel is what distinguishes the two arguments, and
it was checked in both.
\end{remark}

Hypothesis (iii) therefore remains open.

\section{Relation to prior work}\label{sec:prior}

The one-corner problem was settled by Baek \cite{Bae24}, who proved Gerver's sofa optimal.
Our reading of that paper, for the purpose of separating what is his from what is not:

\begin{itemize}
\item The \emph{arm lengths} $f,g$ are his (Definition 6.2.1), defined through the contact
points $A$ and $C$; our $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ are these shifted by the corridor width.
\item His injectivity condition (Definition 6.1.2) has three parts. Part (1) requires the
surface measure $\sigma_K$ to be absolutely continuous on $[0,\pi/2)$ and on $(\pi/2,\pi]$,
so an atom is permitted only at $\theta=\pi/2$; part (3) is
$x_K'(t)\cdot u_t<0$ and $x_K'(t)\cdot v_t>0$ on $(0,\pi/2)$.
\item Our (RC) shares part (1) verbatim and \emph{adds} a bound on the density, while
dropping part (3) entirely. No curvature bound appears in \cite{Bae24}; its only mentions
of curvature are the standard identification of the surface-measure density with the
radius of curvature, which we also use. The bound itself is classical, however: it is the
sliding-ball condition of Remark~\ref{rem:rcclassical}, so (RC) is a known condition put to
a new use rather than a new condition.
\item His concavity argument attaches Mamikon regions to the overestimating region so that
the total is linear in the cap; ours subtracts them from the cap. He works on a space of
triples $(K,B,D)$ of convex bodies; we work with the single support function $H$ on
$[0,\pi]$. Whether the two bookkeepings are equivalent we do not determine.
\item The ambidextrous problem is not treated in \cite{Bae24}, and we have found no upper
bound for it in the literature.
\end{itemize}

The constants $a_1,\beta,f_1$ and the ODE families are Romik's \cite{Rom18}; the cubics of
Section~\ref{sec:cubics} are the standard ones and we claim no priority for them. This
audit was made against \cite{Bae24} and the Kallus--Romik line \cite{KR18} only, not
against the wider literature.

\section{The main theorem}\label{sec:main}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:final}
Let $\mathcal D$ be the set of support functions $H$ on $[0,\pi]$ of admissible caps such
that
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $H(0)=H(\pi/2)=1$ \emph{(the gauge: horizontal translation, unit corridor)};
\item[(b)] \emph{(RC)} $\sigma_K$ is absolutely continuous on $[0,\pi/2)$ and $(\pi/2,\pi]$
with density at most $1$, the only atom being at $\theta=\pi/2$;
\item[(c)] $c_y(t)=(H(t)-1)\sin t+(H(t+\pi/2)-1)\cos t\le\tfrac12$ for $t\in[0,\pi/2]$
\emph{(note $\le$, not $<$: this gives $|U\cap\rho U|=0$, which is all an area bound needs)};
\item[(d)] $\alpha_1<0$ exactly on $[0,\beta)$ and $\alpha_2>0$ exactly on
$[0,\pi/2-\beta)$.
\end{enumerate}
Then $\mathcal D$ is convex, $\Sig\in\mathcal D$, and
\[
  |T|\ \le\ A_R^\ast
\]
for every ambidextrous moving sofa $T$ whose cap data lies in $\mathcal D$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each of (a)--(d) is a family of affine inequalities or equalities in $H$, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:corner} and \eqref{eq:a1}--\eqref{eq:sig}, so $\mathcal D$ is convex.
$\Sig$ satisfies (b) by Proposition~\ref{prop:rcsigma}, (c) with margin
$\tfrac12-M=0.1121\ldots$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, and (d) by definition of $\beta$.

By (b) and Theorems~\ref{thm:rc} and \ref{thm:cross} the combined sweep is injective, so
$V=|N|$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:convex}, whose proof also supplies the covering and
containment that Proposition~\ref{prop:V} assumes. By (c) and Lemma~\ref{lem:ceiling},
$U\subseteq\{y\le\tfrac12\}$ and $\rho U\subseteq\{y\ge\tfrac12\}$, so
$|U\cap\rho U|=0$; with $C_2$ $\rho$-invariant this gives
$|T|\le|C_2|-|N|-|\rho N|=|C_2|-2|N|=Q$. (Corollary~\ref{cor:sep} states the strict form
$M<\tfrac12$, which is what $\Sig$ satisfies; the null-set form used here is what the
hypothesis $\le$ delivers.) By (d) and
Theorem~\ref{thm:concave}, $\delta^2Q\le0$ on $\mathcal D$; by Theorem~\ref{thm:el},
$\delta Q(\Sig)=0$. A concave function with a critical point on a convex set attains its
maximum there, so $Q(H)\le Q(\Sig)$, and $Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:QA}.
Combining, $|T|\le Q(H)\le A_R^\ast$.
\end{proof}

Because $Q$ is exactly quadratic on the cell and $\delta Q(\Sig)=0$, the same ingredients
give more than a bound.

\begin{theorem}[Stability]\label{thm:stab}
For every ambidextrous moving sofa $T$ whose cap data $H=H_\Sig+\eta$ lies in $\mathcal D$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stab}
  |T|\ \le\ A_R^\ast-\tfrac{371}{1000}\,\|\eta\|_{L^2(0,\pi/2)}^2
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  |T|\ \le\ A_R^\ast-\tfrac{153}{1000}\,\|\eta\|_{L^2(0,\pi)}^2 .
\end{equation}
Neither implies the other: the first has the better constant, the second controls the whole
interval.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On the cell $\mathcal C$ the functional $Q$ is a quadratic polynomial in $H$, so
$Q(H_\Sig+\eta)=Q(\Sig)+\delta Q(\Sig)[\eta]+\tfrac12\delta^2Q[\eta]$ exactly. The first
variation vanishes by Theorem~\ref{thm:el} and $Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:QA}, while \eqref{eq:garding} gives
$\tfrac12\delta^2Q[\eta]\le-1.6013\,J_1-0.3710\,J_2\le-0.3710\,\|\eta\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi/2)}$,
and Proposition~\ref{prop:SL} with $\delta=0.07$, $\kappa=3$, $\lambda=0.257$ gives
coefficients $-0.4194,-0.1572,-0.1532$ on $J_1,J_2,J_3$, hence
$\tfrac12\delta^2Q[\eta]\le-0.1532\|\eta\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)}$.
Combining each with $|T|\le Q(H)$ from Theorem~\ref{thm:final} gives \eqref{eq:stab}.
\end{proof}

The second constant needs the $J_3$ coefficient to be negative, which the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:concave} does not deliver: there it is $1-P_3=0$. The fix is to stop
splitting the $E_2$ term off from the cap.

\begin{proposition}[{The $[\pi/2,\pi]$ block}]\label{prop:SL}
Write the $E_2$ bound as $-\lambda\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi-\beta}\eta'^2
+\tfrac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\int_0^{\pi/2-\beta}\eta^2$ and split
$\lambda=\lambda_r+\lambda_J$, with $\lambda_r=B\beta^2/2$ absorbing the $r^2$ remainder as
before. Then the $[\pi/2,\pi]$ block of $\tfrac12\delta^2Q$ is
\[
  \int_{\pi/2}^\pi\eta^2-\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi-\beta}(1+\lambda_J)\eta'^2
  -\int_{\pi-\beta}^\pi\eta'^2 ,
\]
whose sign is governed by the weighted Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue
\[
  \Lambda(\lambda_J)=\min\frac{\int_{\pi/2}^\pi w\,\eta'^2}{\int_{\pi/2}^\pi\eta^2},
  \qquad w=1+\lambda_J \text{ on }[\pi/2,\pi-\beta],\quad w=1\text{ on }[\pi-\beta,\pi],
\]
over $\eta$ with $\eta(\pi/2)=0$ and $\eta(\pi)$ free. The $J_3$ coefficient is
$1-\Lambda(\lambda_J)$, and
\[
  \begin{array}{r|cccccc}
    \lambda_J & 0 & 0.05 & 0.10 & 0.165 & 0.25 & 0.35\\\hline
    \Lambda & 1 & 1.0495 & 1.0989 & 1.1629 & 1.2468 & 1.3452
  \end{array}
\]
so $\Lambda(0)=1$ exactly, recovering the marginal case $P_3=1$, and $\Lambda>1$ for every
$\lambda_J>0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}[Uniqueness]\label{cor:uniq}
$\Sig$ is the unique ambidextrous moving sofa of area $A_R^\ast$ whose cap data lies in
$\mathcal D$; every other one has strictly smaller area, by the amount \eqref{eq:stab}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Equality in \eqref{eq:stab} forces $\|\eta\|_{L^2(0,\pi/2)}=0$, and then $\eta\equiv0$ on
$[0,\pi/2]$; Theorem~\ref{thm:concave} shows $\delta^2Q<0$ strictly unless $\eta\equiv0$
throughout, since with $\eta\equiv0$ on $[0,\pi/2]$ the $E_2$ term of \eqref{eq:Q2}
contributes $-\int_0^{\pi/2-\beta}\eta'(t+\pi/2)^2dt<0$ unless $\eta$ is constant on
$[\pi/2,\pi]$, and $\eta(\pi/2)=0$ makes that constant zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Beyond $\Sig$'s own cell]\label{prop:beyond}
$Q$ is $C^1$ and piecewise quadratic, so if every cell met by the segment
$[H_\Sig,H]$ has $\delta^2Q\le0$, then $Q$ is concave along that segment and
$Q(H)\le Q(\Sig)+\delta Q(\Sig)[H-H_\Sig]=A_R^\ast$. Convexity of the union of those cells
is not needed. In particular this holds whenever the segment meets only cells whose sign
sets are the anchored intervals $E_1=[0,\tau_1)$, $E_2=[0,\tau_2)$ with $\tau_1\le\tau_2$:
across $(\tau_1,\tau_2)=(0,\tfrac\pi2),(0.1,0.2),(0.3,0.5),(0.5,0.9),(0.8,1.2),
(1.0,1.3),(1.2,1.4),(1.4,1.5),(1.5,\tfrac\pi2),(0.2,1.5),(0.05,0.06)$ the values of
$\lambda_{\max}$ are $-0.863,-0.139,-0.355,-0.533,-0.445,-0.340,-0.228,-0.109,-0.047,
-0.777,-0.040$, all negative, with $\Sig$'s own cell giving $-0.723$.
\end{proposition}

Since $\Sig$ sits at $(\beta,\pi/2-\beta)$ with $\beta<\pi/2-\beta$, it lies in that
family, and the class of competitors covered is strictly larger than $\mathcal D$ and is
checkable from the sign pattern alone. (The restriction to \emph{anchored} intervals is
essential: Remark~\ref{rem:conc} exhibits $E_1=E_2=[0.4,1.2]$, unanchored, with
$\lambda_{\max}=+0.407$.)

The constant in \eqref{eq:stab} is the one the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:concave} delivers.
Measured, the sharp constant for the $L^2(0,\pi)$ norm is larger:
$\sup\tfrac12\delta^2Q[\eta]/\|\eta\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)}$ equals
$-0.7339,-0.7331,-0.7323$ at $m=64,128,256$. The factor between $0.371$ and
$0.732$ is the $J_3$ coefficient that the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:concave} discards:
$P_3=1$ makes $\int_{\pi/2}^\pi(\eta^2-\eta'^2)\le0$ only marginally, and recovering a
positive coefficient there needs the $E_2$ term to be spent on $[\pi/2,\pi]$ rather than
on $J_1$ and $J_2$. We have not carried that out.

\begin{proposition}[$H^1$ stability]\label{prop:h1}
The second variation controls the derivative as well: measured against correctly assembled
mass and stiffness matrices, the largest $c_0$ with
$\tfrac12\delta^2Q[\eta]\le-c_0\|\eta\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)}-c_1\|\eta'\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)}$ is
\[
  \begin{array}{r|cccccc}
    c_1 & 0 & 0.1 & 0.3 & 0.5 & 0.7 & 1.0\\\hline
    c_0 & 0.7323 & 0.6114 & 0.3571 & 0.0760 & -0.2560 & -1.0000
  \end{array}
\]
at $m=256$, each converging in $m$. So $\tfrac12\delta^2Q\le-0.357\|\eta\|^2_{L^2}
-0.3\|\eta'\|^2_{L^2}$, and the frontier is close to $c_0=0.732-1.31\,c_1$, vanishing near
$c_1=0.56$.
\end{proposition}

Two simplifications are worth recording. First, the $\sigma$ term has no singularity:
since $-2\int\eta\eta'\tan t=-\int(\eta^2)'\tan t=\int\eta^2\sec^2t$ when
$\eta(0)=\eta(\pi/2)=0$, and $\sec^2t-\tan^2t=1$,
\[
  -\int_0^{\pi/2}\bigl(\eta\tan t+\eta'\bigr)^2dt
  =\int_0^{\pi/2}\bigl(\eta^2-\eta'^2\bigr)dt ,
\]
checked to $10^{-12}$ on five test functions, so \eqref{eq:Q2} may be written with bounded
coefficients throughout. Second, the sharp $L^2$ constant is $0.7323$, not the $0.7285$ of
an earlier draft; that figure came from a mass matrix that gave the endpoint hat at
$\theta=\pi$ the interior weight $2h/3$ instead of $h/3$.

\begin{remark}[The size of $\mathcal D$, and what Theorem~\ref{thm:final} is not]
\label{rem:notopt}
It is not a proof that $\Sig$ is optimal, and the reason is quantitative.

Measuring the radius of $\mathcal D$ along $H_\Sig+\varepsilon\sin(2k\theta)$ gives
$\varepsilon_k=0.0864,\ 0.0117,\ 0.0047,\ 0.0036,\ 0.0021$ at $k=2,4,6,8,10$, so
$\varepsilon_kk^2$ stays between $0.17$ and $0.35$: the radius scales like $k^{-2}$.
$\mathcal D$ therefore contains a ball in the $C^2$ norm and in no weaker one, which is
exactly what a bound on $H''$ should give. It is a genuine ball rather than a sliver ---
of $60$ random eight-mode perturbations with coefficients of size $0.02/k^2$, $51$ land
inside --- but every member is a small $C^2$-perturbation of $\Sig$.
\textbf{Theorem~\ref{thm:final} is thus a $C^2$-local statement}, with explicit convex
hypotheses in place of a neighbourhood of unspecified size.

In every one of those probes the binding constraint was (b); condition (c) never became
active. That is a statement about the probe and not about the geometry: (c) is
\emph{independent} of (b). Adding $\delta\sin(\theta-\pi/2)$ on $[\pi/2,\pi]$ and $0$ on
$[0,\pi/2]$ leaves $H+H''$ identically unchanged, so (b) and the gauge hold exactly, while
the ceiling facet grows by $\delta$ and $c_y$ grows by $\delta\sin t\cos t$; at
$\delta=0.2243\ldots$ the maximum $M$ crosses $\tfrac12$. So (c) cannot be derived and must
remain a hypothesis.

$\mathcal D$ does not reach Gerver's cap, and by Remark~\ref{rem:sharp} injectivity
genuinely fails once the density exceeds $1$. The failure is sharp in the condition but
gentle in magnitude: comparing the flux $\tfrac12\int(\alpha_2^+)^2$ with the area actually
swept, the excess is $5.4\cdot10^{-7}$ at density $1.047$ and $7.4\cdot10^{-5}$ at $1.267$,
and Gerver's own excess is about $0.25\%$. Widening $\mathcal D$ therefore does not need a
weaker constant; it needs an upper bound on that excess which is \emph{concave} in $H$, so
that subtracting it preserves the concavity of $Q$. Truncating the sweep at
$\inf_{t'}s_{22}(t,t')$ fails because that infimum is concave in $H$ and
$\tfrac12\lambda^2$ then loses the convexity the Mamikon step supplies; the first-capture
decomposition is exact but its parameter domain stops being of the form
$0\le s\le\alpha_2$, so its area is no longer quadratic in $H$.
So the honest reading is that the one-corner architecture transfers to the ambidextrous
problem on an explicit convex $C^2$-neighbourhood of $\Sig$, and that enlarging that
neighbourhood is where the remaining difficulty sits.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[(RC) is sharp]\label{rem:sharp}
The constant $1$ in the forcing $R=1-(G+G'')$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:rc} is the corridor
width: it enters through $c(t)=(F-1)\mu_t+(G-1)\nu_t$, so
$\langle c(t),\nu_{t'}\rangle=n+G-1$ and $n''=-n+1-(G+G'')$. The argument therefore cannot
tolerate a density above $1$. Nor can the conclusion: perturbing $\Sig$ so that the maximum
density passes through $1$, the face-2 sweep acquires self-intersections exactly there. At
densities $0.838,0.921,0.976$ there are none; at $1.004,1.047,1.119$ there are
$160,1510,3086$ among $\binom{301}{2}$-many tested pairs, and a second mode gives $0$ at
$0.976$ against $200$ and $526$ at $1.045$ and $1.114$. So (b) is sharp as stated.
\end{remark}
