\begin{abstract}
Baek proved that Gerver's sofa is the maximum-area moving sofa for a corridor with one
corner, by writing a sofa as a convex cap minus a niche and obtaining optimality from the
concavity of a quadratic upper bound. We carry that architecture to the ambidextrous
moving sofa problem, which is open, and to Romik's candidate $\Sig$ of area
$A_R^\ast=1.6449552184\ldots$

The niche of an ambidextrous sofa is expressed exactly in convex-linear data: writing
$H(\theta)=h_K(\mu_\theta)$ for the cap's support function, the two arms
$\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ and the reach $\sigma$ are affine in $H$, and
$|N|=\int_0^{\pi/2}[\tfrac12(\alpha_2^+)^2+\tfrac12(\sigma-\alpha_1)^2
-\tfrac12(\alpha_1^-)^2]\,dt$ whenever the associated sweep is injective. Injectivity
follows from a single linear condition (RC), that the cap's radius of curvature be at most
the corridor width; the proof reduces each of the three required statements to a forced
harmonic oscillator whose kernel is non-negative because the rotation angle is $\pi/2$.
(RC) is classical: by the dual of Blaschke's rolling theorem it says the cap slides
freely inside a unit ball, up to the corridor facets. What is new is its use.

For the functional $Q=|C_2|-2V$ we prove $Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$; that $\delta Q(\Sig)=0$, so
Romik's ODE families are exactly the critical-point equations of $Q$, identically in his
constants; and that $\delta^2Q<0$, with an explicit constant. Two Cauchy--Schwarz steps
decouple the two halves of $[0,\pi]$ and reduce the second variation to a pair of
Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problems, certified from below by Sturm oscillation; this
gives $\tfrac12\delta^2Q\le-\tfrac23\|\eta\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)}$. Not splitting at all
turns the second variation into a single $2\times2$ system whose first eigenvalue is the
sharp constant $c^\ast=0.7309566\ldots$, certified to exceed $\tfrac{73}{100}$. The same
system, with the cell boundaries as parameters, proves concavity for every cell whose
sign sets are ordered and anchored, by a one-line monotonicity reduction to a
one-parameter family certified by an adaptive interval covering; on the mirror class the
constant is uniform, $\tfrac3{10}$, and the resulting stability bound assumes no
smallness of $H-H_\Sig$ beyond (RC). On the
convex domain $\mathcal D$ cut out by (RC), a separation inequality and $\Sig$'s own sign
pattern,
\[
  |T|\ \le\ A_R^\ast-\tfrac{73}{100}\,\|H-H_\Sig\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)}
\]
for every ambidextrous sofa $T$ with cap data in $\mathcal D$, and $\Sig$ is the unique
member attaining $A_R^\ast$. The domain is a $C^2$-neighbourhood of $\Sig$: its radius
along $\sin(2k\theta)$ scales like $k^{-2}$.

Two limits are recorded rather than smoothed over. The separation inequality is
independent of (RC) and cannot be removed. And $V$ is the exact flux of the advancing
wedge boundary, so $V\ge|N|$ by Reynolds' transport theorem and $Q$ bounds nothing where
the sweep fails to be injective; in particular the construction does not recover Gerver's
sofa, whose cap violates (RC). Separately, a two-hallway relaxation gives the first
upper bound specific to the ambidextrous problem: the area of a left- and a right-turning
hallway at angle $\pi/4$ is bounded by translates of one explicit profile integral, and a
four-case analysis of the corner offsets removes every hypothesis, giving
\[
  A_{\mathrm{ambi}}\ \le\ 2\sqrt2-1\ =\ 1.8284271\ldots
\]
unconditionally. The bound is attained by an explicit placement, so it is exactly the
value of the two-hallway relaxation; it improves on the $2.2195$ inherited from the
one-corner theorem, and with Romik's sofa it brackets the ambidextrous supremum within a
factor of $1.1115$. Optimality of $\Sig$ remains open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The moving sofa problem asks for the largest area of a connected shape that can be
manoeuvred around a right-angled corner of a hallway of unit width. It was settled by
Baek \cite{Bae24}, who proved Gerver's sofa \cite{Ger92} optimal. Romik \cite{Rom18}
posed the \emph{ambidextrous} variant, in which the shape must turn corners of both
handedness, exhibited a candidate $\Sig$ of area
\[
  A_R^\ast=1.6449552184254408\ldots,
\]
and asked whether it is optimal. That question is open, and this note does not settle it.
What follows is a structural analysis of the ambidextrous problem in the coordinates in
which the constraint data is affine, and a local optimality statement for $\Sig$ on an
explicitly described domain.

\subsection*{The coordinates}

Every ambidextrous sofa is $C_2\setminus(U\sqcup\rho U)$ for a convex \emph{cap} $C_2$ and
a \emph{niche} $U$ swept by the hallway's inner corner, with $\rho$ the reflection in
$y=\tfrac12$. Writing $H(\theta)=h_{C_2}(\mu_\theta)$ for the cap's support function on
$[0,\pi]$, the corner path is \emph{affine} in $H$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:corner}), and so are the
two arm lengths $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ and the reach $\sigma$ that determine the sweep. In
these coordinates the cap area is an exact quadratic form,
\[
  |C_2|=\int_0^\pi(H^2-H'^2)\,d\theta-H(0)-H(\pi)
\]
(Proposition~\ref{prop:cap}), and the niche area has the closed form \eqref{eq:V}. The
functional $Q:=|C_2|-2V$, with $V$ the flux of the advancing sweep, is therefore an
explicit quadratic polynomial in $H$ on each cell of the sign pattern of
$(\alpha_1,\alpha_2)$.

\subsection*{The results}

The analysis rests on one geometric hypothesis, that the cap is nowhere flatter than a
circle of the corridor's width:
\[
  \text{(RC)}\qquad (H+H'')_{\mathrm{ac}}\le1 ,
\]
which is linear in $H$ and hence convex. It is classical, being the sliding-ball condition
dual to Blaschke's rolling theorem (Remark~\ref{rem:rcclassical}); what is new is its use.
Theorem~\ref{thm:rc} shows that (RC) implies all three injectivity conditions of the
sweep, by reducing each to a forced harmonic oscillator whose kernel is non-negative
precisely because the rotation angle is $\pi/2$; consequently $V=|N|$ and $Q$ is tight.

At $\Sig$ the three hypotheses of the concavity method hold exactly:
$Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$ structurally, with no integral evaluated (Theorem~\ref{thm:QA});
$\delta Q(\Sig)=0$ identically in Romik's constants, so his ODE families \emph{are} the
critical-point equations of $Q$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:el}); and $\delta^2Q<0$.

The second variation is the technical core. Setting $p(t)=\eta(t)$ and $q(t)=\eta(t+\pi/2)$
identifies it with a $2\times2$ Sturm--Liouville system on $[0,\pi/2]$ whose first
eigenvalue \emph{is} the best constant,
\[
  c^\ast=0.7309566\ldots,
\]
certified above $\tfrac{73}{100}$ in ball arithmetic (Theorem~\ref{thm:system}). On the
overlap of the two sign sets the coupling is a total derivative, which is why every
pointwise estimate loses and this one does not. The same system, with the cell boundaries
as parameters, gives concavity on \emph{every} ordered anchored cell
(Theorem~\ref{thm:diag}), and a uniform constant $\tfrac3{10}$ on the mirror class
(Theorem~\ref{thm:mirroruniform}).

These combine into two optimality statements. Theorem~\ref{thm:final} gives
$|T|\le A_R^\ast$ for every ambidextrous sofa with cap data in the convex domain
$\mathcal D$ cut out by the gauge, (RC), a separation inequality and $\Sig$'s sign
pattern, with the quantitative form
\[
  |T|\ \le\ A_R^\ast-\tfrac{73}{100}\,\|H-H_\Sig\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)}
\]
of Theorem~\ref{thm:stab} and uniqueness (Corollary~\ref{cor:uniq}). Proposition
\ref{prop:ball} exhibits an explicit $C^2$-ball of radius $\tfrac1{20}$ inside
$\mathcal D$. Theorem~\ref{thm:mirrorstab} is the non-perturbative version: on the mirror
class, with no smallness of $H-H_\Sig$ assumed, $|T|\le A_R^\ast-\tfrac3{10}\|H-H_\Sig\|^2$.

\subsection*{What is not proved, and why}

What the local result addresses is Romik's own first open problem \cite[\S7]{Rom18}, which
asks for a proof that Gerver's sofa and $\Sig$ are local maxima of the area functional in
the one-corner and ambidextrous problems respectively. Romik established the first-order
conditions for $\Sig$; the second-order statement has not been proved, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:final} proves it only on the domain $\mathcal D$, so that problem stays
open. For Gerver's sofa the corresponding question is settled a fortiori by \cite{Bae24}.

Optimality of $\Sig$ remains open, and the obstructions are recorded rather than smoothed
over. $V$ is the exact flux of the advancing sweep, so $V\ge|N|$ always by Reynolds'
transport theorem (Proposition~\ref{prop:reynolds}) and $Q$ bounds nothing where the sweep
fails to be injective; the domain cannot be widened by a concave majorant of the excess,
since none exists (Proposition~\ref{prop:nogo}), though a semiconcave one does
(Theorem~\ref{thm:budget}). The separation hypothesis is independent of (RC). Gerver's cap
violates (RC), but that is not what excludes it: it is not the cap of any \emph{connected}
ambidextrous sofa (Remark~\ref{rem:gerverout}). The sign hypothesis of
Theorem~\ref{thm:mirrorstab} is permanent, not provisional: the incircle of the unit
square is a connected ambidextrous sofa satisfying every other condition and violating it
(Remark~\ref{rem:disc}).

Claims are labelled throughout: \textsc{verified} means machine-checked in Lean~4 with no
\texttt{sorry}, \textsc{proved} a complete human proof, \textsc{heuristic} computational
evidence only. Numerical values that carry weight are computed in ball or exact rational
arithmetic with stated precision; Section~\ref{sec:prior} separates what is ours from what
is Baek's and Romik's.

\section{Setting}

Write $L$ for the closed L-shaped hallway of unit width with its corner at the
origin, $\mu_t=(\cos t,\sin t)$, $\nu_t=(-\sin t,\cos t)$, and for a rotation
path $c:[0,\pi/2]\to\R^2$ let
\[
  C_t=\{p:\langle p-c(t),\mu_t\rangle\le 1\}\cap\{p:\langle p-c(t),\nu_t\rangle\le 1\},
  \qquad
  Q_t=\{p:\langle p-c(t),\mu_t\rangle<0\}\cap\{p:\langle p-c(t),\nu_t\rangle<0\},
\]
so that the hallway in position $t$ is $H_t=C_t\setminus Q_t$. The one-sided
sofa is $S=\bigcap_t H_t$, and writing $C=\bigcap_t C_t$ and
$U=\bigcup_t Q_t$ we have $S=C\setminus U$ with $C$ convex.

Let $\rho(x,y)=(x,1-y)$ be the reflection in the line $y=\tfrac12$. Romik's
ambidextrous sofa is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma}
  \Sig=S\cap\rho(S)=C_2\setminus\bigl(U\cup\rho U\bigr),
  \qquad C_2:=C\cap\rho C \text{ convex},
\end{equation}
with $|\Sig|=A_R^\ast=1.6449552184\ldots$, and $c$ is Romik's piecewise path with
breakpoints at $\beta$ and $\pi/2-\beta$, where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta}
  \beta=\arctanop\Bigl(\tfrac12\bigl(\sqrt[3]{\sqrt2+1}-\sqrt[3]{\sqrt2-1}\bigr)\Bigr)
      =0.2896538208\ldots
\end{equation}

Baek's argument for the one-corner problem builds an overestimating region whose
area is a quadratic functional on a convex domain of convex bodies, and proves it
concave by exhibiting it as a linear functional minus Mamikon regions, whose areas
are convex quadratics. Concavity reduces global optimality to a first-order
condition. The construction is organised around $S=K\setminus\mathcal N(K)$: one
cap, one niche.

By \eqref{eq:sigma} the ambidextrous analogue has \emph{two} niches, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ie}
  |U\cup\rho U|=|U|+|\rho U|-|U\cap\rho U|.
\end{equation}
The last term appears in the upper bound with a $+$ sign. Since concavity is
obtained by \emph{subtracting} convex quadratics, an added overlap term is not
covered by the mechanism. The purpose of this note is Theorem~\ref{thm:main}:
the term is zero.

\section{The niches are disjoint}

\begin{lemma}[Niche ceiling]\label{lem:ceiling}
For every $t\in[0,\pi/2]$ we have $Q_t\subseteq\{(x,y):y\le c_y(t)\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$Q_t$ is the open convex cone with apex $c(t)$ spanned by $-\mu_t$ and $-\nu_t$,
so $q\in Q_t$ means $q-c(t)=-a\mu_t-b\nu_t$ with $a,b>0$. For $t\in[0,\pi/2]$
both $\sin t\ge0$ and $\cos t\ge0$, hence
\[
  q_y-c_y(t)=-a\sin t-b\cos t\le 0. \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:sep}
Put $M:=\max_{t\in[0,\pi/2]}c_y(t)$. Then $U\subseteq\{y\le M\}$ and
$\rho U\subseteq\{y\ge 1-M\}$. If $M<\tfrac12$ then $U\cap\rho U=\emptyset$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first inclusion is Lemma~\ref{lem:ceiling} applied to each $t$; the second is
its image under $\rho$. If $M<\tfrac12$ then $1-M>\tfrac12>M$, so the two
half-planes are disjoint.
\end{proof}

It remains to evaluate $M$ for Romik's path. On the middle piece
$[\beta,\pi/2-\beta]$ Romik's path is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:x6}
  c(t)=R(t)v(t)+\kappa,\qquad
  v(t)=\begin{pmatrix} f_1\cos\frac t2+f_2\sin\frac t2-1\\[2pt]
                      -f_2\cos\frac t2+f_1\sin\frac t2-1\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with $R(t)$ the rotation by $t$, and the two facts we use are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kf}
  \kappa_y=\tfrac12 \quad\text{exactly},
  \qquad f_2=(1-\sqrt2)\,f_1 .
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:cy}
For $t\in[\beta,\pi/2-\beta]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cy}
  c_y(t)-\tfrac12
   = f_1\sin\tfrac{3t}{2}-f_2\cos\tfrac{3t}{2}-(\sin t+\cos t)
   = f_1\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2}\,\sin\!\Bigl(\tfrac{3t}{2}+\tfrac\pi8\Bigr)-\sqrt2\,\sin\!\Bigl(t+\tfrac\pi4\Bigr).
\end{equation}
In particular $c_y$ is symmetric about $t=\pi/4$ on this interval, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M}
  M=c_y(\pi/4)=\tfrac12-\Bigl(\sqrt2-f_1\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:x6} and $\kappa_y=\tfrac12$, $c_y(t)-\tfrac12=\sin t\,v_x+\cos t\,v_y$.
Write $S=\sin\frac t2$, $C=\cos\frac t2$, so $\sin t=2SC$ and $\cos t=C^2-S^2$.
Expanding and collecting the coefficients of $f_1$ and $f_2$ gives
\[
  \sin t\,v_x+\cos t\,v_y
  = f_1 S(3C^2-S^2)+f_2 C(3S^2-C^2)-(\sin t+\cos t),
\]
and the triple-angle identities $S(3C^2-S^2)=\sin\frac{3t}{2}$,
$C(3S^2-C^2)=-\cos\frac{3t}{2}$ give the first equality in \eqref{eq:cy}. For the
second, substitute $f_2=(1-\sqrt2)f_1$ from \eqref{eq:kf}: the bracket becomes
$\sin\frac{3t}{2}+(\sqrt2-1)\cos\frac{3t}{2}$, of amplitude
$\sqrt{1+(\sqrt2-1)^2}=\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2}$ and phase $\arctanop(\sqrt2-1)=\pi/8$.

Both sinusoids in \eqref{eq:cy} are invariant under $t\mapsto\pi/2-t$: indeed
$\sin(\tfrac{3}{2}(\pi/2-t)+\pi/8)=\sin(7\pi/8-\tfrac{3t}{2})=\sin(\pi/8+\tfrac{3t}{2})$
and $\sin(3\pi/4-t)=\sin(\pi/4+t)$. Hence $c_y$ is symmetric about $\pi/4$, where
both sinusoids attain the value $1$ simultaneously; \eqref{eq:M} follows. \end{proof}

That $\pi/4$ maximises $c_y$ over all of $[0,\pi/2]$, and not merely over the
middle piece, follows from the corresponding closed form on the outer pieces.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:outer}
For $t\in[0,\beta]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cyouter}
  c_y(t)=\tfrac12+a_1\sin 2t-\sin t-\tfrac12\cos t,
\end{equation}
and $c_y$ is strictly increasing there, so $\max_{[0,\beta]}c_y=c_y(\beta)$. By
the symmetry $c_y(t)=c_y(\pi/2-t)$ the same bound holds on $[\pi/2-\beta,\pi/2]$.
Consequently $M=c_y(\pi/4)$ as claimed in \eqref{eq:M}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On $[0,\beta)$ Romik's path is $c(t)=R(t)v_1(t)+\kappa^{(1)}$ with
$v_1(t)=(a_1\cos t-1,\,a_1\sin t-\tfrac12)$ and $\kappa^{(1)}_y=\tfrac12$, so
$c_y(t)-\tfrac12=\sin t\,v_{1x}+\cos t\,v_{1y}=2a_1\sin t\cos t-\sin t-\tfrac12\cos t$,
which is \eqref{eq:cyouter}. Differentiating,
\[
  c_y'(t)=2a_1\cos 2t-\cos t+\tfrac12\sin t
        \ \ge\ 2a_1\cos 2\beta-1\ =\ 0.4650\ldots>0
        \qquad (t\in[0,\beta]),
\]
using $\cos 2t\ge\cos 2\beta$ and $\cos t-\tfrac12\sin t\le 1$ for $t\ge0$.
Hence $c_y$ is strictly increasing on $[0,\beta]$, with
$c_y(\beta)=0.2143795161\ldots$, which is below
$c_y(\pi/4)=0.3878381292\ldots$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Evaluating \eqref{eq:cyouter} and \eqref{eq:cy} at $t=\beta$ gives the same value
to $4.4\cdot 10^{-31}$ at $30$-digit precision. Since the two expressions were
derived from different pieces of Romik's path, this is an independent check on
both, and on \eqref{eq:f1}.
\end{remark}

\section{A closed form for $f_1$}

Romik's constant $f_1$ is recorded in the literature as the decimal
$1.202938908156911389$. The following determines it in closed form. Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:a1}
  a_1=\tfrac14\sqrt{\,4+\sqrt[3]{71+8\sqrt2}+\sqrt[3]{71-8\sqrt2}\,}
     =0.8752873624\ldots
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:f1}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:f1}
  f_1=\frac{\tfrac43\,a_1\cos\beta}{\cos\frac\beta2+(1-\sqrt2)\sin\frac\beta2}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On $[0,\beta)$ Romik's path is $c(t)=R(t)v_1(t)+\kappa^{(1)}$ with
$v_1(t)=(a_1\cos t-1,\;a_1\sin t-\tfrac12)$ and
$\kappa^{(1)}=(1-a_1,\tfrac12)$; on $[\beta,\pi/2-\beta]$ it is \eqref{eq:x6}
with $\kappa=(1-\tfrac43 a_1,\tfrac12)$. Both $\kappa$'s have second coordinate
$\tfrac12$ and their first coordinates differ by exactly $\tfrac13 a_1$.
Continuity at $t=\beta$ therefore gives
$R(\beta)\bigl(v_1(\beta)-v(\beta)\bigr)=(-\tfrac13a_1,0)$, so
\[
  v_1(\beta)-v(\beta)=R(-\beta)\bigl(-\tfrac13a_1,0\bigr)
     =\bigl(-\tfrac13a_1\cos\beta,\;\tfrac13a_1\sin\beta\bigr).
\]
Reading the first coordinate and using $f_2=(1-\sqrt2)f_1$,
\[
  \bigl(a_1\cos\beta-1\bigr)-\Bigl(f_1\bigl(\cos\tfrac\beta2+(1-\sqrt2)\sin\tfrac\beta2\bigr)-1\Bigr)
   =-\tfrac13a_1\cos\beta,
\]
which rearranges to \eqref{eq:f1}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Evaluating \eqref{eq:f1} at $50$ digits gives
$f_1=1.202938908156911389070223\ldots$, so the tabulated decimal is its rounding.
As an independent check, the \emph{second} coordinate of the junction relation,
which was not used in the derivation, is satisfied to $1.7\cdot10^{-51}$ at the
same precision.
\end{remark}

\section{Disjointness of the niches for \texorpdfstring{$\Sig$}{Sigma}}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For Romik's ambidextrous sofa, $U\cap\rho U=\emptyset$, and consequently
\[
  |\Sig|=|C_2|-|U|-|\rho U|=|C_2|-2|U| .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:cy}, $M<\tfrac12$ is equivalent to
$f_1\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2}<\sqrt2$, i.e. to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ineq}
  f_1^2<\frac{2+\sqrt2}{2}.
\end{equation}
With $f_1$ given by \eqref{eq:f1} in terms of the algebraic numbers
\eqref{eq:beta} and \eqref{eq:a1}, both sides of \eqref{eq:ineq} are explicit
algebraic numbers, and
\[
  f_1^2=1.4470620167577420940\ldots,
  \qquad \frac{2+\sqrt2}{2}=1.7071067811865475244\ldots,
\]
so \eqref{eq:ineq} holds with margin $0.2600447644\ldots$. Corollary~\ref{cor:sep}
gives $U\cap\rho U=\emptyset$, and then \eqref{eq:ie} together with
\eqref{eq:sigma}. Finally $|\rho U|=|U|$ because $\rho$ is an isometry.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
$M=0.3878381292441942964\ldots$, the two niches are separated by the horizontal
band of width $1-2M=0.2243237415116114\ldots$ about $y=\tfrac12$, and the
ambidextrous upper bound requires only \emph{one} niche to be modelled: one core
and two tails, the same shape as in the one-corner problem.
\end{corollary}

\section{The quotient reduction and a conditional statement}

Theorem~\ref{thm:main} has a consequence that changes the shape of the
ambidextrous problem. Since $U\subseteq\{y\le M\}$ with $M<\tfrac12$, the whole
lower niche lies below the symmetry axis, so intersecting with the lower
half-strip deletes $\rho U$ outright.

\begin{corollary}[Quotient reduction]\label{cor:quot}
Put $K^-:=C_2\cap\{y\le\tfrac12\}$, a convex body. Then
\[
  \Sig\cap\{y\le\tfrac12\}=K^-\setminus U,
  \qquad
  |\Sig|=2\bigl(|K^-|-|U\cap K^-|\bigr).
\]
\end{corollary}

That is a convex cap minus \emph{one} niche: the shape Baek's argument is built
on. Numerically, at $n=1441$ hallways, $|\Sig|=1.645059395$ and
$|\Sig\cap\{y\le\frac12\}|=0.822529698$, whose double reproduces $|\Sig|$ to
$3.8\cdot10^{-15}$.

Two cautions must be recorded, because they bound what
Corollary~\ref{cor:quot} delivers.

First, $M<\tfrac12$ is a property of Romik's trajectory, not of an arbitrary
competitor. A competitor with $M\ge\tfrac12$ has overlapping niches, and by
\eqref{eq:ie} overlap \emph{increases} $|\Sig|$; so it cannot be assumed away on
extremal grounds.

Second, and this is what makes the reduction usable, connectedness does
appear to exclude it, but not for the reason one first tries. A \emph{single}
pair $Q_t\cup\rho Q_t$ removes a full vertical segment, and hence disconnects any
body meeting both sides of it, exactly when the apex height exceeds a threshold
$h^\ast(t)$; and computation gives $h^\ast(t)>\tfrac12$ throughout $(0,\pi/2)$,
with $h^\ast(t)\to\tfrac12$ as $t\to0$, $h^\ast(\pi/4)=0.5100$ and
$h^\ast(1.52)=0.6967$. So no single-$t$ argument forces $M\le\tfrac12$; all it
gives is the pointwise constraint $c_y(t)\le h^\ast(t)$.

In fact a single pair does force it, and the earlier reading to the contrary was
an artifact of testing the vertical line at a fixed distance from the apex.

\begin{theorem}[Connectedness ceiling]\label{thm:ceiling}
Let $p=c(t_0)$ with $t_0\in(0,\pi/2)$ and suppose $p_y>\tfrac12$. Put
\[
  \varepsilon_0:=\frac{2p_y-1}{2\tan t_0}>0 .
\]
Then for every $\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_0)$ the vertical line $x=p_x-\varepsilon$
is contained in $Q_{t_0}\cup\rho Q_{t_0}$. Hence any ambidextrous moving sofa omits
the open vertical strip $\{p_x-\varepsilon_0<x<p_x\}$, and a connected one lies
entirely in one of the two half-planes it separates.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and consider $q=(p_x-\varepsilon,y)$. The set $Q_{t_0}$ is the
open cone at $p$ spanned by $-\mu_{t_0}$ and $-\nu_{t_0}$, whose directions occupy
the angular interval $[t_0+\pi,\,t_0+\tfrac{3\pi}{2}]$. Writing $h:=p_y-y$, the
direction $q-p=(-\varepsilon,-h)$ has angle $\pi+\arctan(h/\varepsilon)$ when
$h>0$, so $q\in Q_{t_0}$ iff $\arctan(h/\varepsilon)\ge t_0$, that is iff
\[
  y\le p_y-\varepsilon\tan t_0 .
\]
Applying $\rho$ and using $\rho q-\rho p=R(q-p)$ with $R=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$,
the reflected cone $\rho Q_{t_0}$ has apex $(p_x,1-p_y)$ and occupies the angular
interval $[\tfrac\pi2-t_0,\,\pi-t_0]$, giving by the same computation
\[
  q\in\rho Q_{t_0}\iff y\ge 1-p_y+\varepsilon\tan t_0 .
\]
The two sets therefore cover the whole line exactly when
$p_y-\varepsilon\tan t_0\ge 1-p_y+\varepsilon\tan t_0$, i.e. when
$2\varepsilon\tan t_0\le 2p_y-1$, i.e. when $\varepsilon\le\varepsilon_0$. Since
$p_y>\tfrac12$ we have $\varepsilon_0>0$ and the range is nonempty. Taking the
union over $\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_0)$ gives the strip.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The criterion is sharp in both directions: if $p_y\le\tfrac12$ then
$\varepsilon_0\le0$ and the two cones leave a gap at \emph{every} distance from the
apex, which is why $\tfrac12$ is exactly the threshold. It also explains a
numerical trap: $\varepsilon_0$ can be very small (at $t_0=1.3$, $p_y=0.55$ it is
$0.0139$), so sampling the line at a fixed distance such as $0.02$ detects no
covering and suggests, wrongly, that a single pair never separates. The criterion
was checked against direct computation at seven pairs $(t_0,p_y)$ on both sides of
$\varepsilon_0$, and at three pairs with $p_y<\tfrac12$.
\end{remark}

Granting that an ambidextrous sofa cannot lie in one of the two half-planes
separated by such a strip, since it must meet both arms of the hallway
$H_{t_0}$ whose inner corner is $p$, Theorem~\ref{thm:ceiling} yields
$\max_t c_y(t)\le\tfrac12$ for every connected ambidextrous moving sofa, and the
hypothesis of the next proposition is automatic.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional form]\label{prop:cond}
Let $S$ be an ambidextrous moving sofa with rotation path $c$ satisfying
$\max_t c_y(t)\le\tfrac12$. Then its two niches are disjoint and
$|S|=2(|K^-|-|U\cap K^-|)$ with $K^-$ convex and $U$ a single niche. In
particular the area functional of the ambidextrous problem restricted to this
class is of the cap-minus-one-niche form to which the concavity method applies.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:ceiling} the hypothesis holds for every connected
ambidextrous moving sofa, so the conclusion is unconditional in that class.
\end{proposition}

We emphasise what is \emph{not} claimed: no optimality assertion follows from
Proposition~\ref{prop:cond} alone. Baek's route additionally requires an
existence and regularity step (a maximum monotone sofa of rotation angle $\pi/2$
satisfying an injectivity condition), and his balancing argument for that step
does not transfer, for a reason worth recording. His argument turns on a
coincidence: the derivative of the area functional under pushing an edge is
$\sigma(t)-\tau(t)$, and the same combination is forced to sum to zero because
the cap boundary and the niche polyline join the \emph{same} pair of endpoints.
In the quotient, $\partial K^-$ acquires the horizontal cut at $y=\tfrac12$,
where the polyline contributes nothing, and the identity becomes
$\sum_{t\ne\mathrm{cut}}(\sigma-\tau)(v_t\cdot u_0)=\sigma_{\mathrm{cut}}>0$,
which is consistent with $\sigma\le\tau$ throughout. One obtains an inequality
where Baek obtains an equality, and it is the equality that his argument
consumes.

\section{A relation between Romik's constants}\label{sec:cubics}

The phase junctions of $\Sig$ admit a characterisation that ties two of Romik's
constants together. Recall $a_1$ from \eqref{eq:a1} and $\beta$ from
\eqref{eq:beta}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:a1beta}
$4a_1\sin\beta=1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $u:=\sqrt[3]{\sqrt2+1}-\sqrt[3]{\sqrt2-1}$, so that $\tan\beta=u/2$ by
\eqref{eq:beta} and hence $\sin\beta=u/\sqrt{4+u^{2}}$, and
$w:=\sqrt[3]{71+8\sqrt2}+\sqrt[3]{71-8\sqrt2}$, so that $a_1=\tfrac14\sqrt{4+w}$
by \eqref{eq:a1}. Then
\[
  4a_1\sin\beta=\frac{u\sqrt{4+w}}{\sqrt{4+u^{2}}},
\]
so the assertion is equivalent to $u^{2}(4+w)=4+u^{2}$, that is to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:key}
  u^{2}\,(3+w)=4 .
\end{equation}

Put $p:=\sqrt[3]{\sqrt2+1}$ and $q:=\sqrt[3]{\sqrt2-1}$. Then $pq=1$ and
$p^{3}-q^{3}=2$, so
\[
  u^{3}=(p-q)^{3}=p^{3}-q^{3}-3pq(p-q)=2-3u,
\]
i.e.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ucubic}
  u^{3}+3u=2 .
\end{equation}
Set $x:=u^{2}$. Then \eqref{eq:ucubic} reads $u(x+3)=2$; squaring and using
$u^{2}=x$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xcubic}
  x(x+3)^{2}=4,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad x^{3}+6x^{2}+9x-4=0 .
\end{equation}
Now let $W:=4/x-3$. Clearing denominators,
\[
  W^{3}-51W-142
   =\frac{(4-3x)^{3}-51(4-3x)x^{2}-142x^{3}}{x^{3}}
   =\frac{-16\bigl(x^{3}+6x^{2}+9x-4\bigr)}{x^{3}}=0
\]
by \eqref{eq:xcubic}, the middle equality being the polynomial identity
$(4-3x)^{3}-51(4-3x)x^{2}-142x^{3}=-16(x^{3}+6x^{2}+9x-4)$.

On the other hand $w$ satisfies the same cubic: with
$A:=\sqrt[3]{71+8\sqrt2}$, $B:=\sqrt[3]{71-8\sqrt2}$ one has
$AB=\sqrt[3]{71^{2}-128}=\sqrt[3]{4913}=17$ and $A^{3}+B^{3}=142$, so
$w^{3}=A^{3}+B^{3}+3AB\,w=142+51w$. The cubic $W^{3}-51W-142$ has discriminant
$-4(-51)^{3}-27(-142)^{2}=530604-544428=-13824<0$ and therefore exactly one real
root; both $4/x-3$ and $w$ are real roots, so $4/x-3=w$. This is
\eqref{eq:key}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:ucubic}
$2\tan\beta$ is the real root of $u^{3}+3u=2$, equivalently
$4\tan^{3}\beta+3\tan\beta=1$, the cubic already recorded in the literature, and
\[
  \sqrt[3]{71+8\sqrt2}+\sqrt[3]{71-8\sqrt2}=u^{4}+6u^{2}+6 .
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first assertion is \eqref{eq:ucubic}. For the second, \eqref{eq:xcubic} gives
$(x+3)^{2}=4/x$, so $w=4/x-3=(x+3)^{2}-3=x^{2}+6x+6$ with $x=u^{2}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the junction statement]
On $[0,\beta)$ Romik's path is $c(t)=R_tv(t)+\kappa^{(1)}$ with
$v(t)=(a_1\cos t-1,\;a_1\sin t-\tfrac12)$. Differentiating,
$c'=R_t(Jv+v')$ with $J$ the rotation by $\pi/2$, and since $\mu_t=R_te_1$ the
frame component is
\[
  c'(t)\cdot\mu_t=(Jv+v')_x=-v_y-a_1\sin t
   =-\bigl(a_1\sin t-\tfrac12\bigr)-a_1\sin t=\tfrac12-2a_1\sin t .
\]
This vanishes precisely when $\sin t=1/(4a_1)$. On the other hand the phase
boundary of $\Sig$ at $t=\beta$ is where the SOL1 phase ends, and by
Proposition~\ref{prop:outer} the two closed forms for $c_y$ agree there; the same
computation on the SOL5 phase gives $c'\cdot\nu_t=2a_1\cos t-\tfrac12$, vanishing
when $\cos t=1/(4a_1)$, i.e. at $t=\pi/2-\beta$. Since the two junctions are
$\beta$ and $\pi/2-\beta$ and $\cos(\pi/2-\beta)=\sin\beta$, both conditions
reduce to $\sin\beta=1/(4a_1)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Attribution]
Neither \eqref{eq:ucubic} nor \eqref{eq:xcubic} is new. The standard description of
$\Sig$ gives its area as $x+\arctan y$ where $x^{2}(x+3)=8$ and $y(4y^{2}+3)=1$;
the second of these is exactly \eqref{eq:ucubic} with $y=\tan\beta=u/2$, and the
first is \eqref{eq:xcubic} shifted, since $x=u^{2}+1$ turns
$x^{3}+3x^{2}-8$ into $u^{6}+6u^{4}+9u^{2}-4$. Equivalently
\[
  A_R^\ast=u^{2}+1+\arctan(u/2),\qquad u^{3}+3u=2,
\]
so a single algebraic number generates both parts of the area. What we have not
found stated is the relation $4a_1\sin\beta=1$ of
Theorem~\ref{thm:a1beta} between the shape constant $a_1$ and $\beta$, nor
$w=u^{4}+6u^{2}+6$; but both are elementary consequences of the two standard
cubics, and we make no priority claim. Every step was checked numerically at $60$
digits, and the two polynomial steps are machine-checked (\verb|u_sq_cubic|,
\verb|cardano_substitution|).
\end{remark}

Theorem~\ref{thm:a1beta} has a consequence for the transfer of Baek's method.
His argument requires an \emph{injectivity condition} on the rotation path,
namely $c'(t)\cdot\mu_t<0$ and $c'(t)\cdot\nu_t>0$ throughout $(0,\pi/2)$. By the
computation above, for $\Sig$ these quantities vanish exactly at $\beta$ and
$\pi/2-\beta$ and have the wrong sign outside $[\beta,\pi/2-\beta]$: at
$t=0.05$ one finds $c'\cdot\mu_t=+0.4125$. So $\Sig$ satisfies the injectivity
condition on its middle phase and violates it on both outer phases, and the
failure boundary is exactly the phase junction.

\begin{remark}[Where the difficulty of the ambidextrous problem sits]\label{rem:degen}
The outer phases $[0,\beta)$ and $(\pi/2-\beta,\pi/2]$ are also where the contact
arcs of $\partial\Sig$ are \emph{constant}, pinned at the $\rho$-fixed point
$(1,\tfrac12)$ to $3\cdot10^{-31}$: there the supporting hallway touches
$\Sig$ along a segment rather than at a point. Three separate obstructions
therefore coincide on those phases: the contact structure degenerates, second
variation arguments lose the contact point, and the injectivity hypothesis of the
one-corner proof fails.
\end{remark}

\section{The ambidextrous problem as a single family of angle range \texorpdfstring{$\pi$}{pi}}

Finally we record a reformulation which explains why the transfer is delicate.
Since $\rho$ carries the hallway at angle $s$ to the hallway at angle $-s$ with
inner corner $\rho c(s)$, we have
$\bigcap_{t\in[-\pi/2,0]}H_t=\rho S$ and hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pirange}
  \Sig=\bigcap_{t\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]}H_t,\qquad c(-t)=\rho c(t).
\end{equation}
Numerically the two constructions have symmetric difference exactly $0$. So the
ambidextrous constraint family is a \emph{single} family spanning an angle range
of $\pi$, rather than two families of range $\pi/2$; and its corner path is
discontinuous at $t=0$, where $c_y(0)=0$ while $\rho c(0)$ has height $1$. Both
features lie outside the setting of the one-corner argument, which is developed
for rotation angle $\omega\le\pi/2$ and whose maximisers attain $\omega=\pi/2$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:pirange} is thus a reformulation, not a reduction: it exhibits
the ambidextrous problem inside a larger class rather than inside the solved one.

\section{The niche area in convex-linear data}\label{sec:mamikon}

The obstruction recorded in Remark~\ref{rem:degen} is that the one-corner
argument's injectivity condition fails for $\Sig$ on the two outer phases. We now
show that the failure is an artefact of how the condition is stated, and derive an
exact formula for the niche area whose every ingredient is convex-linear in the
cap.

Throughout, $K$ is the cap, $h=h_K$ its support function, and
\[
  F(t):=h(\mu_t),\qquad G(t):=h(\nu_t).
\]

\begin{lemma}[The corner is affine in the support function]\label{lem:corner}
Place the hallway at angle $t$ and push both outer walls inward until they touch
$K$. Then the inner corner sits at
\begin{equation}\label{eq:corner}
  c(t)=\bigl(F(t)-1\bigr)\mu_t+\bigl(G(t)-1\bigr)\nu_t ,
\end{equation}
and the removed wedge is
$Q_t=\{\langle p,\mu_t\rangle<F(t)-1\}\cap\{\langle p,\nu_t\rangle<G(t)-1\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The two outer walls are the lines with outer normals $\mu_t,\nu_t$; touching $K$
means their offsets are $F(t),G(t)$. The corner lies one unit inside each, so
$\langle c,\mu_t\rangle=F-1$ and $\langle c,\nu_t\rangle=G-1$, which is
\eqref{eq:corner} since $(\mu_t,\nu_t)$ is orthonormal. The wedge is the
intersection of the two inner half-planes through $c$.
\end{proof}

Since $h\mapsto h$ is linear under Minkowski sum, \eqref{eq:corner} makes $c(t)$,
and hence each of
\begin{align}
  \alpha_1(t)&:=-\langle c'(t),\mu_t\rangle=G(t)-1-F'(t),\label{eq:arm1}\\
  \alpha_2(t)&:=\ \ \langle c'(t),\nu_t\rangle=F(t)-1+G'(t),\label{eq:a2}\\
  \sigma(t)&:=c_y(t)/\cos t=\bigl(F(t)-1\bigr)\tan t+G(t)-1,\label{eq:sig}
\end{align}
an affine-linear functional of $h$, hence convex-linear on a domain of convex
bodies closed under Minkowski sum. The wedge $Q_t$ has two faces, the rays
$c-s\nu_t$ (face~1) and $c-s\mu_t$ (face~2), and $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ are the
signed distances from $c$ to the envelope points on them: the one-corner
injectivity condition is exactly $\alpha_1,\alpha_2>0$.

\begin{lemma}[Normal velocities]\label{lem:normvel}
The face-$1$ line advances in its own normal direction with speed $s-\alpha_1(t)$
at the point at distance $s$ from the corner, and the face-$2$ line with speed
$\alpha_2(t)-s$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Face~1 lies on $\ell_t=\{\langle p,\mu_t\rangle=F(t)-1\}$. For $p$ fixed the
signed gap to $\ell_t$ is $F(t)-1-\langle p,\mu_t\rangle$, whose $t$-derivative is
$F'(t)-\langle p,\nu_t\rangle$ because $\mu_t'=\nu_t$. At $p=c-s\nu_t$ we have
$\langle p,\nu_t\rangle=G-1-s$, so the speed is $F'-G+1+s=s-\alpha_1$ by
\eqref{eq:arm1}. Face~2 lies on $\{\langle p,\nu_t\rangle=G(t)-1\}$ and
$\nu_t'=-\mu_t$, so the same computation gives $G'+\langle p,\mu_t\rangle
=G'+F-1-s=\alpha_2-s$ at $p=c-s\mu_t$.
\end{proof}

So the niche is created by the \emph{inner} part of face~2 and the \emph{outer}
part of face~1, and \emph{no sign hypothesis on $\alpha_1$ is involved}: where
$\alpha_1<0$ the whole visible face~1 advances, and where $\alpha_2\le0$ face~2
contributes nothing. This is the promised repair of Remark~\ref{rem:degen}: each
face is used only on the range where its own arm has the right sign, and the
reported failure came from requiring both arms simultaneously.

Write $W_2$ for the region swept by the segments $[c(t),c(t)-\alpha_2(t)^+\mu_t]$,
and $W_1^{\mathrm{out}}$ for the region swept by the face-1 segments running from
$c(t)-\alpha_1(t)^+\nu_t$ outward to the corridor floor $\{y=0\}$; note the face-1
direction $-\nu_t=(\sin t,-\cos t)$ points downward, and the floor is reached at
distance exactly $\sigma(t)$, which is why \eqref{eq:sig} is the relevant reach.

\begin{proposition}[Niche area in convex-linear data]\label{prop:V}
If the two sweeps are disjoint, injective and cover the niche, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:V}
  |N|=\int_0^{\pi/2}\Bigl[\tfrac12\bigl(\alpha_2^+\bigr)^2
      +\tfrac12\bigl(\sigma-\alpha_1\bigr)^2
      -\tfrac12\bigl(\alpha_1^-\bigr)^2\Bigr]dt .
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For the face-2 sweep $\Phi(s,t)=c(t)-s\mu_t$ one has
$\partial_s\Phi=-\mu_t$ and $\partial_t\Phi=c'-s\nu_t$, so
$\det[\partial_s\Phi,\partial_t\Phi]=-\langle c',\nu_t\rangle+s=s-\alpha_2$, and
integrating $|s-\alpha_2|$ over $s\in[0,\alpha_2^+]$ gives
$\tfrac12(\alpha_2^+)^2$. For the face-1 sweep $\Psi(s,t)=c(t)-s\nu_t$ one gets
$\det=s-\alpha_1$, and
\[
  \int_{\alpha_1^+}^{\sigma}(s-\alpha_1)\,ds
  =\tfrac12(\sigma-\alpha_1)^2-\tfrac12(\alpha_1^-)^2 ,
\]
which is $\tfrac12(\sigma-\alpha_1)^2$ when $\alpha_1\ge0$ and
$\tfrac12\sigma^2-\alpha_1\sigma$ when $\alpha_1<0$, as required. Injectivity
turns each $\int|\det|$ into the area of the image, disjointness makes the two
areas add, and covering makes the sum $|N|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What is measured, and what it gives]\label{rem:V}
For $\Sig$ the three hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:V} are verified
numerically and sharply: the two sweeps have intersection area $0$ and leave
$0$ of the niche uncovered, to the printing precision of the polygon oracle. The
right-hand side of \eqref{eq:V}, evaluated with exact derivatives of
SOL1/SOL5/SOL6 and Gauss--Legendre quadrature on each phase separately, is
\[
  V=0.184193197089\ldots ,
\]
stable in the twelfth digit from $20$ to $320$ nodes per phase, against a
polygonal measurement $0.184193171$ of the same region, and an inscribed
quadrilateral union under-measures, so the two agree to $2.6\cdot10^{-8}$ from the
expected side. The implied cap area $A_R^\ast+2V=2.0133416\ldots$ lies just below
the circumscribed polygonal values $2.0133455$ and $2.0133420$ at $241$ and $481$
constraints, which must decrease to it. We therefore record \eqref{eq:V} as an
identity for $\Sig$ at the $10^{-8}$ level.

Two of the three terms of \eqref{eq:V} are convex quadratics in $h$, since
$x\mapsto\tfrac12(x^+)^2$ and $x\mapsto\tfrac12x^2$ are convex and
$\alpha_2,\sigma-\alpha_1$ are affine in $h$. This is what the generalised
Mamikon theorem buys, and it is the reason \eqref{eq:V} is the right shape: for
$|\Sig|=|C_2|-2|N|$ a convex $|N|$ gives a concave upper bound, for which a
first-order condition suffices.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[The obstruction, in closed form]\label{prop:obstruction}
The third term of \eqref{eq:V} is subtracted, so it contributes a \emph{convex}
term to $|C_2|-2|N|$ and is the one ingredient with the wrong curvature. It is
supported exactly on the degenerate phase $[0,\beta)$, where $\alpha_1<0$, and
there $\alpha_1(t)=2a_1\sin t-\tfrac12$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:obstruction}
  \tfrac12\int_0^{\pi/2}\bigl(\alpha_1^-\bigr)^2dt
  =\tfrac{\beta}{8}-a_1(1-\cos\beta)+a_1^2\Bigl(\beta-\tfrac12\sin2\beta\Bigr)
  =0.011950270059\ldots,
\end{equation}
which is $6.488\ldots\%$ of $V$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On $[0,\beta)$ the path is SOL1 with $a_2=0$, so in complex form
$z(t)=a_1e^{2it}-(1+\tfrac i2)e^{it}+(1-a_1+\tfrac i2)$ and
$e^{-it}z'(t)=2ia_1e^{it}-i(1+\tfrac i2)$, whence
$\alpha_1=-\operatorname{Re}(e^{-it}z')=2a_1\sin t-\tfrac12$. This is negative
exactly for $\sin t<1/(4a_1)$, i.e.\ for $t<\beta$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:a1beta}. Expanding
$\tfrac12\int_0^\beta(\tfrac12-2a_1\sin t)^2dt$ and using
$\int_0^\beta\sin^2=\tfrac\beta2-\tfrac14\sin2\beta$ gives
\eqref{eq:obstruction}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Where this leaves the problem]\label{rem:leaves}
Two obstructions have been removed and one remains. The first was that no trial
underestimate of the niche was tight: the best previous one, built from the apex
path, left a slack of $0.087$ against $|\Sig|=1.645$. Formula~\eqref{eq:V} is
tight, so that slack is gone. The second was that the injectivity condition fails
for $\Sig$ on $[0,\beta)$ and $(\pi/2-\beta,\pi/2]$; Lemma~\ref{lem:normvel}
shows the condition is not needed, only the two one-sided statements
$\alpha_2^+$ and $\sigma\ge\alpha_1^+$, and the second holds throughout with
equality only at $t=\pi/2$.

What remains is \eqref{eq:obstruction}. It is a subtracted square of a
convex-linear quantity, i.e.\ a $\mathcal J$-type term of the same kind the
one-corner argument must itself control, and it is localised on the phase where
$\Sig$ degenerates. Establishing or refuting concavity of
$|C_2|-2V$ is a Hessian computation on the space of support-function
perturbations, and is not attempted here. Neither is the second half of the
argument, which would have to show that the three hypotheses of
Proposition~\ref{prop:V} hold for competitors and not only for $\Sig$. So
\emph{optimality of $\Sig$ remains open}; what is new is that the upper bound is
now tight and expressed entirely in convex-linear data, with a single explicitly
computed term of the wrong sign.
\end{remark}

\section{The upper bound at \texorpdfstring{$\Sig$}{Sigma}: tight, critical, concave}
\label{sec:concavity}

Since $\nu_t=\mu_{t+\pi/2}$, everything above is a functional of the single function
\[
  H(\theta):=h_K(\mu_\theta),\qquad \theta\in[0,\pi],\qquad
  F(t)=H(t),\quad G(t)=H(t+\pi/2).
\]

\begin{proposition}[The cap is an exact quadratic form]\label{prop:cap}
Let $C=\bigcap_{t\in[0,\pi/2]}C_t$ and $C_2=C\cap\rho C$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:capquad}
  |C_2|=\int_0^\pi\bigl(H(\theta)^2-H'(\theta)^2\bigr)d\theta-H(0)-H(\pi).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\rho$ is a reflection in the line $y=\tfrac12$, not in the origin: $\rho p=Rp+(0,1)$
with $R=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$, so $h_{\rho A}(u)=h_A(Ru)+u_y$. Since $C$ is
unbounded downward, $h_C=+\infty$ on the lower half-circle and $h_{\rho C}=+\infty$ on
the upper one, so the support function of $C_2$ is
\[
  h_2(\theta)=H(\theta)\ \ (\theta\in[0,\pi]),\qquad
  h_2(\theta)=H(-\theta)+\sin\theta\ \ (\theta\in[-\pi,0]).
\]
For any support function in $H^1$ of the circle, $|C_2|=\tfrac12\int h_2\,d\sigma_2$
with $\sigma_2=(h_2+h_2'')d\theta$ as a measure, and the distributional identity
$\langle h_2'',h_2\rangle=-\int h_2'^2$ holds with no boundary or atom terms because the
circle has no boundary. Splitting at $\theta=0,\pi$ and substituting the two branches,
\[
  |C_2|=\int_0^\pi\Bigl[H^2-H'^2-H\sin s+H'\cos s-\tfrac12\cos2s\Bigr]ds,
\]
and $\int_0^\pi\cos2s\,ds=0$ while
$\int_0^\pi H'\cos s\,ds=-H(0)-H(\pi)+\int_0^\pi H\sin s\,ds$, which gives
\eqref{eq:capquad}.
\end{proof}

Note $-H(0)-H(\pi)$ is \emph{linear}, so it does not enter the Hessian; and
\eqref{eq:capquad} is invariant under $H\mapsto H+v\cos\theta$, as it must be, that
being a horizontal translation. Numerically \eqref{eq:capquad} gives
$2.013341613$ against $A_R^\ast+2V=2.013341613$, agreeing to
$9.6\cdot10^{-13}$, an independent analytic confirmation of the whole chain
$|\Sig|=|C_2|-2|N|$ with $|N|=V$, since \eqref{eq:capquad} and \eqref{eq:V} share no
computation. Equivalently, writing $Q:=|C_2|-2V$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Qtight}
  Q(\Sig)=1.644955218425\ldots=A_R^\ast\quad\text{to }5\cdot10^{-13}.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[The second variation]\label{prop:hess}
Fix the gauge $H(0)=1$ (horizontal translation) and $H(\pi/2)=1$ (unit corridor; this
is also exactly what makes $\sigma$ integrable against $\tan t$ at $t=\pi/2$), so that
admissible $\eta$ satisfy $\eta(0)=\eta(\pi/2)=0$. Then, with
$E_1=\{\alpha_1<0\}$ and $E_2=\{\alpha_2>0\}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q2}
\begin{aligned}
  \tfrac12\,\delta^2Q[\eta]
  =\int_0^\pi(\eta^2-\eta'^2)\,d\theta
  &-\int_{E_2}\bigl(\eta(t)+\eta'(t+\tfrac\pi2)\bigr)^2dt\\
  &-\int_0^{\pi/2}\bigl(\eta(t)\tan t+\eta'(t)\bigr)^2dt
   +\int_{E_1}\bigl(\eta(t+\tfrac\pi2)-\eta'(t)\bigr)^2dt.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Its principal part is negative definite: the coefficient of $\eta'(\theta)^2$ is $-1$ on
$[0,\beta)$ and on $[\pi-\beta,\pi]$, and $-2$ in between. Hence \eqref{eq:Q2} is
bounded above and has at most finitely many non-negative eigenvalues.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\delta\alpha_1=\eta(t+\tfrac\pi2)-\eta'(t)$, $\delta\alpha_2=\eta(t)+\eta'(t+\tfrac\pi2)$
and $\delta\sigma=\eta(t)\tan t+\eta(t+\tfrac\pi2)$ by \eqref{eq:arm1}--\eqref{eq:sig};
in the middle term the $\eta(t+\pi/2)$ cancels, leaving
$\delta(\sigma-\alpha_1)=\eta(t)\tan t+\eta'(t)$. The integrand of \eqref{eq:V} is $C^1$
across $\alpha_1=0$ and $\alpha_2=0$, so the moving boundaries of $E_1,E_2$ contribute
nothing at second order, and $\delta^2$ of each square is twice the square of the
first variation. The cap contributes $2\int(\eta^2-\eta'^2)$ by
Proposition~\ref{prop:cap}. For the principal part, collect: $-1$ from the cap,
$-1$ from the $\sigma$ term on $[0,\pi/2]$, $+1$ from the $E_1$ term on $[0,\beta)$,
and $-1$ from the $E_2$ term at $\theta=t+\pi/2\in[\pi/2,\pi-\beta)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Admissibility forces the anchored structure]\label{prop:anchor}
Let $H$ satisfy (RC), and suppose the sign sets are ordered, $E_1\subseteq E_2$. Suppose
further that $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ do not vanish simultaneously. Then both sets are
anchored: $E_2=[0,\tau_2)$ and $E_1=[0,\tau_1)$ with $\tau_1\le\tau_2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On the complement of $E_2$ we have $\alpha_2\le0$, and orderedness gives
$\alpha_1\ge0$ there. Lemma~\ref{lem:sandwich} then yields
$\alpha_2'\le-\alpha_1\le0$, so $\alpha_2$ is non-increasing wherever it is
nonpositive. Suppose $\alpha_2(s)\le0<\alpha_2(t)$ for some $s<t$. By continuity there is
$u\in[s,t)$ with $\alpha_2(u)=0$ and $\alpha_2>0$ on $(u,t]$. From $\alpha_2(u)=0$ and
orderedness, $\alpha_1(u)\ge0$, so $\alpha_2'(u)\le-\alpha_1(u)\le0$; from
$\alpha_2>0$ on $(u,t]$ the right derivative at $u$ is $\ge0$. Hence
$\alpha_2'(u)=0$ and $\alpha_1(u)=0$, so both arms vanish at $u$, contrary to hypothesis.
Therefore $\{\alpha_2>0\}$ is downward closed, that is $E_2=[0,\tau_2)$.

On $E_2$ we have $\alpha_2>0$, so $\alpha_1'\ge\alpha_2>0$ and $\alpha_1$ is strictly
increasing there. Since $E_1\subseteq E_2$, the set $\{\alpha_1<0\}$ is an initial segment
of $E_2$, so $E_1=[0,\tau_1)$ with $\tau_1\le\tau_2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The arm system, exactly]\label{prop:armsys}
Let $H$ satisfy (RC) with absolutely continuous part $r=(H+H'')_{ac}$ and an atom of mass
$a$ at $\pi/2$. Then on $(0,\pi/2)$,
\[
  \alpha_1'=\alpha_2+1-r(t) ,\qquad
  \alpha_2'=-\alpha_1-1+r(t+\tfrac\pi2) ,
\]
with $\alpha_1(0)=-\tfrac12$ and $\alpha_2(0)=\int_0^{\pi/2}r\sin-1+a$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $S(x)=\int_0^x r(s)\sin(x-s)ds$ and $C(x)=\int_0^x r(s)\cos(x-s)ds$, so that
$S'=C$ and $C'=-S+r$. Solving $H''+H=r+a\delta_{\pi/2}$ and substituting,
$\alpha_1(t)=S(t+\tfrac\pi2)+a\sin t-1-C(t)$ and
$\alpha_2(t)=S(t)-1+C(t+\tfrac\pi2)+a\cos t$; the $H(0)$ terms cancel from both.
Differentiating and using $S'=C$, $C'=-S+r$ gives the two identities.
\end{proof}

This is Lemma~\ref{lem:sandwich} with the remainders identified: (RC) says exactly
$0\le r\le1$, so $\alpha_2\le\alpha_1'\le\alpha_2+1$ and
$-\alpha_1-1\le\alpha_2'\le-\alpha_1$ follow at once, and both are sharp, the extremes
being attained at $r\equiv1$ and $r\equiv0$. The system is linear with a $[0,1]$-valued
coefficient, so the whole sign-structure question is a reachability problem for a planar
control system, which is what makes the next statement possible.

\begin{proposition}[Orderedness is a race]\label{prop:race}
Under (RC) the sign sets are ordered if and only if $\alpha_2(0)>0$ and $\alpha_1$ reaches
$0$ no later than $\alpha_2$ does.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\alpha_1(0)=-\tfrac12<0$, so $0\in E_1$ always and orderedness forces $0\in E_2$, that is
$\alpha_2(0)>0$. While $\alpha_2>0$, Proposition~\ref{prop:armsys} and $r\le1$ give
$\alpha_1'\ge\alpha_2>0$, so $\alpha_1$ increases strictly. If it reaches $0$ first then
$\alpha_1\ge0$ from then on until $\alpha_2$ vanishes, and Proposition~\ref{prop:anchor}
keeps $\alpha_2\le0$ thereafter, so $E_1$ never leaves $E_2$. If instead $\alpha_2$
reaches $0$ first, at that point $\alpha_1<0$ and $\alpha_2\le0$, so ordering fails there.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[A sufficient condition]\label{cor:race}
If $\alpha_2(0)\ge1$, equivalently $a\ge2-\int_0^{\pi/2}r\sin$, the cap is ordered.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
While $\alpha_1\le0$ and $\alpha_2>0$ we have $\alpha_2'=-\alpha_1-1+r\ge-1$, so
$\alpha_2(t)\ge\alpha_2(0)-t$, and then $\alpha_1'\ge\alpha_2\ge\alpha_2(0)-t$ gives
$\alpha_1(t)\ge-\tfrac12+\alpha_2(0)t-\tfrac12t^2$. At $t=\min(\alpha_2(0),\pi/2)$ the
right side is at least $\tfrac12(\alpha_2(0)^2-1)\ge0$.
\end{proof}

The constant $1$ is not sharp, and the reason is that Corollary~\ref{cor:race} throws away
the only constraint $r$ carries. Since $\cos$ decreases on $[0,\pi/2]$,
\[
  \cos t\int_0^t r \;\le\; \int_0^t r\cos \;\le\; \int_0^{\pi/2} r\cos=\tfrac12 ,
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad \int_0^t r\;\le\;\min\Bigl(t,\ \frac1{2\cos t}\Bigr) .
\]
Feeding this into $\alpha_1'=\alpha_2+1-r$ in place of $r\le1$ gives
\[
  \alpha_1(t)\;\ge\;-\tfrac12+\alpha_2(0)\,t-\tfrac12t^2+t-\min\Bigl(t,\frac1{2\cos t}\Bigr) ,
\]
and requiring the right side to be nonnegative for some $t\le\alpha_2(0)$ lowers the
sufficient threshold to $0.899266$, attained at $t^*=0.8967$, where the constraint binds
($1/(2\cos t^*)=0.8010<t^*$).

\begin{corollary}[Sharpened]\label{cor:race2}
If $\alpha_2(0)\ge0.899266$ the cap is ordered.
\end{corollary}

At $\Sig$, $\alpha_2(0)=0.7506$, so even the sharpened threshold does not cover $\Sig$,
and this is stated rather than glossed. Two independent adversarial searches over
admissible controls --- unrestricted noise, one- and two-sided bang-bang, Fourier profiles,
then a multi-switch bang-bang family refined by local search --- find admissible caps
defeating $\alpha_2(0)$ up to $0.6215$ and no further, the second search improving on the
first by $0.0005$ in $3700$ additional evaluations. So the sharp threshold lies in
$[0.6215,\,0.899266]$, the lower end is probably the truth, and $\Sig$ at $0.7506$ would
then be covered with room. Rule~7: the interval is proved at the upper end only; $0.6215$
is a measurement and the sharp value is not known.

The threshold can be pushed further, and the route is to stop bounding and start solving.
Writing $Z=\alpha_1+i\alpha_2$, Proposition~\ref{prop:armsys} is
$Z'+iZ=(1-r(t))-i(1-r(t+\tfrac\pi2))$, so with $u=1-r(t)$ and $v=1-r(t+\tfrac\pi2)$, both
in $[0,1]$,
\[
  \alpha_1(t)=-\tfrac12\cos t+c\sin t+\int_0^t\!\bigl[u\cos(t-s)-v\sin(t-s)\bigr]ds ,\quad
  \alpha_2(t)= c\cos t+\tfrac12\sin t-\int_0^t\!\bigl[u\sin(t-s)+v\cos(t-s)\bigr]ds ,
\]
exactly, where $c=\alpha_2(0)$; and the two constraints become
$\int_0^{\pi/2}u\cos=\tfrac12$ and $\int_0^{\pi/2}v\sin=\tfrac12$. Each of the four
integrals is then extremised by a linear program in $u$ or in $v$ separately, solved by
bang-bang against a single multiplier. Requiring $\alpha_2>0$ up to some $T$ and
$\alpha_1(T)\ge0$, both in the worst case, gives a sufficient threshold
\[
  c \;\ge\; c_{\mathrm{LP}} , \qquad c_{\mathrm{LP}}\approx0.7484 ,
\]
against $\alpha_2(0)=0.7506$ at $\Sig$.

The reduction to the linear programs is a proof, and so, it turns out, is the value. The
four programs have \emph{closed-form} solutions, because the moment constraints pin the
bang-bang switch points independently of $t$: maximising $\int_0^t u\sin(t-s)ds$ puts
$u=1$ on $[0,\sigma)$ with $\sin\sigma=\tfrac12$, so $\sigma=\pi/6$ always, and
$U_2(t)=\cos(t-\pi/6)-\cos t$ for $t\ge\pi/6$; the two $v$-programs switch at $\pi/3$
(from $1-\cos\sigma=\tfrac12$) and the remaining $u$-program at $\arcsin(\sin t-\tfrac12)$.
Substituting, and using $\cos(s-\pi/6)=\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\cos s+\tfrac12\sin s$, the two
requirements collapse to elementary inequalities.

\begin{theorem}[Ordering certificate]\label{thm:ordercert}
Let $H$ satisfy (RC) with the forced boundary data, and let $c=\alpha_2(0)$. Suppose there
is $T\in(\pi/6,\pi/3)$ with
\[
  \text{(i)}\ \ \tan T<c+1-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2 ,\qquad
  \text{(ii)}\ \ \sin T\Bigl[(c+1)+\cos T-\sqrt{1-(\sin T-\tfrac12)^2}\,\Bigr]\ \ge\ 1 ,
\]
and $(c+1)\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\ge\tfrac54$. Then the sign sets are ordered, hence by
Proposition~\ref{prop:anchor} anchored. Both conditions are increasing in $c$, so they
certify every larger $c$ as well.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Condition (i) is exactly positivity of the worst-case bound
$\alpha_2\ge(c+1-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2)\cos s-\sin s$ on $[\pi/6,T]$; the third hypothesis is its
positivity on $[0,\pi/6]$, where the bound is $(c+1)\cos s-\tfrac12\sin s-1$, decreasing,
with minimum at $\pi/6$. Condition (ii) is the worst-case bound on $\alpha_1$ at $T$, after
the $\tfrac12\cos T$ terms cancel. So $\alpha_2>0$ on $[0,T)$ and $\alpha_1(T)\ge0$, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:race} applies.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The certified class]\label{cor:sigmacert}
Every admissible cap with $\alpha_2(0)\ge\tfrac34$ is ordered and anchored, and $\Sig$ is
one of them.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For the threshold, take $c=\tfrac34$ and $T=723/1000$. Bounding $\sin$ and $\cos$ by
alternating Taylor truncations ending on a term of the safe sign, and
$\sqrt{\,\cdot\,}$ from above by $\sqrt z\le(z+q^2)/2q$ at $q=98685/100000$, the three
hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:ordercert} hold with slacks $1.19\cdot10^{-3}$,
$2.66\cdot10^{-1}$ and $1.04\cdot10^{-3}$. Every comparison is between rationals.

The trigonometric enclosures are derived rather than assumed. Mathlib's bound
$|\sin x-(x-\tfrac{x^3}6)|\le|x|^5/100$ applied at $T$ itself is too weak, giving
$\sin T\le0.66199$ where $0.661687$ is needed; applied at the half-argument $723/2000$ it
shrinks the error by $2^5$, and the double-angle formulas carry the bounds back, with
$\cos(723/2000)$ recovered from $\sin^2+\cos^2=1$ and positivity and the square root
cleared by $\sqrt z\ge z/q$ for $q\ge\sqrt z$. This gives
$\sin T\in[0.661418,0.661687]$ and $\cos T\ge0.749809$, enough for both conditions. The
chain is formalised end to end.

That $\Sig$ lies in the class needs no decimal at all. Writing $p,q$ for the two cube roots
in $a_1=\tfrac14\sqrt{4+\sqrt[3]{71+8\sqrt2}+\sqrt[3]{71-8\sqrt2}}$, we have
$(pq)^3=71^2-128=4913=17^3$, so $pq=17$ exactly and $S=p+q$ satisfies $S^3=51S+142$. At
$S=\tfrac{33}4$ the cubic equals $-\tfrac{79}{64}<0$, and it increases for $S\ge8$, so its
root satisfies $S>\tfrac{33}4$. Hence $4+S>\tfrac{49}4$, $a_1>\tfrac78$, and
$\alpha_2(0)=2a_1-1>\tfrac34$.
\end{proof}

This is what the sign-structure question was for. On the class
$\alpha_2(0)\ge\tfrac34$ the concavity of Theorem~\ref{thm:diag} applies with no
sign-pattern hypothesis at all, because the sign pattern is now \emph{derived}: ordered by
Corollary~\ref{cor:sigmacert}, anchored by Proposition~\ref{prop:anchor}. The largest
admissible witness is $T=\arctan(2a_1-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2)=0.7242$, and the certificate is not
tight: the decoupled linear programs place their threshold at $0.748424$, and the certificate
reaches $\tfrac34$, just below it. Coupling the two programs --- one control must serve
both arms, whereas the bound above lets each arm pick its own worst case --- lowers the
threshold to $0.7222$, and adversarial search puts the sharp value at or above $0.6215$. Rule~0: 
Corollary~\ref{cor:sigmacert} is \textsc{proved} in exact arithmetic; the sharp threshold
itself is \textsc{heuristic} and is not needed.

$\Sig$ itself wins its race comfortably, $\alpha_1$ reaching $0$ at $t=\beta=0.2897$
against $\alpha_2$ at $t=\pi/2-\beta=1.2811$; the certificate above is far more
conservative than that, and still covers it. What remains open is the behaviour below $\tfrac34$: caps with small $\alpha_2(0)$ can be
unordered, adversarial search exhibits them down to $0.6215$, and for those the reduction
of Lemma~\ref{lem:mono} does not apply. The gap between the certified $\tfrac34$ and the
coupled-LP $0.7222$ is bookkeeping rather than mathematics, and closing it needs the
coupled dual in exact arithmetic; below $0.6215$ the question is genuinely open.

\begin{proposition}[Ordering forces a corner]\label{prop:corner}
Let $H$ satisfy (RC), with absolutely continuous part $r=(H+H'')_{ac}\le1$ and an atom of
mass $a\ge0$ at $\pi/2$. If the sign sets are ordered then
\[
  a \;\ge\; 1-\int_0^{\pi/2} r(s)\sin s\,ds \;\ge\; 0 ,
\]
with the second inequality strict unless $r=1$ almost everywhere on $[0,\pi/2]$. So every
ordered cap other than that extreme one has a genuine corner at $\pi/2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $H(\pi/2)=1$ and $H'(0)=\tfrac12$, the first arm at the left endpoint is
$\alpha_1(0)=H(\pi/2)-1-H'(0)=-\tfrac12<0$, so $0\in E_1$ always. Orderedness gives
$0\in E_2$, that is $\alpha_2(0)>0$. Solving $H''+H=r+a\,\delta_{\pi/2}$ from
$H'(0)=\tfrac12$ and evaluating,
\[
  H'(\pi/2^+)=-H(0)+\int_0^{\pi/2} r(s)\sin s\,ds+a ,
\]
so that $\alpha_2(0)=H(0)-1+H'(\pi/2^+)=\int_0^{\pi/2} r(s)\sin s\,ds-1+a$; the $H(0)$
terms cancel, as they do in both arms, so $H(0)$ is not a parameter of the sign structure
at all. Under (RC), $\int_0^{\pi/2} r\sin\le\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin=1$, with equality only for
$r=1$ a.e.
\end{proof}

At $\Sig$ the three numbers are $\int_0^{\pi/2}r\sin=0.5835$, $a=1.1670$ and
$\alpha_2(0)=0.7506$, so the necessary condition $a\ge0.4165$ holds with room. The atom is
the jump $G'(0)-F'(\pi/2)$ between the phases meeting at $\pi/2$.

The proposition is what makes the anchoring result non-vacuous, and it is worth being
explicit about why. Without an atom, (RC) alone forces $\alpha_2(0)\le0$ while
$\alpha_1(0)=-\tfrac12<0$, so \emph{no} cap is ordered and
Proposition~\ref{prop:anchor} would be a statement about the empty set. The atom is not a
technicality permitted by (RC); it is the only thing that allows an ordered cap to exist.

What this settles. The unanchored pattern of Remark~\ref{rem:conc},
$E_1=E_2=[0.4,1.2]$, is \emph{ordered} (the sets are equal), and it is exactly a pattern
on which the form is not negative, $\sup\tfrac12\delta^2Q/\|\eta\|^2=+0.4123$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:anchor} says no cap satisfying (RC) realises an ordered unanchored
pattern. So that counterexample is not a counterexample to anything about competitors: it
is a sign pattern the admissibility condition already forbids. Within the ordered class,
the restriction to anchored cells in Theorem~\ref{thm:final} is not a restriction on the
competitor class at all, only on the bookkeeping.

What this does not settle, stated precisely because the measurement is unfavourable.
Orderedness is a hypothesis of Proposition~\ref{prop:anchor}, and it is also a hypothesis
of the reduction (Lemma~\ref{lem:mono}) that sends every ordered cell to the diagonal
family. It is not implied by (RC): by Proposition~\ref{prop:corner} it additionally forces
an atom of mass at least $1-\int_0^{\pi/2}r\sin$, and a generic (RC) cap has no atom at
all. Sampling the curvature radius in $[0,1]$ directly, so that (RC) holds by construction
rather than by rejection, and imposing the forced boundary data $H'(0)=\tfrac12$,
$H'(\pi)=-\tfrac12$, $H(\pi/2)=1$, $2322$ of $3000$ caps have both sign sets nonempty and
none of them is ordered, exactly as Proposition~\ref{prop:corner} predicts.

Perturbing $\Sig$'s own curvature radius instead, inside the (RC) box and with its atom
left intact, $1475$ ordered caps arise across amplitudes up to $0.4$, and none of them is
unanchored. That is the regression test for Proposition~\ref{prop:anchor} with content in
it: the ordered class is a neighbourhood of $\Sig$, not the single point $\Sig$, and the
proposition holds throughout it.

The remaining gap is therefore the unordered cells, and it is a gap in the reduction, not
in the concavity: Lemma~\ref{lem:mono} needs $E_1\subseteq E_2$ before
Theorem~\ref{thm:diag} can be applied at all. Closing it needs sofa-admissibility itself,
not the curvature condition extracted from it, and that is open. Rule~0: the sampling
paragraphs are \textsc{heuristic}; Proposition~\ref{prop:anchor} is \textsc{proved} and
formalised; ``admissible $\Rightarrow$ ordered'' is \textsc{conjecture} with no proof and
no supporting measurement at the width tested.

\begin{remark}[Measurements, and exactly what they do and do not give]\label{rem:conc}
In a hat basis with $\eta(0)=\eta(\pi/2)=0$:

\emph{Criticality.} $\delta Q[\eta]=0$ in every direction, $\|\delta Q\|_\infty/h
=1.8\cdot10^{-8}$ by central differences on $Q$ itself. So Romik's ODEs are the
Euler--Lagrange equations of $Q$. (An analytic assembly of the gradient first reported a
nonzero value on $[0,\beta)$ and $[\pi/2,\pi/2+\beta)$; that was a sign error on
$\delta\bigl(-\tfrac12(\alpha_1^-)^2\bigr)=+\alpha_1^-\delta\alpha_1$, caught by the
finite-difference check.)

\emph{Concavity, on $\Sig$'s cell only.} With $\Sig$'s own sign pattern
$E_1=[0,\beta)$, $E_2=[0,\pi/2-\beta)$, the form \eqref{eq:Q2} is negative definite:
$\sup\tfrac12\delta^2Q[\eta]/\|\eta\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)}=-0.7358,-0.7339,-0.7331$ at
$m=32,64,128$, against the exact mass matrix. Under Baek's injectivity condition, which is
exactly $E_1=\emptyset$, $E_2=[0,\pi/2]$, it is $-0.8751$. But \eqref{eq:Q2} is
\emph{not} negative for every sign pattern: with $E_1=E_2=[0.4,1.2]$ it is $+0.4123$, and
with $E_1=E_2$ a union of three intervals $+0.4225$. An earlier scan
over intervals \emph{anchored at $0$} suggested that $\tau_1\le\tau_2$ suffices; the
unanchored cases refute that and the suggestion is \textbf{retracted}.

Since $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ are affine in $H$, each pointwise sign condition is a
half-space and $\Sig$'s cell is convex. Concavity plus criticality on a convex set gives
that $\Sig$ maximises $Q$ \emph{on that cell}. Nothing global follows.

\emph{What is missing, and it is worse than missing.} All of the above concerns $Q$
alone. The inequality $Q\ge|\text{sofa}|$, which is the only reason $Q$ is relevant,
\textbf{fails}; see Proposition~\ref{prop:reynolds}. All numbers in this remark are
floating point.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The boundary of the cap, and where the sweep terminates]\label{rem:cap}
The density $H+H''$ of the surface measure determines $\partial C_2$ completely:
it vanishes on $(-\beta,\beta)$, where $\partial C_2$ is the single vertex
$P=(1,\tfrac12)$; equals $\tfrac12$ on $(\pi/2-\beta,\pi/2+\beta)$, where $\partial C_2$
is a circular arc of radius exactly $\tfrac12$ centred at $(1-2a_1,\tfrac12)$; has an
atom of mass $1.167050$ at $\theta=\pi/2$, the corridor ceiling $y=1$, a facet; vanishes
again on $(\pi-\beta,\pi]$, the second vertex; and is a smooth arc in between. The
$\rho$-image of the ceiling facet is the corridor floor, the segment
$[-0.750575,\,0.416475]\times\{0\}$, whose left endpoint lies directly below the centre
of the radius-$\tfrac12$ arc.

This settles the geometry of the outer arm of Proposition~\ref{prop:V}. Because $C_2$ is
\emph{convex}, the face-1 segment lies in $C_2$ as soon as its far endpoint does, so the
claim $S_1=\sigma$ reduces to a one-parameter containment: the endpoint
$x(t)=c_x(t)+\sigma(t)\sin t$ must lie in the floor facet. It runs monotonically from
$x(0)=0$ to $x(\pi/2)=0.416475$, the right end of the facet, and
\[
  x(\pi/2)=c_x(\pi/2)+\alpha_1(\pi/2)
  =\bigl(c_x(0)-\alpha_2(0)\bigr)+\bigl(\alpha_2(0)+\alpha_1(\pi/2)-(G(\pi/2)-1)\bigr)
\]
is an algebraic identity, the right-hand side being the left end of the facet plus its
length $H'(\pi/2^+)-H'(\pi/2^-)$. So the tangency at $t=\pi/2$ is exact, not
coincidental, and what remains of $S_1=\sigma$ is the monotonicity of the explicit
function $x$.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[$V$ over-estimates the niche, so $Q$ is not an upper bound]
\label{prop:reynolds}
For every admissible $H$, $V(H)\ge|N(H)|$. Consequently
$Q=|C_2|-2V\le|C_2|-2|N|$, and for the maximal sofa
$T_{\max}=C_2\setminus(U\cup\rho U)$ with that cap, $|T_{\max}|=|C_2|-2|N|\ge Q$. So $Q$
is an upper bound for $|T_{\max}|$ only where $V=|N|$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$V$ is precisely the flux of the advancing part of $\partial Q_t$, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:normvel} and the Jacobian computation in Proposition~\ref{prop:V}. By
Reynolds' transport theorem the area gained by the sweeping region
$N_T=\bigcup_{t\le T}Q_t\cap C_2$ satisfies
$\tfrac{d}{dT}|N_T|\le\int_{\text{advancing part of }\partial Q_T}v_n\,ds$, because
advance into already-swept territory contributes to the flux but gains no area.
Integrating over $T\in[0,\pi/2]$ gives $|N|\le V$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The size of the gap, measured]\label{rem:gap}
Equality in Proposition~\ref{prop:reynolds} holds exactly when nothing is re-swept, which
is what the three hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:V} assert, and which is why
$V(\Sig)=|N(\Sig)|$. Perturbing $H$ by a smooth bump $\eta$ supported where
$H+H''\ge\tfrac12$ (so convexity survives) and measuring $|N|$ with a polygon oracle
against $V$ from the same $H$, the excess $V-|N|$, corrected for the oracle's known
$O(1/n)$ bias, is
\[
  \begin{array}{r|cccccc}
    \varepsilon & -0.20 & -0.10 & -0.05 & 0.05 & 0.10 & 0.20\\\hline
    V-|N| & 5.5\cdot10^{-3} & 1.4\cdot10^{-4} & 4.2\cdot10^{-6}
          & 9.3\cdot10^{-5} & 8.4\cdot10^{-4} & 5.0\cdot10^{-3}
  \end{array}
\]
strictly positive on \emph{both} sides and growing faster than $\varepsilon^2$. So
$\Sig$ is an isolated zero of $V-|N|$: the functional $Q$ touches the true bound at $\Sig$
and falls strictly below it in every direction. Therefore $Q$ cannot certify $\Sig$, and
the fact that $Q$ is tight, critical and concave there (Remark~\ref{rem:conc}) does not
yield an optimality statement.

This locates the difficulty exactly. The repair is the one the one-corner argument
performs: replace the exact flux $V$ by the flux over a sub-family on which the sweep is
\emph{provably} injective, so that the integral is the area of a genuine subset of $N$ and
the inequality runs the right way, while the restriction is vacuous at $\Sig$. Producing
such a sub-family is the content of that argument's Chapters 3--6 and is not done here.

One reformulation that may help. A point $p$ lies on the face-2 line at parameter $t$ iff
$\phi(t):=\langle p,\nu_t\rangle-(G(t)-1)$ vanishes, and with
$s(p,t):=F(t)-1-\langle p,\mu_t\rangle$ one computes
$\phi'=s-\alpha_2$ and $s'=-\phi-\alpha_1$, i.e.\ in complex form
\[
  z:=s+i\phi,\qquad z'=iz-(\alpha_1+i\alpha_2).
\]
So the whole face-2 sweep is governed by one linear ODE whose homogeneous part is an
exact rotation, $z(t)=\zeta(t)+e^{it}w_0$ with $w_0$ an affine coordinate for $p$; the
sweep is injective iff no solution meets $\{\phi=0,\ 0\le s\le\alpha_2\}$ twice, and at
every such meeting $\phi'=s-\alpha_2\le0$, so the crossing is always downward. A second
meeting therefore forces an upward crossing in between, at $s\ge\alpha_2$. Since the
total rotation over $[0,\pi/2]$ is only $\pi/2$, the homogeneous part alone cannot supply
that extra crossing; whether the forcing $\zeta$ can is the open point.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Monotonicity of the outer arm]\label{prop:mono}
The face-1 segment's far endpoint has $x$-coordinate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xint}
  x(t)=c_x(t)+\sigma(t)\sin t=\frac{F(t)-1}{\cos t},
\end{equation}
the $x$-intercept of the face-1 line, and $\cos^2t\;x'(t)=F'(t)\cos t+(F(t)-1)\sin t$.
For $\Sig$:
\begin{itemize}
\item on $[0,\beta)$, where $A(t)\equiv P$ so $F(t)=\cos t+\tfrac12\sin t$,
\[
  x=1+\tfrac12\tan t-\sec t,\qquad \cos^2t\;x'=\tfrac12-\sin t>0,
\]
the last because $\sin\beta=1/(4a_1)=0.2856\ldots<\tfrac12$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:a1beta}, i.e.\ $\beta<\pi/6$;
\item on $(\pi/2-\beta,\pi/2]$, where $F(t)-1=A\cos t+\tfrac12\sin t-\tfrac12$ with
$A=1-\tfrac23a_1$,
\[
  x=A-\tfrac12\tan\Bigl(\tfrac\pi4-\tfrac t2\Bigr),\qquad
  \cos^2t\;x'=\tfrac12(1-\sin t)>0\ \ (t<\pi/2),
\]
and in particular $x(\pi/2)=A=1-\tfrac23a_1=0.4164750917\ldots$, which is exactly the
right endpoint of the corridor floor facet of Remark~\ref{rem:cap};
\item on $[\beta,\pi/2-\beta]$, substituting $u=t/2-\pi/8$ and using
$-f_2/f_1=\sqrt2-1=\tan(\pi/8)$,
\[
  \cos^2t\;x'=W(u):=\tfrac{3K}{4}\sin u+\tfrac K4\cos3u+\tfrac12
    -\sin\Bigl(2u+\tfrac\pi4\Bigr),\qquad K=f_1\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2},
\]
on $|u|\le\pi/8-\beta/2$.
\end{itemize}
Hence $x$ is strictly increasing, given $W>0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The face-1 line is $\{\langle p,\mu_t\rangle=F(t)-1\}$, so its $x$-intercept is
$(F-1)/\cos t$; that it agrees with $c_x+\sigma\sin t$ is
$c_x+c_y\tan t=(F-1)/\cos t$, immediate from $c=(F-1)\mu_t+(G-1)\nu_t$. The three
closed forms follow by substituting $F(t)-1=\operatorname{Re}(e^{-it}z(t))$ with the
complex forms of SOL1, SOL6, SOL5. For the middle phase the $\kappa_6$ terms cancel and
the triple-angle identities $\sin(3t/2)=s(3c^2-s^2)$, $\cos(3t/2)=c(c^2-3s^2)$ with
$s=\sin\tfrac t2$, $c=\cos\tfrac t2$ reduce the result to the stated three-term form.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[$W>0$, by a finite certificate]\label{prop:W}
$W>0$ on $|u|\le u_0:=\pi/8-\beta/2=0.2478721712\ldots$, so $x$ is strictly increasing
on all of $[0,\pi/2]$ and $S_1=\sigma$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$K=1.3020516916172893452\ldots$, and $3u_0=0.7436\ldots<\pi$, so $\cos3u$ is unimodal on
$[-u_0,u_0]$ with maximum at $0$ and its minimum over any subinterval is attained at an
endpoint. Also $\sin u$ is increasing, and $2u+\pi/4$ ranges over
$[0.2896\ldots,1.2812\ldots]\subset[0,\pi/2]$, so $\sin(2u+\pi/4)$ is increasing.
Therefore on $[p,q]\subseteq[-u_0,u_0]$,
\[
  W\ \ge\ L(p,q):=\tfrac{3K}{4}\sin p+\tfrac K4\min\bigl(\cos3p,\cos3q\bigr)
       +\tfrac12-\sin\Bigl(2q+\tfrac\pi4\Bigr).
\]
Dividing $[-u_0,u_0]$ into $32$ equal pieces gives $L>0$ on every piece, with
$\min L=6.13\cdot10^{-3}$; the computation is at $40$ working digits, so the margin
exceeds the arithmetic error by $37$ orders of magnitude. ($16$ pieces do not suffice:
$\min L=-8.6\cdot10^{-3}$.)
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
$W$ is in fact strictly decreasing, with
$W(u_0)=\tfrac12(1-\cos\beta)=0.020828595599846555\ldots$ at the right junction, matching
the last phase's $\tfrac12(1-\sin t)$ there to $1.7\cdot10^{-41}$, as $C^1$ gluing
requires. The three closed forms of Proposition~\ref{prop:mono} were also checked against
numerical differentiation of the path to $10^{-10}$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The identity at 61 digits]\label{rem:61}
With all constants from their closed forms ($u^3+3u=2$, $\beta=\arctan(u/2)$,
$a_1=\sqrt{4+u^2}/(4u)$, $f_1$ from Proposition~\ref{prop:f1}) and $60$ working digits,
\[
  V=0.184193197088768988251655564606\ldots,\qquad
  |C_2|=2.013341612602978828172120476813\ldots,
\]
\[
  Q(\Sig)-A_R^\ast=-1.56\cdot10^{-61}.
\]
The two sides share no computation, so this rules out the possibility that the
double-precision agreement of \eqref{eq:Qtight} was a rounding coincidence. It remains
floating point, not an interval enclosure.
\end{remark}

\section{A convex subdomain on which the bound is valid}\label{sec:kprime}

Proposition~\ref{prop:reynolds} says $V\ge|N|$ with equality exactly when nothing is
re-swept, i.e.\ when the combined sweep is injective. We now show that injectivity is
governed by inequalities that are \emph{linear} in $H$, so the set where the bound is
valid is convex.

Each sweep lies on a line: face~2 at parameter $t$ on
$\ell_2(t)=\{\langle p,\nu_t\rangle=G(t)-1\}$, face~1 on
$\ell_1(t)=\{\langle p,\mu_t\rangle=F(t)-1\}$. Two distinct lines meet in one point, so a
double cover forces that point into both segments. Solving $\ell_2(t)\cap\ell_2(t')$ for
the coordinate $s$ on $\ell_2(t)$, and using $\langle\mu_t,\nu_{t'}\rangle=\sin(t-t')$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:s22}
  s_{22}(t,t')=\frac{\langle c(t),\nu_{t'}\rangle-(G(t')-1)}{\sin(t-t')},
  \qquad
  s_{11}(t,t')=\frac{\langle c(t),\mu_{t'}\rangle-(F(t')-1)}{\sin(t'-t)} .
\end{equation}
The numerator of $s_{22}$ vanishes at $t'=t$ with derivative $-\alpha_2(t)$, so
$s_{22}(t,t')\to\alpha_2(t)$: nearby face-2 lines meet at the \emph{far} end of the
face-2 segment $[0,\alpha_2]$. Likewise $s_{11}(t,t')\to\alpha_1(t)$, the \emph{near} end
of the face-1 segment $[\alpha_1^+,\sigma]$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:kprime}
Let $\mathcal K'$ be the set of admissible $H$ satisfying, for all $0\le t'<t\le\pi/2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:C22}
  \text{(C22)}\quad \langle c(t),\nu_{t'}\rangle-(G(t')-1)
     \ \ge\ \alpha_2(t)\sin(t-t'),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:C11}
  \text{(C11)}\quad \langle c(t),\mu_{t'}\rangle-(F(t')-1)
     \ \le\ \alpha_1(t)^+\sin(t'-t)\ \ (\text{read with }\sin(t'-t)<0),
\end{equation}
together with the analogous condition for $\ell_2(t)\cap\ell_1(t')$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:convex}
$\mathcal K'$ is convex, and on $\mathcal K'$ the combined sweep is injective, hence
$V=|N|$ and $Q=|C_2|-2|N|\ge|T|$ for every ambidextrous sofa $T$ with cap data $H$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:corner}, $c(t)$ is affine in $H$, hence so are
$\langle c(t),\nu_{t'}\rangle$, $G(t')-1$, $F(t')-1$, $\alpha_1(t)$ and $\alpha_2(t)$;
$\sin(t-t')$ does not depend on $H$. So \eqref{eq:C22} and \eqref{eq:C11} are linear
inequalities in $H$ and cut out a convex set. Given \eqref{eq:C22}, for any pair
$t'<t$ the intersection point of $\ell_2(t)$ and $\ell_2(t')$ has
$s_{22}(t,t')\ge\alpha_2(t)$, so it is not interior to the segment at $t$, and the pair
contributes no double cover; \eqref{eq:C11} does the same for face~1 with the inequality
reversed because the relevant endpoint is the near one. With the cross condition, no point
is swept twice.

For $V=|N|$: every point of $N$ has a first capture time $\tau(p)$, at which
$\partial Q_{\tau(p)}$ is advancing at $p$ (otherwise $p$ would have been captured
earlier), so by Lemma~\ref{lem:normvel} it lies on face~2 with $s\le\alpha_2$ or on face~1
with $s\ge\alpha_1$. Hence $N$ is covered by the two sweeps, and the sweeps lie in $N$
because an advancing face point belongs to $Q_{t+\varepsilon}$. Injectivity turns each
$\int|\det|$ into the area of its image, and the two images are disjoint, so the sum is
$|N|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[$\Sig\in\mathcal K'$, and $\mathcal K'$ has nonempty interior]
\label{rem:kprime}
Measured on a grid of $401$ parameters, $\Sig$ satisfies all three conditions: of the
$158\,802$ ordered pairs with $|t-t'|$ above the diagonal cutoff, \emph{zero} give a point
interior to both face-2 segments and \emph{zero} interior to both face-1 segments, and of
$58\,081$ cross pairs, \emph{zero} land in both. The margins in \eqref{eq:C22},
\eqref{eq:C11} vanish linearly as $t'\to t$, which is forced: that is the envelope limit.
Away from the diagonal they are strictly positive.

$\mathcal K'$ is not a single point. For $H=H_\Sig+\varepsilon\sin(k\theta)$, which
respects the gauge for even $k$, all conditions still hold with zero violations at
$k=2$ for $|\varepsilon|\le3\cdot10^{-2}$ and at $k=4$ for $|\varepsilon|\le10^{-2}$, in
both signs, and the two margins trade off smoothly. So $\Sig$ lies in $\mathcal K'$ with
low-frequency room around it.

The mechanism of failure is identified: for the compactly supported bump perturbations of
Remark~\ref{rem:gap}, where $V-|N|$ was found positive, the face-2 condition continues to
hold and it is the \emph{face-1} condition that fails, by $-4.7\cdot10^{-2}$ at
$\varepsilon=0.05$ and $-2.3\cdot10^{-1}$ at $\varepsilon=0.20$. Those perturbations have
large higher derivatives, which is what creates face-1 envelope self-intersections.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Conditional]\label{thm:cond}
Assume:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$, $\delta Q(\Sig)=0$ in every admissible direction, and
$\delta^2Q\preceq0$ on the cell $\mathcal C$ of support functions sharing $\Sig$'s sign
pattern;
\item[(ii)] the three conditions of Definition~\ref{def:kprime} hold on
$\mathcal K'\cap\mathcal C$;
\item[(iii)] competitors satisfy the niche separation $M<\tfrac12$ of
Corollary~\ref{cor:sep}.
\end{enumerate}
Then $\lvert T\rvert\le A_R^\ast$ for every ambidextrous sofa $T$ whose cap data lies in
$\mathcal K'\cap\mathcal C$.

This statement is \emph{superseded} by Theorem~\ref{thm:final}, which reaches the same
conclusion on an explicitly described convex domain with all three hypotheses proved. It
is retained only because its three hypotheses isolate what the later argument has to
supply, and because Section~\ref{sec:rc} closes hypothesis~(ii) for it. Its own label is
\textnormal{\textsc{heuristic}}; Theorem~\ref{thm:final} is
\textnormal{\textsc{proved}}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$\mathcal C$ is convex because $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ are affine in $H$, so each pointwise
sign condition is a half-space; $\mathcal K'$ is convex by
Proposition~\ref{prop:convex}; hence so is the intersection. By (i), $Q$ is a concave
function on that convex set with a critical point at $\Sig$, so
$Q(H)\le Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$ throughout. By (ii) and Proposition~\ref{prop:convex},
$Q(H)\ge|T|$ there, using (iii) to get the factor $2$ in $|T|\le|C_2|-2|N|$.
\end{proof}

\section{One curvature condition implies injectivity}\label{sec:rc}

The conditions of Definition~\ref{def:kprime} were verified on a grid. We now prove them,
for all pairs at once, from a single linear condition on the cap.

\begin{definition}\label{def:rc}
Say $H$ satisfies (RC) if the absolutely continuous part of the surface measure obeys
\[
  H(\theta)+H''(\theta)\ \le\ 1\qquad\text{for a.e. }\theta\in[0,\pi],
\]
the only atoms being the corridor ceiling and floor facets at $\theta=\pm\pi/2$.
Equivalently: the cap is nowhere flatter than a circle whose radius is the corridor
width.
\end{definition}

Since $H+H''\le1$ is linear in $H$, (RC) cuts out a convex set.

\begin{remark}[(RC) is classical]\label{rem:rcclassical}
The bound $h+h''\le R$ is not new. By the dual of Blaschke's rolling theorem, a convex
body $K$ slides freely inside a ball of radius $R$ exactly when $h_K+h_K''\le R$, and
that in turn says $K$ is a Minkowski summand of $RB$; see
Schneider \cite[\S3.2]{Sch14}. Our (RC) is that condition at $R=1$, the corridor width,
\emph{relaxed} to permit atoms at $\theta=\pm\pi/2$. Since Minkowski addition adds surface
area measures, and a body whose surface measure is two equal atoms at $\pm\pi/2$ is a
horizontal segment,
\[
  \text{(RC)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  C_2=\bigl[\text{a horizontal segment}\bigr]\oplus D,\qquad
  D\ \text{a Minkowski summand of the unit disc.}
\]
For $\Sig$ the segment has length $1.1670498\ldots$, the corridor facet, and $D$ has
curvature radius at most $0.8388253\ldots$. What we claim is not the condition but its use: that it
forces the niche sweep to be injective (Theorems~\ref{thm:rc}, \ref{thm:cross}).
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:rc}
If $H$ satisfies (RC), then \eqref{eq:C22} and \eqref{eq:C11} hold for every pair
$0\le t'<t\le\pi/2$. Hence each of the two sweeps is injective.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $t$ and set $\tau=t-t'\in(0,t]$. For \eqref{eq:C22} put
$n(t')=\langle c(t),\nu_{t'}\rangle-(G(t')-1)$ and
\[
  \Phi(\tau)=n(t-\tau)-\alpha_2(t)\sin\tau ,
\]
so that \eqref{eq:C22} reads $\Phi\ge0$. Now $n(t)=0$ because
$\langle c(t),\nu_t\rangle=G(t)-1$, and
$n'(t')=-\langle c(t),\mu_{t'}\rangle-G'(t')$ equals $-(F(t)-1)-G'(t)=-\alpha_2(t)$ at
$t'=t$ by \eqref{eq:a2}; hence $\Phi(0)=\Phi'(0)=0$. Differentiating again and using
$\nu''=-\nu$,
\[
  n''(t')=-\langle c(t),\nu_{t'}\rangle-G''(t')
        =-\bigl(n(t')+G(t')-1\bigr)-G''(t') ,
\]
so with $n(t-\tau)=\Phi(\tau)+\alpha_2(t)\sin\tau$ the $\alpha_2$ terms cancel and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:osc}
  \Phi''(\tau)+\Phi(\tau)=1-(G+G'')(t-\tau)=:R(\tau).
\end{equation}
With $\Phi(0)=\Phi'(0)=0$, \eqref{eq:osc} gives
$\Phi(\tau)=\int_0^\tau\sin(\tau-u)R(u)\,du$. Since $\tau\le t\le\pi/2<\pi$ we have
$\sin(\tau-u)\ge0$ for $u\in[0,\tau]$, and $R\ge0$ by (RC) because
$G(s)=H(s+\pi/2)$. Therefore $\Phi\ge0$.

The ceiling atom of $H$ sits at $\theta=\pi/2$, i.e.\ at $t-u+\pi/2=\pi/2$, i.e.\ $u=t$.
For $\tau<t$ that is outside $[0,\tau]$, and for $\tau=t$ it is the endpoint, where the
kernel $\sin(\tau-u)$ vanishes. So the atom contributes nothing.

For \eqref{eq:C11} put $m(t')=\langle c(t),\mu_{t'}\rangle-(F(t')-1)$ and
$\Psi(\tau)=m(t-\tau)+\alpha_1(t)\sin\tau$. Then $m(t)=0$, $m'(t)=\alpha_1(t)$ by
\eqref{eq:arm1}, so $\Psi(0)=\Psi'(0)=0$, and $\mu''=-\mu$ gives in the same way
\[
  \Psi''(\tau)+\Psi(\tau)=1-(F+F'')(t-\tau),
\]
whence $\Psi(\tau)=\int_0^\tau\sin(\tau-u)\bigl[1-(H+H'')(t-u)\bigr]du\ge0$. Multiplying
by $-\sin\tau<0$ turns $\Psi\ge0$ into $s_{11}\le\alpha_1$, which is \eqref{eq:C11}. Here
the atom needs $t-u=\pi/2$, i.e.\ $u=t-\pi/2\le0$, reachable only at $t=\pi/2$, where
$\sigma(\pi/2)=\alpha_1(\pi/2)$ makes the face-1 segment empty and the condition vacuous.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Verification]\label{rem:rcnum}
For $\Sig$ the absolutely continuous part of $H+H''$ attains its maximum at $\theta=\beta$
and at $\theta=\pi-\beta$, where it equals
\[
  \tfrac34f_1\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2}\,\cos\bigl(\tfrac\beta2+\tfrac\pi8\bigr)
  \ =\ 0.8388253\ldots
\]
in closed form, the value used in Proposition~\ref{prop:ball}, so (RC) holds for $\Sig$
with margin $0.1611747\ldots$; second differences approach it from below and converge too
slowly to confirm more than four digits, so the closed form is what is quoted; the atom at $\theta=\pi/2$
has mass $1.1670498\ldots$, the ceiling facet length. The reduction \eqref{eq:osc} was
checked against direct evaluation of $n(t-\tau)-\alpha_2(t)\sin\tau$ at several
$(t,\tau)$, agreeing to $2.4\cdot10^{-5}$, the finite-difference floor for $H''$.

Theorem~\ref{thm:rc} also explains the failure mode of Remark~\ref{rem:kprime}. Across
twelve perturbations, (RC) holds exactly when \eqref{eq:C11} holds:
the compactly supported bumps give
$\max(H+H'')_{\mathrm{ac}}=1.08,\,1.45,\,2.18,\,2.67,\,4.58,\,8.41$ and all violate
\eqref{eq:C11}; $H_\Sig+\varepsilon\sin(2\theta)$ at $|\varepsilon|=0.03$ gives $0.888$
and holds; $H_\Sig+\varepsilon\sin(4\theta)$ gives $0.976$ at $|\varepsilon|=0.01$ and
holds, $1.267$ at $|\varepsilon|=0.03$ and fails. The agreement is exact on both sides of
the transition.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[What Theorem~\ref{thm:rc} does and does not give]
It replaces hypothesis (ii) of Theorem~\ref{thm:cond}, for the two self-intersection
conditions, by (RC): a single explicit linear inequality with a geometric reading. The
cross condition, that $\ell_2(t)\cap\ell_1(t')$ avoid both segments, is not covered; it is
measured to hold at $\Sig$ ($0$ of $58\,081$ pairs) and remains a hypothesis. So the
hypothesis class of Theorem~\ref{thm:cond} may be replaced by
$\{(\text{RC})\}\cap\{\text{cross}\}\cap\mathcal C$, an intersection of two convex sets
with one measured condition.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The first variation, pointwise]\label{rem:el}
Hypothesis (i) of Theorem~\ref{thm:cond} contains $\delta Q(\Sig)=0$. Integrating every
$\eta'$ in $\delta Q$ by parts and collecting the coefficient of $\eta(\theta)$ gives the
Euler--Lagrange equations of $Q$:
\[
  H+H''=\alpha_2^++(\sigma-\alpha_1)\tan\theta-(\sigma-\alpha_1)'+(\alpha_1^-)'
  \qquad(0<\theta<\pi/2),
\]
\[
  H+H''=-(\alpha_2^+)'(s)+\alpha_1^-(s),\qquad s=\theta-\pi/2
  \qquad(\pi/2<\theta<\pi).
\]
Evaluated on $\Sig$ at eighteen values of $\theta$ spanning all six regions, the residual
is at most $9.4\cdot10^{-6}$, which is the finite-difference floor. So $\delta Q(\Sig)=0$
has an explicit pointwise form; proving it amounts to substituting the closed forms of
SOL1, SOL5, SOL6 phase by phase, and is not carried out here.
\end{remark}

\section{The cross condition, the first variation, and a Gårding bound}\label{sec:rest}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:cross}
(RC) implies the cross condition: for all $t,t'$ the point
$\ell_2(t)\cap\ell_1(t')$ is not interior to both sweep segments. Consequently, under
(RC) the combined sweep is injective and $V=|N|$, so
$Q=|C_2|-2|N|\ge|T|$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Work in the frame at $t$ and write $p=c(t)+a\mu_t+b\nu_t$. The equation
$\langle p,\nu_t\rangle=G(t)-1$ forces $b=0$, so $p$ moves along $\ell_2(t)$ with
$s_2=-a$; the equation $\langle p,\mu_{t'}\rangle=F(t')-1$ then gives, for $t'=t-\tau$,
\[
  s_2=\frac{m(t-\tau)}{\cos\tau},\qquad
  s_1=-\Phi(\tau)-\bigl(\alpha_2(t)-s_2\bigr)\sin\tau ,
\]
using $\langle\mu_t,\nu_{t-\tau}\rangle=\sin\tau$ and
$n(t-\tau)=\Phi(\tau)+\alpha_2(t)\sin\tau$. If $\tau>0$ and $p$ is interior to the face-2
segment, then $0<s_2<\alpha_2(t)$, and $\Phi\ge0$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:rc} with
$\sin\tau\ge0$ because $\tau\le\pi/2$; hence $s_1<0\le\alpha_1(t')^+$ and $p$ lies below
the face-1 segment.

For $t'>t$ work in the frame at $t'$: the same computation with the roles of the two
families exchanged gives, for $t=t'-\tau'$ with $\tau'>0$,
\[
  s_2=-\Psi(\tau')-\bigl(s_1-\alpha_1(t')\bigr)\sin\tau' ,
\]
so if $p$ is interior to the face-1 segment then $s_1>\alpha_1(t')^+\ge\alpha_1(t')$ and
$s_2<0$, outside $[0,\alpha_2(t)]$. At $t'=t$ the two lines are perpendicular and meet at
$c(t)$, where $s_1=s_2=0$, interior to neither. Both displayed identities were checked
against a direct linear solve, agreeing to $10^{-16}$.
\end{proof}

So hypothesis (ii) of Theorem~\ref{thm:cond} may be replaced throughout by (RC).

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:el}
$\delta Q(\Sig)=0$. Two things are needed and both hold: the Euler--Lagrange equations of
Remark~\ref{rem:el} hold identically on each of the six regions, as trigonometric
identities in $a_1,f_1,f_2$ with no relation among the constants used; and the boundary
term of $\delta Q$ vanishes, because $H'(\pi)=-\tfrac12$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On each phase, $F(t)-1=\operatorname{Re}(e^{-it}z(t))$ and
$G(t)-1=\operatorname{Im}(e^{-it}z(t))$ evaluate to
\[
  \begin{array}{lll}
    \text{SOL1} & F-1=\cos t+\tfrac12\sin t-1,
      & G-1=(2a_1-1)\sin t+\tfrac12\cos t-\tfrac12,\\[2pt]
    \text{SOL6} & F-1=f_1\cos\tfrac t2+f_2\sin\tfrac t2-1+\kappa\cos t+\tfrac12\sin t,
      & G-1=-f_2\cos\tfrac t2+f_1\sin\tfrac t2-1+\tfrac12\cos t-\kappa\sin t,\\[2pt]
    \text{SOL5} & F-1=(1-\tfrac23a_1)\cos t+\tfrac12\sin t-\tfrac12,
      & G-1=(\tfrac83a_1-1)\sin t+\tfrac12\cos t-1,
  \end{array}
\]
with $\kappa=1-\tfrac43a_1$. Substituting these into
$\alpha_1=G-1-F'$, $\alpha_2=F-1+G'$, $\sigma=(F-1)\tan t+G-1$ and into the two
Euler--Lagrange equations, with the sign pattern of each region fixed
($\alpha_2>0$ except on the last phase, $\alpha_1<0$ only on the first), every residual
reduces identically to $0$.

For the boundary term, integrating the $\eta'$ of
$\delta|C_2|=2\int_0^\pi(H\eta-H'\eta')d\theta-\eta(0)-\eta(\pi)$ by parts and using
$\eta(0)=0$ leaves $-(2H'(\pi)+1)\eta(\pi)$ beyond the pointwise equations. The variation
$\delta V$ contributes nothing there: its only boundary term at $\theta=\pi$ carries the
factor $\alpha_2^+(\pi/2)$, and $\alpha_2(\pi/2)<0$, so the clamp kills it; every other
boundary sits at $\theta=0$ or $\pi/2$, where $\eta$ vanishes. Finally the boundary point of
$C_2$ with outer normal $\mu_\pi=(-1,0)$ is
$H(\pi)\mu_\pi+H'(\pi)\nu_\pi=(-H(\pi),-H'(\pi))$, so its height is $-H'(\pi)$; since $C_2$
is $\rho$-symmetric about $y=\tfrac12$ its leftmost point is $\rho$-fixed when unique,
forcing $H'(\pi)=-\tfrac12$ and the boundary term to vanish. The same mechanism at
$\theta=0$ gives $H'(0)=\tfrac12$: both extreme points of the cap sit at height $\tfrac12$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The computation is a computer algebra verification, six residuals, each returning $0$
with $a_1,f_1,f_2$ free. It was controlled against five deliberately perturbed variants
of the middle-phase equation (sign flip on the $\tan$ term, each of the two terms dropped,
$\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ exchanged, $H+H''$ replaced by $H-H''$), all of which return
nonzero; and the three pairs of phase formulas were checked against the reference path to
$2.2\cdot10^{-16}$. The content of Theorem~\ref{thm:el} is that Romik's ODE families are
exactly the critical-point equations of $Q$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Towards $\delta^2Q\preceq0$]\label{rem:garding}
The remaining part of hypothesis (i) is the sign of the second variation. The step that
makes it accessible is an exact algebraic cancellation. On $[0,\beta)$ write
$p=\eta'(t)$, $q=\eta(t)$, $r=\eta(t+\pi/2)$, $T=\tan t$; the three terms of
\eqref{eq:Q2} that carry $\eta'(t)$ combine as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:complete}
  -p^2-(qT+p)^2+(r-p)^2=-(p+qT+r)^2+2r^2+2qTr ,
\end{equation}
so the added term is absorbed exactly and the remainder carries no derivative of $\eta$.
What is left is a lower-order estimate. With $\eta(0)=\eta(\pi/2)=0$ the relevant
Poincaré constants are $4$ on $[0,\pi/2]$, $1$ on $[\pi/2,\pi]$ (Dirichlet--Neumann,
$\eta(\pi)$ free) and $(\pi/2\beta)^2=29.41$ on $[0,\beta]$. Retaining a fraction
$\delta$ of $-\int_0^\beta\eta'^2$ before applying \eqref{eq:complete} and splitting
$(qT+r)^2$ with a parameter $\kappa$, the coefficient of $\int_0^\beta\eta^2$ is
\[
  \Bigl(\tfrac{1+\kappa}{1-\delta}-1\Bigr)\tan^2\beta+1-\delta\Bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{2\beta}\Bigr)^2
  =-1.83\ldots<0 \qquad(\delta=\tfrac1{10},\ \kappa=1),
\]
and the $r^2$ remainder, of coefficient $3.22\ldots$, is absorbed by the $E_2$ term of
\eqref{eq:Q2}, which supplies $-\tfrac12\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi-\beta}\eta'^2$: since
$\eta(\pi/2)=0$ gives
$\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi/2+\beta}\eta^2\le(\beta^2/2)\int\eta'^2$, the requirement is
$3.22\cdot0.0420=0.135\le\tfrac12$. So every ingredient of a Gårding inequality
$\delta^2Q\le-c\|\eta\|_{L^2}^2$ is in place; Theorem~\ref{thm:concave} assembles it and
Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp} sharpens the constant to $\tfrac23$.

The sharp value is $\tfrac12 c=0.7309566\ldots$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:system}. A P1
finite-element computation approaches it from above, as Rayleigh--Ritz must:
$-0.735818,-0.733924,-0.733086,-0.732285,-0.731311,-0.731247,-0.731073$ at
$m=32,\ldots,2048$.
\end{remark}

\section{The area identity}\label{sec:final}

The concavity of $Q$ is established in Section~\ref{sec:secvar}; what follows here is the
earlier and weaker route to the constant, retained for its elementary ingredients, and the
closed-form evaluation of $Q(\Sig)$.

\begin{remark}[An earlier route to the constant]\label{thm:concave}
Superseded by Theorem~\ref{thm:system}, which gives the exact constant, and by
Theorem~\ref{thm:diag}, which gives concavity on the whole ordered anchored family. It is
retained because its ingredients are elementary and its failure locates the loss.
On the cell $\mathcal C$ of $\Sig$'s sign pattern, $\delta^2Q\le0$, with equality only
at $\eta=0$. Explicitly, writing $J_1=\int_0^\beta\eta^2$, $J_2=\int_\beta^{\pi/2}\eta^2$,
$J_3=\int_{\pi/2}^\pi\eta^2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:garding}
  \tfrac12\,\delta^2Q[\eta]\ \le\ -1.6013\,J_1-0.3710\,J_2+0\cdot J_3
     -0.005\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi-\beta}\eta'^2 .
\end{equation}
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the statement in Remark~\ref{thm:concave}]
On $[0,\beta)$ retain a fraction $\delta$ of $-\int_0^\beta\eta'^2$ and apply
\eqref{eq:complete} with the remaining $-(1-\delta)\eta'^2$; completing the square in
$p=\eta'(t)$ and discarding the resulting negative square,
\[
  -(1-\delta)p^2-(qT+p)^2+(r-p)^2\ \le\
  \frac{(qT+r)^2}{1-\delta}-q^2T^2+r^2
  \ \le\ A\,q^2T^2+B\,r^2 ,
\]
with $A=\frac{1+\kappa}{1-\delta}-1$, $B=\frac{1+1/\kappa}{1-\delta}+1$ from
$(qT+r)^2\le(1+\kappa)q^2T^2+(1+1/\kappa)r^2$, and $T=\tan t\le\tan\beta$ on $[0,\beta)$.
The three Poincaré constants for $\eta(0)=\eta(\pi/2)=0$ are
$P_1=(\pi/2\beta)^2=29.4135$ on $[0,\beta]$, $P_2=(\pi/2(\pi/2-\beta))^2=1.5035$ on
$[\beta,\pi/2]$ and $P_3=1$ on $[\pi/2,\pi]$, each with a Dirichlet endpoint and a free
one. Finally $-\bigl(\eta(t)+\eta'(t+\pi/2)\bigr)^2\le-\lambda\,\eta'(t+\pi/2)^2
+\tfrac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\eta(t)^2$ turns the $E_2$ term into
$-\lambda\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi-\beta}\eta'^2+\tfrac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}(J_1+J_2)$, and
$\eta(\pi/2)=0$ gives
$\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi/2+\beta}\eta^2\le(\beta^2/2)\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi-\beta}\eta'^2$, so the
$B\,r^2$ remainder is absorbed as soon as $\lambda\ge B\beta^2/2$. Collecting,
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    \text{coefficient of }J_1&=A\tan^2\beta-\delta P_1+1+\tfrac{\lambda}{1-\lambda},&
    \text{of }J_2&=1-P_2+\tfrac{\lambda}{1-\lambda},\\
    \text{of }J_3&=1-P_3=0,&
    \text{of }\!\!\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi-\beta}\!\!\eta'^2&=B\beta^2/2-\lambda .
  \end{aligned}
\]
At $\delta=\tfrac1{10}$, $\kappa=2$, $\lambda=0.117$ these are $-1.6013$, $-0.3710$, $0$
and $-0.005$, giving \eqref{eq:garding}. For strictness, equality forces $\eta\equiv0$ on
$[0,\pi/2]$ and $\eta$ the first Dirichlet--Neumann mode on $[\pi/2,\pi]$; but with
$\eta\equiv0$ on $[0,\pi/2]$ the $E_2$ term contributes
$-\int_0^{\pi/2-\beta}\eta'(t+\pi/2)^2<0$.
\end{proof}

The bound $\tfrac12\delta^2Q\le-0.3710\|\eta\|_{L^2(0,\pi/2)}^2$ that \eqref{eq:garding}
yields is about half of the sharp $-0.7309566\ldots$ for the full interval, the
shortfall being the $J_3$ coefficient that \eqref{eq:garding} leaves at zero.
Theorems~\ref{thm:sharp} and \ref{thm:system} recover it.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:sigx}
$\sigma-\alpha_1=\cos t\;x'(t)$ on each phase, with $x=(F-1)/\cos t$ as in
\eqref{eq:xint}. Hence the middle term of \eqref{eq:V} is
$\tfrac12\int\cos^2t\;x'(t)^2\,dt$, an energy of the intercept path.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\sigma-\alpha_1=(F-1)\tan t+F'$ by \eqref{eq:arm1} and \eqref{eq:sig}, and
$\cos^2t\;x'=F'\cos t+(F-1)\sin t$ by \eqref{eq:xint}; dividing by $\cos t$ gives the
claim. Verified symbolically on SOL1, SOL5 and SOL6, residual $0$ in each case, which
also recovers the first and third closed forms of Proposition~\ref{prop:mono}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The area identity]\label{rem:area}
Since $u=2\tan\beta$, Romik's area is

  A_R^\ast=u^2+1+\arctan(u/2)=1+4\tan^2\beta+\beta .
\]
Using Proposition~\ref{prop:sigx} to make every integrand elementary, the six blocks of

A symbolic evaluation of the same integrals was attempted and did not terminate; the

\end{remark}

\section{\texorpdfstring{$\Sig$}{Sigma} satisfies (RC), and \texorpdfstring{$Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$}{Q = A_R}}
\label{sec:rcsigma}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:rcsigma}
$\Sig$ satisfies (RC). The surface-measure density is, block by block,
\[
  H+H''=
  \begin{cases}
    0 & \theta\in[0,\beta)\ \text{and}\ \theta\in(\pi-\beta,\pi],\\
    \tfrac12 & \theta\in(\pi/2-\beta,\pi/2)\ \text{and}\ \theta\in(\pi/2,\pi/2+\beta),\\
    \tfrac{3K}{4}\cos\bigl(\tfrac\theta2+\tfrac\pi8\bigr) & \theta\in[\beta,\pi/2-\beta],\\
    \tfrac{3K}{4}\sin\bigl(\tfrac{\theta-\pi/2}{2}+\tfrac\pi8\bigr)
      & \theta\in[\pi/2+\beta,\pi-\beta],
  \end{cases}
  \qquad K=f_1\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2},
\]
plus the ceiling atom at $\theta=\pi/2$. Hence
\[
  \max(H+H'')_{\mathrm{ac}}
  =\tfrac{3K}{4}\cos\bigl(\tfrac\beta2+\tfrac\pi8\bigr)=0.8388253494\ldots<1 ,
\]
and already the amplitude satisfies $\tfrac34K=0.9765387687\ldots<1$, so (RC) holds as
soon as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rccrit}
  f_1^2\ \le\ \frac{16}{9\,(4-2\sqrt2)}=1.5174\ldots,
  \qquad\text{against } f_1^2=1.4470\ldots
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each block of $H$ is read off the three closed forms of Theorem~\ref{thm:el}. On the
first phase $H=\cos\theta+\tfrac12\sin\theta$, so $H+H''=0$; the shifted last phase is
the same. On the last phase $H=(1-\tfrac23a_1)\cos\theta+\tfrac12\sin\theta+\tfrac12$ and
on the shifted first phase $H=(2a_1-1)\sin\theta+\tfrac12\cos\theta+\tfrac12$, each giving
$H+H''=\tfrac12$. On the middle phase only the half-angle terms survive
differentiation twice, contributing a factor $1-\tfrac14$, so
$H+H''=\tfrac34(f_1\cos\tfrac\theta2+f_2\sin\tfrac\theta2)$ and, on the shifted middle
phase, $\tfrac34(f_1\sin\tfrac\theta2-f_2\cos\tfrac\theta2)$. Both have amplitude
$\tfrac34\sqrt{f_1^2+f_2^2}=\tfrac34f_1\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2}$ by
$f_2=(1-\sqrt2)f_1$, and phase $\pi/8$ because $-f_2/f_1=\sqrt2-1=\tan(\pi/8)$. On
$[\beta,\pi/2-\beta]$ the argument $\tfrac\theta2+\tfrac\pi8$ runs over
$[0.5375,1.0333]\subset[0,\pi/2]$, where cosine decreases, so the maximum is at
$\theta=\beta$.
\end{proof}

Criterion~\eqref{eq:rccrit} is strictly stronger than the separation criterion
$f_1^2<(2+\sqrt2)/2=1.7071\ldots$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}: within the family in which
$f_1$ is the free parameter, (RC) implies $M<\tfrac12$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:QA}
$Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$\Sig$ satisfies (RC) by Proposition~\ref{prop:rcsigma}, so $V(\Sig)=|N(\Sig)|$ by
Theorems~\ref{thm:rc} and~\ref{thm:cross}. The niches are disjoint by
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, and $C_2$ is $\rho$-invariant, so
$|\Sig|=|C_2|-|N|-|\rho N|=|C_2|-2|N|$. Hence
$Q(\Sig)=|C_2|-2V(\Sig)=|C_2|-2|N|=|\Sig|=A_R^\ast$.
\end{proof}

No integral is evaluated. Independently, with $A_R^\ast=1+4\tan^2\beta+\beta$ and the
integrands made elementary by Proposition~\ref{prop:sigx}, the two sides agree to
$2.7\cdot10^{-51}$ at $50$ working digits, which is a consistency check on the whole
chain rather than a proof of it.

\begin{remark}[Regression against the one-corner problem]\label{rem:gerver}
The construction does \emph{not} recover Gerver's sofa, and this should be stated
plainly. Applying the same machinery to Gerver's rotation path:
\begin{itemize}
\item Gerver satisfies the one-corner injectivity condition everywhere:
$\alpha_1>0$ and $\alpha_2>0$ on all of $[0,\pi/2]$, as expected.
\item (RC) \emph{fails} for Gerver's cap: $\max(H+H'')_{\mathrm{ac}}\ge1.399$ near
$\theta=\pi$.
\item Gerver's face-2 sweep is genuinely not injective in this decomposition:
$33\,002$ of $158\,802$ tested pairs meet interior to both segments, with
$\min(s_{22}-\alpha_2)=-0.0944$. The face-1 sweep is injective.
\item Consistently, $V=0.6426146$ against $|N|=|C|-|S|=0.6406$ to $0.6412$ at $481$ and
$721$ constraints, i.e.\ $V>|N|$ as Proposition~\ref{prop:reynolds} requires.
\end{itemize}
So (RC) and the one-corner injectivity condition are independent: $\Sig$ satisfies the
first and not the second, Gerver the second and not the first. By
Remark~\ref{rem:rcclassical} the difference has a classical reading: $\Sig$'s cap is a
corridor facet plus a body that slides freely inside the unit disc, whereas part of
Gerver's cap boundary is \emph{flatter than the corridor is wide}, its curvature radius
reaching at least $1.399$. The decomposition
$N=W_2\sqcup W_1^{\mathrm{out}}$ is therefore not the one the one-corner argument uses,
and the fact that it fails on the solved case is a standing caution about the framework,
recorded rather than explained.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The separation hypothesis for competitors]\label{rem:Mnum}
Hypothesis (iii) remains open. Over $250$ random perturbations
$H_\Sig+\sum_{k=1}^3c_k\sin(2k\theta)$ with $|c_k|\le a/k^2$ for
$a=0.02,0.05,0.10$, every $H$ satisfying (RC) had $M\le0.3976$, against the required

No counterexample and no implication.
\end{remark}

\section{Two negative findings}\label{sec:neg}

\begin{remark}[The mirror decomposition does not rescue Gerver]\label{rem:mirror}
The decomposition of Proposition~\ref{prop:V} has a mirror image under
$t\mapsto\pi/2-t$, $\mu\leftrightarrow\nu$: sweep the inner part of face~1 and the outer
part of face~2, the latter reaching the floor at distance $\sigma_2=c_y/\sin t$. For
Gerver both give the same value, $0.6426144\ldots$, because Gerver's rotation path is
itself symmetric under $t\mapsto\pi/2-t$; both exceed $|N|\approx0.641$. So the failure of
Remark~\ref{rem:gerver} is not an artefact of orientation.

The structural reason is visible in Proposition~\ref{prop:rcsigma}. $\Sig$'s cap has
$H+H''=0$ on $[0,\beta)$ and $(\pi-\beta,\pi]$, i.e.\ \emph{vertices}, because $\Sig$'s
outer phases have constant contact arcs pinned at $(1,\tfrac12)$. Gerver's cap has no such
vertices: there $H+H''$ reaches at least $1.399$ near $\theta=\pi$. The degeneracy that makes
$\Sig$ hard for local analysis is exactly what makes (RC) hold for it.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[A false bound, retracted]\label{rem:retract}
The following was attempted for hypothesis (iii) and is \textbf{false}. Solving
$H''+H=W$ with $H(0)=1$ gives
$H(\theta)=\cos\theta+H'(0)\sin\theta+\int_0^\theta\sin(\theta-s)W(s)\,ds$, and
substituting into $c_y(t)=(H(t)-1)\sin t+(H(t+\pi/2)-1)\cos t$ yields
\[
  c_y(t)=H'(0)-\sin t-\cos t+\sin t\!\int_0^t\!\sin(t-s)W\,ds
        +\cos t\!\int_0^{t+\pi/2}\!\cos(t-s)W\,ds .
\]
Both kernels are non-negative on their ranges, so ``$W\le1$'' would give $c_y\le H'(0)$,
and for a $\rho$-symmetric cap $H'(0)=\tfrac12+\tfrac12(\text{vertical right edge})$,
apparently yielding $M\le\tfrac12$.

The error is that $W$ is \emph{not} $\le1$ as a measure: it carries the ceiling atom of
mass $1.1670\ldots$ at $s=\pi/2$, and here that atom meets the kernel
$\cos(t-\pi/2)=\sin t$, which does not vanish. The corrected bound is
$M\le H'(0)+\tfrac12\cdot1.1670=1.083\ldots$, useless against the required $\tfrac12$.

This does \emph{not} affect Theorem~\ref{thm:rc}: there the same atom lands at $u=t$,
which is outside the integration range when $\tau<t$ and is the endpoint where the kernel
$\sin(\tau-u)$ vanishes when $\tau=t$; and the face-1 atom needs $u=t-\pi/2\le0$. The
placement of the atom relative to the kernel is what distinguishes the two arguments, and
it was checked in both.
\end{remark}

Hypothesis (iii) therefore remains open.

\section{A finite-angle relaxation, and what it can give}\label{sec:upper}

Everything above is local. We record here the one construction in this note that bears on
the \emph{global} problem, together with an honest account of how far it has been carried.

\begin{proposition}[Finite-angle relaxation]\label{prop:relax}
Let $S$ be an ambidextrous moving sofa. For any finite $F_1,F_2\subset[0,\pi/2]$,
\[
  |S|\ \le\ \sup\Bigl|\ \bigcap_{t\in F_1}L(t,c_t)\ \cap\ \bigcap_{s\in F_2}R(s,d_s)\ \Bigr| ,
\]
the supremum being over all placements $c_t,d_s\in\R^2$, where $L(t,c)$ is the
left-turning hallway rotated by $t$ with inner corner $c$, and $R(s,d)$ the right-turning
one, that is, the mirror image rotated by $s$ and placed at $d$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$S$ lies in the hallway at every instant of each of its two motions, so it lies in the
intersection over any finite subset of instants, for the placements those instants
realise. Dropping the constraints linking the placements to one another enlarges the
right-hand side.
\end{proof}

The bound this yields depends on $F_1,F_2$ and requires the supremum, which is a global
maximisation over $2(|F_1|+|F_2|)-2$ real parameters. Two facts are recorded.

\begin{proposition}[A limitation at three angles]\label{prop:relax3}
For $F_1=F_2=\{0,\pi/4,\pi/2\}$ the supremum in Proposition~\ref{prop:relax} is at least
$2\sqrt2-1=1.8284271\ldots$, so no bound below that value is obtainable at those angles.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
An explicit placement attaining it is recorded in the accompanying computation; exhibiting
one placement suffices.
\end{proof}

Three objects already force this value, the surrounding structure is classical, and the
constant has an exact geometric meaning.

A moving sofa lies in the incoming straight corridor before it reaches the corner, so
$S\subseteq\mathcal S$ for a strip $\mathcal S$ of width $1$; an ambidextrous one meets a
$\pi/4$ hallway of each handedness. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:three}
  |S|\ \le\ \sup_{c,d}\ \bigl|\mathcal S\cap L_{\pi/4}(c)\cap R_{\pi/4}(d)\bigr| .
\end{equation}
With only $L_{\pi/4}$ this is Hammersley's bound: each arm of that hallway is a strip of
width $1$ at $\pi/4$ to $\mathcal S$, meeting it in a parallelogram of area
$1/\sin(\pi/4)=\sqrt2$, so $|S|\le2\sqrt2$. The second handedness changes the geometry,
and the following is what the constant $2\sqrt2-1$ measures.

\begin{lemma}[Cap lemma]\label{lem:cap}
Let $T$ be a unit square whose diagonals are horizontal and vertical, and $\mathcal S$ a
horizontal strip of width $1$. Then $|T\cap\mathcal S|\le\sqrt2-\tfrac12$, with equality
when the strip is centred on $T$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$T$ has vertical extent $\sqrt2>1$, and its horizontal cross-section at height $y$ from
the centre has length $\sqrt2-2|y|$ for $|y|\le\sqrt2/2$. The cap above height $h$ has
area $\int_h^{\sqrt2/2}(\sqrt2-2y)\,dy=\tfrac12-\sqrt2\,h+h^2$, decreasing in $h$ on
$[0,\sqrt2/2]$, so a strip of width $1$ removes least when centred, at $h=\tfrac12$:
\[
  |T\cap\mathcal S|\ \le\ 1-2\bigl(\tfrac12-\tfrac{\sqrt2}2+\tfrac14\bigr)=\sqrt2-\tfrac12 . \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

All four arms of the two $\pi/4$ hallways are strips of width $1$ with normal
$(1,1)/\sqrt2$ or $(-1,1)/\sqrt2$. Two arms from different hallways with perpendicular
normals meet in exactly such a square, so each perpendicular pair contributes at most
$\sqrt2-\tfrac12$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:cap}, and
\[
  2\bigl(\sqrt2-\tfrac12\bigr)=2\sqrt2-1 .
\]
This is not merely numerology: the maximising configuration found for \eqref{eq:three}
splits into exactly two connected components, each of area $0.914213562=\sqrt2-\tfrac12$,
one for each perpendicular pair, with the two parallel pairs empty. The corners sit at
heights $0.995169$ and $0.004831$, symmetric about the midline.

The conjecture below reduces to a one-dimensional problem. Let $\mu=(1,1)/\sqrt2$,
$\nu=(-1,1)/\sqrt2$ and use the orthonormal coordinates $u=\langle p,\mu\rangle$,
$v=\langle p,\nu\rangle$; write $a=\langle c,\mu\rangle$, $a'=\langle c,\nu\rangle$,
$b=\langle d,\mu\rangle$, $b'=\langle d,\nu\rangle$.

\begin{lemma}[Rotated coordinates]\label{lem:uv}
In these coordinates both hallways are axis-aligned and
\[
  L_{\pi/4}(c)\cap R_{\pi/4}(d)
  =\bigl([b-1,a+1]\times[b'-1,a'+1]\bigr)\setminus
   \bigl(\{u<a,\ v<a'\}\cup\{u>b,\ v>b'\}\bigr),
\]
a rectangle less two opposite quadrants, while $\mathcal S=\{0\le u+v\le\sqrt2\}$.
Consequently, with $s=u+v$ and $t=u-v$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xsec}
  \bigl|\mathcal S\cap L_{\pi/4}(c)\cap R_{\pi/4}(d)\bigr|
  =\tfrac12\int_0^{\sqrt2}\ell(s)\,ds ,
\end{equation}
where $\ell(s)$ is the length of
$\bigl[\max(p,r)-2,\ \min(q,w)+2\bigr]\setminus\bigl((p,q)\cup(r,w)\bigr)$ with
$p=s-2a'$, $q=2a-s$, $r=2b-s$, $w=s-2b'$. In particular $\ell$ is piecewise linear in
$s$, and the two removed intervals are nonempty exactly for $s<a+a'$ and $s>b+b'$
respectively.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$L_{\pi/4}(c)=\{u\le a+1,v\le a'+1\}\setminus\{u<a,v<a'\}$ and
$R_{\pi/4}(d)=\{u\ge b-1,v\ge b'-1\}\setminus\{u>b,v>b'\}$ directly from the definitions,
since reflection carries $\mu,\nu$ to $-\nu,-\mu$. The cross-section of the rectangle at
level $s$ is $[\max(p,r)-2,\min(q,w)+2]$ in $t$, the two quadrants cut out $(p,q)$ and
$(r,w)$, and $du\,dv=\tfrac12\,ds\,dt$.
\end{proof}

Formula \eqref{eq:xsec} agrees with direct polygon computation to $2.5\cdot10^{-11}$ on
test placements. The crude bound $\ell\le4$ recovers Hammersley: it gives
$\tfrac12\cdot4\sqrt2=2\sqrt2$. The conjectured value is attained, and can be computed
exactly.

\begin{proposition}[The extremal configuration]\label{prop:extremal}
At $a=a'=b=b'=\sqrt2/4$, that is with both corners at the same point at mid-corridor
height, $\ell(s)=4-4\bigl|s-\tfrac{\sqrt2}2\bigr|$ and \eqref{eq:xsec} equals exactly
$2\sqrt2-1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put $\delta=s-\sqrt2/2$. Then $p=w=\delta$ and $q=r=-\delta$, so the outer interval is
$[|\delta|-2,\,2-|\delta|]$, of length $4-2|\delta|$. Exactly one of $(p,q)=(\delta,-\delta)$
and $(r,w)=(-\delta,\delta)$ is nonempty, according to the sign of $\delta$, and it has
length $2|\delta|$ and lies inside the outer interval because $|\delta|\le\sqrt2/2<1$.
Hence $\ell=4-4|\delta|$ and
$\tfrac12\int_0^{\sqrt2}\bigl(4-4|\delta|\bigr)ds=\tfrac12\bigl(4\sqrt2-4\cdot\tfrac12\bigr)
=2\sqrt2-1$.
\end{proof}

A search of $600$ multistarts over $[-3,3]^4$ in exact piecewise-linear arithmetic returns
$1.828427124746$, agreeing with $2\sqrt2-1$ to $2.7\cdot10^{-15}$, and no configuration
exceeding it was found.

The supremum in \eqref{eq:three} can be evaluated in closed form on most of the parameter
space, and this gives the first upper bound specific to the ambidextrous problem. The key
is that the set left after the two quadrants are removed is confined to two short end
pieces, whose lengths are controlled by two gauge-invariant combinations of $c$ and $d$.

\begin{lemma}[Four pieces]\label{lem:fourpiece}
With the notation of Lemma~\ref{lem:uv}, set
\[
  m_1=\langle c,\nu\rangle+\langle d,\mu\rangle=a'+b,\qquad
  m_2=\langle c,\mu\rangle+\langle d,\nu\rangle=a+b',
\]
\[
  \dmu=\langle d-c,\mu\rangle=b-a,\qquad \dnu=\langle d-c,\nu\rangle=b'-a' .
\]
All four are unchanged by the translation
$(a,a',b,b')\mapsto(a+\varepsilon,a'-\varepsilon,b+\varepsilon,b'-\varepsilon)$, and for every $s$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fourpiece}
  \ell(s)\ \le\ \bigl(2-2|s-m_1|\bigr)^+ +\bigl(2-2|s-m_2|\bigr)^+
            +\min\bigl(2(s-m_1)^+,2\dmu^+\bigr)+\min\bigl(2(s-m_2)^+,2\dnu^+\bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $I=[\max(p,r)-2,\min(q,w)+2]$. Since $I\setminus(p,q)=\{x\in I:x\le p\}\cup\{x\in
I:x\ge q\}$ and likewise for $(r,w)$, intersecting the two gives
\[
  I\setminus\bigl((p,q)\cup(r,w)\bigr)\ =\ \bigl(A\cup B\cup C\cup D\bigr)\cap I,
\]
\[
  A=(-\infty,\min(p,r)],\quad B=[q,r],\quad C=[w,p],\quad D=[\max(q,w),\infty).
\]
Now $|A\cap I|\le\min(p,r)-\max(p,r)+2=(2-|p-r|)^+$ and $p-r=2s-2(a'+b)$, giving the first
term; $|D\cap I|\le\min(q,w)+2-\max(q,w)=(2-|q-w|)^+$ and $q-w=2(a+b')-2s$, giving the
second. For the cross piece $C=[w,p]$: it is empty unless $p>w$, i.e.\ $\dnu>0$, and
$C\setminus A\subseteq[\min(p,r),p]$, so $|C\setminus A|\le\min\bigl((p-r)^+,(p-w)^+\bigr)
=\min\bigl(2(s-m_1)^+,2\dnu^+\bigr)$. Symmetrically $B$ is empty unless $\dmu>0$ and
$|B\setminus D|\le\min\bigl((w-q)^+,(r-q)^+\bigr)=\min\bigl(2(s-m_2)^+,2\dmu^+\bigr)$.
Adding the four bounds can only over-count overlaps.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The profile integral]\label{prop:gfun}
Let $g(x)=\int_0^{\sqrt2}\bigl(2-2|s-x|\bigr)^+ds$. Then $g(\sqrt2-x)=g(x)$, $g$ vanishes
off $(-1,\sqrt2+1)$, on $[\sqrt2-1,1]$ it equals $2\sqrt2-x^2-(\sqrt2-x)^2$, and
\[
  \max_{x\in\R}g(x)\ =\ g\bigl(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigr)\ =\ 2\sqrt2-1 .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The substitution $s\mapsto\sqrt2-s$ gives the symmetry. Differentiating under the integral,
$g'(x)=2\bigl|[0,\sqrt2]\cap(x-1,x)\bigr|-2\bigl|[0,\sqrt2]\cap(x,x+1)\bigr|$, which is
$\le0$ for $x\ge\sqrt2/2$ because the second window then contains at least as much of
$[0,\sqrt2]$ as the first; with the symmetry, $g$ increases up to $\sqrt2/2$ and decreases
after. For $\sqrt2-1\le x\le1$ the integrand is nonnegative throughout $[0,\sqrt2]$, so
$g(x)=2\sqrt2-2\int_0^{\sqrt2}|s-x|\,ds=2\sqrt2-x^2-(\sqrt2-x)^2$, and at $x=\sqrt2/2$ this
is $2\sqrt2-\tfrac12-\tfrac12$.
\end{proof}

Combining \eqref{eq:fourpiece} with $\ell\ge0$ and integrating over the band gives a bound
in closed form, with no computation of any kind.

\begin{theorem}[The sharp constant, off the cross region]\label{thm:upper}
For all $c,d$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:closedform}
  \bigl|\mathcal C_2\cap L_{\pi/4}(c)\cap R_{\pi/4}(d)\bigr|\ \le\
  \tfrac12\bigl[g(m_1)+g(m_2)\bigr]+\sqrt2\bigl[\min(1,\dmu^+)+\min(1,\dnu^+)\bigr].
\end{equation}
In particular, if $\langle d-c,\mu\rangle\le0$ and $\langle d-c,\nu\rangle\le0$ then
\[
  \bigl|\mathcal C_2\cap L_{\pi/4}(c)\cap R_{\pi/4}(d)\bigr|\ \le\ 2\sqrt2-1
  \ =\ 1.8284271\ldots,
\]
with equality exactly when $m_1=m_2=1/\sqrt2$ and $\dmu=\dnu=0$. Consequently every
ambidextrous moving sofa whose two corner positions at angle $\pi/4$ satisfy those two
inequalities has area at most $2\sqrt2-1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Integrate \eqref{eq:fourpiece} over $s\in[0,\sqrt2]$ and halve, as in \eqref{eq:xsec}. The
first two terms give $\tfrac12 g(m_1)+\tfrac12 g(m_2)$ by definition of $g$; the third is
at most $\tfrac12\int_0^{\sqrt2}2\min(1,\dmu^+)\,ds=\sqrt2\min(1,\dmu^+)$ and likewise the
fourth. If $\dmu\le0$ and $\dnu\le0$ the cross terms are absent and
Proposition~\ref{prop:gfun} bounds the rest by $\tfrac12\cdot2(2\sqrt2-1)$. Equality forces
both $g$'s to be maximal, hence $m_1=m_2=1/\sqrt2$; the configuration
$a=a'=b=b'=\sqrt2/4$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:extremal} realises it, and
\eqref{eq:closedform} is therefore sharp.
\end{proof}

The bound \eqref{eq:closedform} was tested against the objective computed independently
from the definition at $4\cdot10^4$ random parameter triples over scales from $0.1$ to
$160$: the minimum slack is $-1.0\cdot10^{-14}$, i.e.\ zero to rounding, so the bound is
both correct and attained.

When $\dmu>0$ or $\dnu>0$ the cross terms in \eqref{eq:closedform} are genuinely present
and the estimate exceeds $2\sqrt2-1$. The next three lemmas remove the sign hypothesis.
Throughout, the reflection across the diagonal $u=v$ preserves the band and exchanges
$(a,a')$ with $(a',a)$ and $(b,b')$ with $(b',b)$, hence $\dmu\leftrightarrow\dnu$ and
$m_1\leftrightarrow m_2$; so assume without loss of generality
$t:=\dnu=\max(\dmu,\dnu)>0$.

\begin{lemma}[Wide offsets]\label{lem:wide}
If $t\ge1$ then
$\bigl|\mathcal C_2\cap L_{\pi/4}(c)\cap R_{\pi/4}(d)\bigr|\le\sqrt2\,(2-t)^+\le\sqrt2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$w-p=(s-2b')-(s-2a')=-2t$ identically in $s$, so
$\ell(s)\le|I|\le4+w-p=4-2t$ for every $s$, and the band has length $\sqrt2$:
$\tfrac12\int_0^{\sqrt2}\ell\le\tfrac12\sqrt2\,(4-2t)^+$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Both offsets positive]\label{lem:bothpos}
If $0<\dmu\le\dnu=t\le1$ then $\ell(s)\le(2-2|s-m_1|)^++(2-2|s-m_2|)^+$ for every $s$, so
the area is at most $\tfrac12[g(m_1)+g(m_2)]\le2\sqrt2-1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write, as in Lemma~\ref{lem:fourpiece}, the kept set as $(A\cup B\cup C\cup D)\cap I$
with $A=(-\infty,\min(p,r)]$, $B=[q,r]$, $C=[w,p]$, $D=[\max(q,w),\infty)$, and note
$m_1\le m_2$ since $\dmu\le\dnu$. For $s\le m_1$ we have $p\le r$ and $w\le q$, so
$C\subseteq A$ and $B\subseteq D$, and
$\ell\le|A\cap I|+|D\cap I|\le(2-2(m_1-s))^++(2-2(m_2-s))^+$ exactly as in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:upper}; symmetrically for $s\ge m_2$. On the middle window $m_1<s<m_2$:
$A=(-\infty,r]$, $D=[q,\infty)$, and since $\dmu>0$ gives $q\le r$,
\[
  A\cap D=[q,r]=B,\qquad A\cap C\cap D=[q,r]\cap[w,p]=[q,r]=B,
\]
the latter because $w\le q$ and $r\le p$ on the window. In the inclusion--exclusion for
$|A\cup C\cup D|$ the terms $-|A\cap D|$ and $+|A\cap C\cap D|$ therefore cancel, leaving
\[
  \ell=|A\cap I|+|D\cap I|+\bigl[\,|C\cap I|-|A\cap C\cap I|-|C\cap D\cap I|\,\bigr].
\]
Since $t\le1$, $C=[w,p]\subseteq[p-2,w+2]=I$, so $|C\cap I|=2t$; and
$A\cap C=[w,r]$, $C\cap D=[q,p]$ are subsets of $C$, of lengths $2(n_2-s)$ and $2(s-n_1)$
with $n_1=a+a'$, $n_2=b+b'$, both nonnegative on the window. The bracket equals
$2t-2(n_2-n_1)=2t-2(\dmu+\dnu)=-2\dmu<0$, and the first two terms are the two tents.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Mixed offsets, pointwise]\label{lem:mixed}
If $\dmu\le0<\dnu=t\le1$, write $u=-\dmu$, so $m_2-m_1=t+u$. Then for every $s$
\[
  \ell(s)\ \le\ (2-2|s-m_1|)^++(2-2|s-m_2|)^+
          +\min\bigl(2(s-m_1)^+,\ 2(m_2-s)^+,\ 2\min(t,u)\bigr).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Outside the middle window the absorption of the previous proof applies verbatim (here
$B=\emptyset$). On the window, $A\cap D=[q,r]=\emptyset$ since $\dmu<0$, and
\[
  \ell=|A\cap I|+|D\cap I|+c(s),\qquad
  c(s)=|C\cap I|-|A\cap C\cap I|-|C\cap D\cap I|=2t-2(n_2-s)^+-2(s-n_1)^+ ,
\]
by the same computations ($C\subseteq I$ from $t\le1$; $A\cap C=[w,r]$ when $s\le n_2$
and empty otherwise, likewise $C\cap D$). Now $n_2=m_1+t$ and $n_1=m_2-t$ give
$c\le2t-2(n_2-s)=2(s-m_1)$ and $c\le2(m_2-s)$; and
$(n_2-s)^++(s-n_1)^+\ge(n_2-n_1)^+=(t-u)^+$ gives $c\le2t-2(t-u)^+=2\min(t,u)$. The
first two terms are at most the two tents, as before.
\end{proof}

Integrating Lemma~\ref{lem:mixed} over the band reduces everything to one two-variable
inequality about the profile $g$.

\begin{lemma}[The profile inequality]\label{lem:profineq}
For $m_1\le m_2$ set $D=m_2-m_1$, $\tau^\ast=\min(1,D/2)$ and
\[
  E(m_1,m_2)=\int_0^{\sqrt2}\min\bigl(2(s-m_1)^+,\ 2(m_2-s)^+,\ 2\tau^\ast\bigr)\,ds .
\]
Then $g(m_1)+g(m_2)+E(m_1,m_2)\le2\bigl(2\sqrt2-1\bigr)$, with equality exactly at
$m_1=m_2=1/\sqrt2$.
\end{lemma}

Before the four regions, the statement simplifies. Write
$C(x)=\int_0^{\sqrt2}\min(|s-x|,1)\,ds$. The identity $\max(0,y)=y+\max(0,-y)$ gives
$(2-2|s-x|)^+=2-2\min(|s-x|,1)$ pointwise, hence $g(x)=2\sqrt2-2C(x)$; and
$E=2\int_0^{\sqrt2}F$ with
$F(s)=\min\bigl((s-m_1)^+,(m_2-s)^+,\tau^\ast\bigr)$. Substituting, the inequality
$g(m_1)+g(m_2)+E\le2(2\sqrt2-1)$ is \emph{equivalent} to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lemEred}
  \int_0^{\sqrt2}\Bigl[\min(|s-m_1|,1)+\min(|s-m_2|,1)-F(s)\Bigr]ds\ \ge\ 1 .
\end{equation}
Neither $g$ nor $E$ appears. Since $\int_0^{\sqrt2}\min(|s-x|,1)ds\ge\tfrac12$ for every
$x$, which is the pairing argument of the formalisation section, the two positive terms
already contribute $1$, and \eqref{eq:lemEred} says exactly that $\int F$ does not exceed
the excess of those two terms over $\tfrac12$ each. On the central square that excess is
$x_1^2+x_2^2$ with $x_i=m_i-\sqrt2/2$, and $\int F\le D^2/4=(x_2-x_1)^2/4$, so region~(i)
reduces to $(x_2-x_1)^2\le4(x_1^2+x_2^2)$, which is $(x_1+x_2)^2\ge0$ together with
$(x_2-x_1)^2\le2(x_1^2+x_2^2)$.

\begin{remark}[A tempting simplification that is false]\label{rem:lemEfalse}
$F\le\min\bigl(\min(|s-m_1|,1),\min(|s-m_2|,1),\tau^\ast\bigr)=:M$ pointwise, so replacing
$F$ by $M$ in \eqref{eq:lemEred} would give a symmetric statement with no one-sided parts.
It is false: on a $241\times241$ grid of $[-1.6,3.2]^2$ the left side with $M$ has minimum
$0.8751$, at $(m_1,m_2)=(0.520,0.880)$. The loss is at $m_1=m_2$, where $F\equiv0$ but
$M=\min(|s-m_1|,1)$ does not vanish. The one-sided form is not a convenience.
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}
Write $C(x)=\int_0^{\sqrt2}\min(|s-x|,1)\,ds$ and $e(x)=C(x)-\tfrac12$, which is
nonnegative by the pairing argument. By \eqref{eq:lemEred} the claim is
$\int_0^{\sqrt2}F\le e(m_1)+e(m_2)$. Set $D=m_2-m_1\ge0$ and $u_i=m_i-\sqrt2/2$.

\emph{(L1) $\int F\le D^2/4$.} $F$ is supported on $[m_1,m_2]$ and dominated by
$\min(s-m_1,m_2-s)$, a triangle of base $D$ and height $D/2$, of area $D^2/4$; when
$\tau^\ast=1<D/2$ the profile is instead a trapezoid of area $D-1$, and $D-1\le D^2/4$
because $(D-2)^2\ge0$. Clipping the domain to $[0,\sqrt2]$ only decreases the integral.

\emph{(L2) $\int F\le\min(C(m_1),C(m_2))$.} Pointwise $F\le\tau^\ast\le1$ and
$F\le(s-m_1)^+\le|s-m_1|$, so $F\le\min(|s-m_1|,1)$; likewise for $m_2$.

\emph{(L3) $C(x)<1$ forces $|x-\sqrt2/2|\le1$, and (L4) on that range
$e(x)\ge(x-\sqrt2/2)^2/2$.} Both are finite piecewise-polynomial verifications: with no
clipping the pairing identity gives $e(x)=\int_0^{\sqrt2/2}2\operatorname{dist}
\bigl(x,[s,s+\sqrt2/2]\bigr)ds$, equal to $u^2$ for $|u|\le1-\sqrt2/2$, and clipping
contributes the remaining two polynomial pieces.

\emph{Assembly.} If $\max(C(m_1),C(m_2))\ge1$ then by (L2)
\[
  \int F\ \le\ \min(C_1,C_2)\ =\ C_1+C_2-\max(C_1,C_2)\ \le\ C_1+C_2-1\ =\ e_1+e_2 .
\]
Otherwise both $C(m_i)<1$, so (L3) gives $|u_i|\le1$ and (L4) gives
$e(m_i)\ge u_i^2/2$; then
\[
  e_1+e_2\ \ge\ \tfrac12\bigl(u_1^2+u_2^2\bigr)\ \ge\ \tfrac14(u_2-u_1)^2\ =\ \tfrac{D^2}4
  \ \ge\ \int F
\]
by (L1), the middle step being $(u_1+u_2)^2\ge0$. Equality throughout requires
$u_1=u_2=0$, that is $m_1=m_2=1/\sqrt2$.
\end{proof}

The case split is on a comparison of real numbers, not on a box: no covering, and no
interval arithmetic, appears in the proof. An earlier version certified this lemma by an
adaptive covering of $28\,512$ boxes in ball arithmetic. Numerically the assembled
inequality has minimum slack $6.6\cdot10^{-7}$ over $6\cdot10^4$ sampled pairs, attained
at $m_1=m_2=\sqrt2/2$, which is the extremal placement of
Proposition~\ref{prop:extremal}.

\begin{theorem}[The unconditional bound]\label{thm:uncond}
Every ambidextrous moving sofa satisfies
\[
  |S|\ \le\ 2\sqrt2-1\ =\ 1.8284271\ldots
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:three} it suffices to bound the two-hallway area for every placement. If
$\dmu\le0$ and $\dnu\le0$ this is Theorem~\ref{thm:upper}. Otherwise, WLOG
$t=\dnu=\max>0$. If $t\ge1$, Lemma~\ref{lem:wide} gives $\sqrt2<2\sqrt2-1$. If $t<1$ and
$\dmu>0$, Lemma~\ref{lem:bothpos} and Proposition~\ref{prop:gfun} give $2\sqrt2-1$. If
$t<1$ and $\dmu\le0$, integrate Lemma~\ref{lem:mixed} over the band: the tents give
$\tfrac12[g(m_1)+g(m_2)]$ and the flat-top term gives $\tfrac12$ of an integral that is
monotone in its height, so with $\min(t,u)\le\min(1,D/2)=\tau^\ast$ it is at most
$\tfrac12E(m_1,m_2)$; Lemma~\ref{lem:profineq} finishes.
\end{proof}

Since Romik's sofa is ambidextrous, Theorem~\ref{thm:uncond} brackets the ambidextrous
supremum:
\[
  1.6449552\ldots\ =\ A_R^\ast\ \le\ A_{\mathrm{ambi}}\ \le\
  2\sqrt2-1=1.8284271\ldots,
\]
a factor of $1.1115$. The previously available bound was $2.2195\ldots$: an ambidextrous
sofa is in particular a one-corner sofa, so it inherits whatever the one-corner problem
gives, and since \cite{Bae24} that is Gerver's area itself. Before \cite{Bae24} the
inherited value was the $2.37$ of \cite{KR18}.

Improving on the inherited bound is a question Kallus and Romik put in print. Their
concluding section observes that any one-corner upper bound applies to the ambidextrous
variant, calls the gap between the bounds an appealing opportunity, and asks for their
techniques to be extended to the ambidextrous setting so as to obtain better upper bounds
on the ambidextrous moving sofa constant \cite[\S6]{KR18}. Theorem~\ref{thm:uncond}
answers that question, though by evaluating a small relaxation in closed form rather than
by extending their branch and bound. As of the most recent survey-level treatment
\cite[Problem 6.63]{AE25}, which records the lower bound $1.6449\ldots$ and states that
whether it is sharp remains open, no upper bound specific to the problem had been given.

The constant $2\sqrt2-1$ cannot be improved by adding angles to this pair: it is attained,
by Proposition~\ref{prop:extremal}, so it is exactly the value of the two-hallway
relaxation. The route to a smaller constant is more constraints, not a better estimate of
these.


The construction is not new, and its lineage should be stated. Relaxing a sofa to the
intersection of finitely many placed hallways is Hammersley's idea \cite{Ham68} and is the
framework of Kallus and Romik \cite{KR18}; the worked example following their Proposition~4
evaluates the one-angle case in closed form, finds the optimum at $\alpha=\pi/4$, and
recovers exactly Hammersley's $2\sqrt2$, which those authors describe as Hammersley's proof
rewritten in their notation. What follows is the two-hallway ambidextrous analogue of that
example. The novelty claimed here is the resulting bound, not the method that produces it:
we know of no closed-form evaluation of a relaxation with more than one hallway, in either
handedness, and Romik has described the multi-hallway maximum as very hard to obtain by
hand, which is why \cite{KR18} passes to interval-arithmetic branch and bound.

Four of the six constraints of Proposition~\ref{prop:relax} also force this value.

\begin{proposition}[Four constraints suffice, and they recover Hammersley]\label{prop:ham}
Write $L_\theta$, $R_\theta$ for a left- and a right-turning hallway at angle $\theta$,
with free placements. Then
\[
  \sup\bigl|L_0\cap L_{\pi/4}\cap L_{\pi/2}\bigr|=2\sqrt2,\qquad
  \sup\bigl|L_0\cap R_0\cap L_{\pi/4}\cap R_{\pi/4}\bigr|\ \ge\ 2\sqrt2-1 ,
\]
the first being Hammersley's classical bound for the one-corner problem.
\end{proposition}

The first equality is a regression check rather than a new result: it says the construction
reproduces the known elementary bound $2\sqrt2$ for one corner, from three left-turning
hallways.

That $2\sqrt2-1$ is Hammersley's constant less exactly $1$ is structural, not a
coincidence, and Lemma~\ref{lem:cap} says where the $1$ goes. In Hammersley's
configuration the strip meets the $\pi/4$-rotated corridor in two unit squares set on
their diagonals, of total area $2\sqrt2$. Adding the oppositely-handed hallway at the same
angle cuts a quadrant from each square, and Lemma~\ref{lem:cap} evaluates what survives in
one of them as $\sqrt2-\tfrac12$. Two of those give $2\sqrt2-1$: each square loses exactly
$\tfrac12$.
Since dropping constraints only enlarges the supremum, the four-constraint value already
bounds the six-constraint one, so a proof of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:four}
  \bigl|L_0\cap R_0\cap L_{\pi/4}\cap R_{\pi/4}\bigr|\ \le\ 2\sqrt2-1
  \qquad\text{for all placements}
\end{equation}
gives $A_{\mathrm{ambi}}\le2\sqrt2-1$ outright, with no branch and bound and no appeal to
the remaining angles. That is what Theorem~\ref{thm:upper} and
Lemmas~\ref{lem:wide}--\ref{lem:profineq} establish: \eqref{eq:four} \emph{holds}, since
the corridor pair $L_0\cap R_0$ is the strip whose rotated coordinates give the band, and
the four sign cases of the offsets exhaust the placements. Theorem~\ref{thm:uncond} is its
restatement for sofas. Two structurally different placements attain the value: one splits
the region into two congruent pieces of area $\sqrt2-\tfrac12$, one leaves it connected.
So $2\sqrt2-1$ \emph{is} the supremum at those angles, attained by
Proposition~\ref{prop:extremal} and bounded above by Theorem~\ref{thm:uncond}, with no
interval arithmetic anywhere in the two-hallway argument.

The construction was validated before use: intersecting along $\Sig$'s own corner path
must give an area decreasing to $|\Sig|$, and it does, through
$2.050458,\,2.000690,\,1.991986,\,1.990120,\,1.645249$ at $3,9,33,129,513$ angles per side
for the two-family intersection. Two errors were caught by that check, one in the side of
the inner corner quadrant and one in the identification of the mirrored family, before any
bound was computed.

\section{Relation to prior work}\label{sec:prior}

The one-corner problem was settled by Baek \cite{Bae24}, who proved Gerver's sofa optimal.
Our reading of that paper, for the purpose of separating what is his from what is not:

\begin{itemize}
\item The \emph{arm lengths} $f,g$ are his (Definition 6.2.1), defined through the contact
points $A$ and $C$; our $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ are these shifted by the corridor width.
\item His injectivity condition (Definition 6.1.2) has three parts. Part (1) requires the
surface measure $\sigma_K$ to be absolutely continuous on $[0,\pi/2)$ and on $(\pi/2,\pi]$,
so an atom is permitted only at $\theta=\pi/2$; part (3) is
$x_K'(t)\cdot u_t<0$ and $x_K'(t)\cdot v_t>0$ on $(0,\pi/2)$.
\item Our (RC) shares part (1) verbatim and \emph{adds} a bound on the density, while
dropping part (3) entirely. No curvature bound appears in \cite{Bae24}; its only mentions
of curvature are the standard identification of the surface-measure density with the
radius of curvature, which we also use. The bound itself is classical, however: it is the
sliding-ball condition of Remark~\ref{rem:rcclassical}, so (RC) is a known condition put to
a new use rather than a new condition.
\item His concavity argument attaches Mamikon regions to the overestimating region so that
the total is linear in the cap; ours subtracts them from the cap. He works on a space of
triples $(K,B,D)$ of convex bodies; we work with the single support function $H$ on
$[0,\pi]$. Whether the two bookkeepings are equivalent we do not determine.
\item The ambidextrous problem is not treated in \cite{Bae24}, and we have found no upper
bound for it in the literature.
\end{itemize}

The constants $a_1,\beta,f_1$ and the ODE families are Romik's \cite{Rom18}; the cubics of
Section~\ref{sec:cubics} are the standard ones and we claim no priority for them. This
audit was made against \cite{Bae24} and the Kallus--Romik line \cite{KR18} only, not
against the wider literature.

\section{The main theorem}\label{sec:main}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:final}
Let $\mathcal D$ be the set of support functions $H$ on $[0,\pi]$ of admissible caps such
that
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $H(0)=H(\pi/2)=1$ \emph{(the gauge: horizontal translation, unit corridor)};
\item[(b)] \emph{(RC)} $\sigma_K$ is absolutely continuous on $[0,\pi/2)$ and $(\pi/2,\pi]$
with density at most $1$, the only atom being at $\theta=\pi/2$; equivalently, on $(0,\pi)$
$0\le(H+H'')_{\mathrm{ac}}\le1$ with no atom, together with $H'(0)=\tfrac12$ and
$H'(\pi)=-\tfrac12$, the last two being the vanishing of the junction atoms of the
$\rho$-extension (Lemma~\ref{lem:bdry});
\item[(c)] $c_y(t)=(H(t)-1)\sin t+(H(t+\pi/2)-1)\cos t\le\tfrac12$ for $t\in[0,\pi/2]$
\emph{(note $\le$, not $<$: this gives $|U\cap\rho U|=0$, which is all an area bound needs)};
\item[(d)] $\alpha_1<0$ exactly on $[0,\beta)$ and $\alpha_2>0$ exactly on
$[0,\pi/2-\beta)$.
\end{enumerate}
Then $\mathcal D$ is convex, $\Sig\in\mathcal D$, and
\[
  |T|\ \le\ A_R^\ast
\]
for every ambidextrous moving sofa $T$ whose cap data lies in $\mathcal D$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each of (a)--(d) is a family of affine inequalities or equalities in $H$, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:corner} and \eqref{eq:arm1}--\eqref{eq:sig}, so $\mathcal D$ is convex.
$\Sig$ satisfies (b) by Proposition~\ref{prop:rcsigma}, (c) with margin
$\tfrac12-M=0.1121\ldots$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, and (d) by definition of $\beta$.

By (b) and Theorems~\ref{thm:rc} and \ref{thm:cross} the combined sweep is injective, so
$V=|N|$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:convex}, whose proof also supplies the covering and
containment that Proposition~\ref{prop:V} assumes. By (c) and Lemma~\ref{lem:ceiling},
$U\subseteq\{y\le\tfrac12\}$ and $\rho U\subseteq\{y\ge\tfrac12\}$, so
$|U\cap\rho U|=0$; with $C_2$ $\rho$-invariant this gives
$|T|\le|C_2|-|N|-|\rho N|=|C_2|-2|N|=Q$. (Corollary~\ref{cor:sep} states the strict form
$M<\tfrac12$, which is what $\Sig$ satisfies; the null-set form used here is what the
hypothesis $\le$ delivers.) By (d) and
Theorem~\ref{thm:concave}, $\delta^2Q\le0$ on $\mathcal D$; by Theorem~\ref{thm:el},
$\delta Q(\Sig)=0$. A concave function with a critical point on a convex set attains its
maximum there, so $Q(H)\le Q(\Sig)$, and $Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:QA}.
Combining, $|T|\le Q(H)\le A_R^\ast$.
\end{proof}

\section{The second variation}\label{sec:secvar}

The bound above needs $\delta^2Q\le0$ on the cells the segment meets. This section
establishes that, with the best constant, and on a family of cells rather than one.

Two routes to the constant are recorded and only the second is load-bearing. The first
splits $[0,\pi/2]$ into $[0,\beta]$ and $[\beta,\pi/2]$, applies a separate Poincaré
inequality on each, and discards the $E_2$ term, giving the weak constants of
Remark~\ref{thm:concave}. Treating each half as a \emph{single} weighted eigenvalue
problem removes both losses; not splitting at all is better still and gives the exact
value.

\begin{proposition}[An elementary constant, no computer]\label{prop:elem}
On the cell $\mathcal C$, for every $\eta$ with $\eta(0)=\eta(\pi/2)=0$,
\[
  \tfrac12\,\delta^2Q[\eta]\ \le\ -\tfrac1{12}\,\|\eta\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)} ,
\]
by elementary inequalities alone: no transfer matrix, no oscillation argument, and no
ball arithmetic anywhere in the proof.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Run the decoupling of Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp} at $\lambda=\tfrac9{20}$,
$\kappa=\tfrac14$, so $r=\tfrac9{11}$ and $q=5$, and bound the two halves by hand,
using only $\beta\le\tfrac3{10}$ and $\tfrac\pi2\le1.5708$.

\emph{Left half.} $w_L\ge1-\kappa=\tfrac34$ and $m_L\le2+r$, and the
Dirichlet--Dirichlet Poincar\'e inequality on $[0,\tfrac\pi2]$ gives
$\int p'^2\ge4\int p^2$, so
\[
  \int w_Lp'^2-\int m_Lp^2\ \ge\ \bigl[3-2-\tfrac9{11}\bigr]\int p^2
  \ =\ \tfrac2{11}\int p^2 .
\]

\emph{Right half.} Write $c=\tfrac\pi2-\beta$ and $s=\lambda-q\beta^2/2$. Three
elementary steps. The heavy mass sits on $[0,\beta)$, next to the Dirichlet end, so
$q\int_0^\beta\eta^2\le q\tfrac{\beta^2}2\int_0^\beta\eta'^2\le\tfrac9{40}
\int_0^{c}\eta'^2$ by the half-Poincar\'e inequality
$\eta(t)^2\le t\int_0^t\eta'^2$; this absorbs the mass into the weight surplus and
leaves $s=\tfrac9{20}-\tfrac9{40}=\tfrac9{40}$. The surplus converts to mass through
the cut at $c$: by the same half-Poincar\'e inequality and
$\eta(t)^2\le2\eta(c)^2+2\beta\int_c^{\pi/2}\eta'^2$ for $t\ge c$,
\[
  \int_0^{\pi/2}\eta^2\ \le\ \Bigl(\tfrac{c^2}2+2\beta c\Bigr)\int_0^{c}\eta'^2
  +2\beta^2\int_c^{\pi/2}\eta'^2\ \le\ \tfrac{11}5\int_0^{c}\eta'^2
  +\tfrac9{50}\int_c^{\pi/2}\eta'^2 ,
\]
so $\int_0^{c}\eta'^2\ge\tfrac5{11}\int\eta^2-\tfrac9{110}\int_c^{\pi/2}\eta'^2$.
With the Dirichlet--Neumann Poincar\'e inequality $\int\eta'^2\ge\int\eta^2$ on
$[0,\tfrac\pi2]$,
\[
  \int w_R\eta'^2-\int m_R\eta^2\ \ge\
  \Bigl(1-\tfrac9{40}\cdot\tfrac9{110}\Bigr)\int\eta'^2
  -\Bigl(1-\tfrac9{40}\cdot\tfrac5{11}\Bigr)\int\eta^2
  \ \ge\ \tfrac{369}{4400}\int\eta^2 .
\]
Since $\tfrac{369}{4400}>\tfrac1{12}$ and $\tfrac2{11}>\tfrac1{12}$, the two halves give
the claim. Every constant is rational and every inequality is Cauchy--Schwarz, a
Poincar\'e inequality on an interval, or arithmetic.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[A rational certificate for the decoupled half]\label{rem:barrier}
The gap between $\tfrac1{12}$ and the decoupled truth is closed almost entirely by a
certificate that is still exact arithmetic. In Prüfer form the half-problem
$-(w\eta')'-m\eta=c\eta$ becomes $\theta'=\cos^2\theta/w+(m+c)\sin^2\theta$ with
$\theta(0)=0$, and $c_1>c$ exactly when $\theta$ stays below $\pi/2$; a piecewise-linear
$B$ with $B(0)=0$ and $B'\ge$ the right side dominates $\theta$, so a barrier reaching
less than $\pi/2$ is a proof and every check is an inequality between rationals. The
identity
\[
  \frac{\cos^2\theta}{w}+(m+c)\sin^2\theta
  \ =\ \frac1w+\Bigl(m+c-\frac1w\Bigr)\sin^2\theta
\]
is what makes it work: the coefficient is positive on every piece, so an upper bound on
$\sin^2\theta$ gives one on the whole, which is exactly what an upper barrier supplies.
Bounding $\cos^2\le1$ instead is fatal, since the slope then never decays and the barrier
crosses $\pi/2$ prematurely. With $\sin$ bounded above by $x-x^3/6+x^5/120$, every
constant rounded in the safe direction, and slopes rounded upward to denominator $10^7$,
$1200$ segments give
\[
  c_1\ >\ \tfrac{37}{100}
\]
for the right half. The left half is Dirichlet--Dirichlet, so the phase runs to $\pi$,
and there the method has a genuine weakness: once an upper barrier passes $\pi/2$ it knows
only $\sin^2\le1$, because an upper bound on $\theta$ is a \emph{lower} bound on $\sin$
beyond the maximum. At the note's $(\lambda,\kappa)=(\tfrac9{20},\tfrac14)$ that made the
left half, not the right, the limit. Raising $\kappa$ shrinks the left weight until the
left half is slack; scanning both halves, $(\lambda,\kappa)=(\tfrac{11}{20},\tfrac15)$
gives
\[
  \tfrac12\,\delta^2Q[\eta]\ \le\ -\tfrac9{20}\,\|\eta\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)}
\]
on the cell, by exact rational arithmetic: $5.4$ times the $\tfrac1{12}$ of
Proposition~\ref{prop:elem} and $61.6\%$ of the sharp $c^\ast$. The true decoupled values
there are $c_R=0.4748$ and $c_L=2.4414$, so the certificate captures $95\%$ of what is
available and the right half binds. Both negative controls behave: the right half at
$c=1$ crosses $\pi/2$ and the left at $c=3$ crosses $\pi$ (\verb|ambi_barrier.py|). A
two-sided barrier, tracking a lower phase as well as an upper one, does \emph{not} remove
the limitation, and the reason is worth recording. It does recover information past
$\pi/2$: with a bracket $[L,B]$ the bound $\sin^2\le1$ is replaced by
$\max(\sin^2L,\sin^2B)$, which the trace shows falling back to $0.55$ and then $0.26$ once
the bracket clears the maximum. But beyond $\pi/2$ the upper barrier's slope must be
driven by $\sin^2 L$, the value at the \emph{lower} endpoint, because $\sin$ decreases
there; the lagging endpoint therefore sets the pace and the bracket widens. At
$(\tfrac9{20},\tfrac14)$ the left half still fails at $c=1$, the barrier reaching $\pi$ at
$t=1.5624$ against $T=1.5708$, and refining the mesh to $20\,000$ segments does not close
that gap, so the loss is structural rather than a discretisation artefact. At
$(\tfrac7{10},\tfrac25)$ it fails at every $c$ tested. Reaching $\tfrac23$ by a barrier
would need a genuinely different device, not a finer one. Both the monotone bound and the comparison
principle that turns a barrier into a bound on the phase are machine-checked
(\verb|prufer_rhs_eq|, \verb|prufer_rhs_le|, \verb|sin_sq_le_of_le|,
\verb|coef_pos_right|, \verb|coef_pos_left|, \verb|prufer_comparison|,
\verb|barrier_certifies|). The comparison is not an ODE-theoretic fact requiring a
Lipschitz constant or a Grönwall bound: it is the fencing lemma of the mean value theory,
whose hypothesis is checked only where the two functions touch, and at a touching point
the barrier's own strictness gives the conclusion.
\end{remark}

The constant is $11\%$ of the sharp $c^\ast$, and that is the price of dispensing with
the transfer matrices: Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp} reaches $\tfrac23$ and
Theorem~\ref{thm:system} the sharp $\tfrac{73}{100}$, both through certified
evaluations. What Proposition~\ref{prop:elem} buys is qualitative: strict concavity of
the form on the cell, hence the local maximality statements at $\Sigma$, no longer
depends on any computation.

\begin{theorem}[Concavity, sharp order]\label{thm:sharp}
On the cell $\mathcal C$, for every $\eta$ with $\eta(0)=\eta(\pi/2)=0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sharp}
  \tfrac12\,\delta^2Q[\eta]\ \le\ -\tfrac23\,\|\eta\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)} .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $\lambda\in(0,1)$ and $\kappa\in(0,1)$, and put $r=\lambda/(1-\lambda)$ and
$q=1+1/\kappa$. Two elementary inequalities dispose of the two coupling terms of
\eqref{eq:Q2}. Since $(1+r)(1-\lambda)=1$,
\[
  (1+r)a^2+2ab+(1-\lambda)b^2=\bigl(\sqrt{1+r}\,a+\sqrt{1-\lambda}\,b\bigr)^2\ \ge\ 0,
  \qquad\text{that is}\qquad -(a+b)^2\ \le\ -\lambda\,b^2+r\,a^2 ,
\]
applied with $a=\eta(t)$, $b=\eta'(t+\pi/2)$ on $E_2=[0,\pi/2-\beta)$; it buys gradient
weight $\lambda$ on $[\pi/2,\pi-\beta]$ at the price of mass $r$ on $[0,\pi/2-\beta)$. And
$(x-y)^2\le(1+1/\kappa)x^2+(1+\kappa)y^2$ with $x=\eta(t+\pi/2)$, $y=\eta'(t)$ on
$E_1=[0,\beta)$ pays mass $q$ on $[\pi/2,\pi/2+\beta]$ and gradient weight $1+\kappa$ on
$[0,\beta)$. Substituting $s=t+\pi/2$ in both, the two halves decouple:
\[
  \tfrac12\delta^2Q[\eta]\ \le\ \int_0^{\pi/2}\bigl(m_L\eta^2-w_L\eta'^2\bigr)
    +\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi}\bigl(m_R\eta^2-w_R\eta'^2\bigr)\ =:\ B[\eta] ,
\]
\[
  \begin{array}{ll}
   w_L=1-\kappa\ \text{on }[0,\beta),\ 2\ \text{after}, &
   m_L=2+r\ \text{on }[0,\tfrac\pi2-\beta),\ 2\ \text{after},\\[2pt]
   w_R=1+\lambda\ \text{on }[\tfrac\pi2,\pi-\beta),\ 1\ \text{after}, &
   m_R=1+q\ \text{on }[\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2+\beta),\ 1\ \text{after}.
  \end{array}
\]
Each half is a Rayleigh quotient, so $B[\eta]\le-\min(c_L,c_R)\,\|\eta\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)}$,
where $c_L$ and $c_R$ are the first eigenvalues of
\[
  -(w_L\eta')'-m_L\eta=c\,\eta \ \text{ on }(0,\tfrac\pi2),\quad \eta(0)=\eta(\tfrac\pi2)=0;
  \qquad
  -(w_R\eta')'-m_R\eta=c\,\eta \ \text{ on }(\tfrac\pi2,\pi),\quad
  \eta(\tfrac\pi2)=0,\ \eta'(\pi)=0 .
\]
The Dirichlet conditions at $0$ and $\pi/2$ are the gauge, not a restriction, and nothing
pins $\eta(\pi)$.

Both coefficients are piecewise constant with three pieces, so the solution of the initial
value problem $\eta=0$, $w\eta'=1$ at the left endpoint is a product of three transfer
matrices acting on $(\eta,\,w\eta')^{\!\top}$,
\[
  T(\ell,w,m;c)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos k\ell & (wk)^{-1}\sin k\ell\\[2pt]
   -wk\sin k\ell & \cos k\ell\end{pmatrix},\qquad k=\sqrt{(m+c)/w}
\]
(hyperbolic when $m+c<0$). Write $\Phi_L(c)$ for the resulting $\eta(\pi/2)$ and
$\Phi_R(c)$ for $(w\eta')(\pi)$. Its zeros are exactly the eigenvalues, and by Sturm
oscillation $\Phi>0$ on $(-\infty,c_1)$, so a single sign evaluation bounds $c_1$ from
\emph{below}, which is the direction a certificate needs and the one a Rayleigh--Ritz or
finite-element computation cannot supply.

Positivity alone does not locate $c$, because $\Phi>0$ again on $(c_2,c_3)$; one also
needs $c<c_2$. That comes from min--max, crudely. Since $\int w\eta'^2\ge
w_{\min}\int\eta'^2$ and $-\int m\eta^2\ge-m_{\max}\int\eta^2$, the form dominates the
constant-coefficient one, so $c_k\ge w_{\min}\lambda_k-m_{\max}$ with $\lambda_k$ the
eigenvalues on an interval of length $\pi/2$, namely $(2k)^2$ for Dirichlet--Dirichlet and
$(2k-1)^2$ for Dirichlet--Neumann. Hence
\[
  c_2^L\ \ge\ \tfrac34\cdot16-\tfrac{63}{13}=7.153\ldots,
  \qquad
  c_2^R\ \ge\ 1\cdot 9-6=3 ,
\]
both above $\tfrac23$. (At $k=1$ the same bound gives $-1.85$ and $-5$, useless; the
second eigenvalue is far enough away to be reached crudely while the first is not, which
is exactly why the transfer matrix is needed.)

It remains to evaluate two signs. At $\kappa=\tfrac14$, $\lambda=\tfrac{37}{50}$ and
$c=\tfrac23$ every weight and mass is rational ($w_L=\tfrac34,2,2$ and
$m_L=\tfrac{63}{13},\tfrac{63}{13},2$; $w_R=\tfrac{87}{50},\tfrac{87}{50},1$ and
$m_R=6,1,1$), so each $k^2=(m+c)/w$ is exact, and the only inexact inputs are the three
lengths $\beta,\ \tfrac\pi2-2\beta,\ \beta$ and the trigonometric functions of $k\ell$. In
ball arithmetic at $300$ bits, with $\beta=\arctan(u/2)$ enclosed from $u^3+3u=2$ by exact
rational bisection,
\[
  \Phi_L(\tfrac23)\in0.0046739486452026048\ldots\pm6.4\cdot10^{-73},\qquad
  \Phi_R(\tfrac23)\in0.0026447861413929500\ldots\pm8.3\cdot10^{-73},
\]
both certified strictly positive. Hence $c_L,c_R>\tfrac23$, which is \eqref{eq:sharp}.
\end{proof}

Optimising the two parameters gives $\min(c_L,c_R)=0.674944$ at
$(\kappa,\lambda)=(0.26,0.74)$. Since $\|\eta\|_{L^2(0,\pi/2)}\le\|\eta\|_{L^2(0,\pi)}$,
\eqref{eq:sharp} also improves the $[0,\pi/2]$ constant of Theorem~\ref{thm:concave} from
$0.371$ to $\tfrac23$.

The splitting into two scalar problems is itself avoidable, and avoiding it gives the
sharp constant.

\begin{theorem}[The sharp constant]\label{thm:system}
The best constant in \eqref{eq:sharp} is the first eigenvalue $c^\ast$ of an explicit
$2\times2$ Sturm--Liouville system, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sharpsys}
  \tfrac12\,\delta^2Q[\eta]\ \le\ -\tfrac{73}{100}\,\|\eta\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)},
  \qquad c^\ast=0.7309566\ldots
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The two halves of $[0,\pi]$ are the same interval re-indexed. Put $p(t)=\eta(t)$ and
$q(t)=\eta(t+\pi/2)$ for $t\in[0,\pi/2]$; then \eqref{eq:Q2} reads
$\tfrac12\delta^2Q=\int_0^{\pi/2}L$ with
\[
  L=2p^2-2p'^2+q^2-q'^2-\mathbf 1_{E_2}(p+q')^2+\mathbf 1_{E_1}(q-p')^2 ,
\]
and every boundary condition is forced: $p(0)=p(\pi/2)=0$ is the gauge, $q(0)=\eta(\pi/2)=0$
is the same gauge seen from the other half, and $q(\pi/2)=\eta(\pi)$ is free. Nothing has
been discarded, so the first eigenvalue of this system is the best constant. Writing
$M:=-L$ as $a p'^2+b q'^2+g p^2+d q^2+2e\,pq'+2f\,qp'$,
\[
  \begin{array}{r|cccccc}
    \text{piece} & a & b & g & d & e & f\\\hline
    {[0,\beta)} & 1 & 2 & -1 & -2 & 1 & 1\\
    {[\beta,\tfrac\pi2-\beta)} & 2 & 2 & -1 & -1 & 1 & 0\\
    {[\tfrac\pi2-\beta,\tfrac\pi2)} & 2 & 1 & -2 & -1 & 0 & 0
  \end{array}
\]
On $[0,\beta)$, exactly where the obstruction sits, $e=f=1$ and
$2pq'+2qp'=2(pq)'$ is a total derivative: the $E_1$ obstruction and the $E_2$ resource
cancel there up to the single boundary value $2p(\beta)q(\beta)$. The coupling is not
pointwise, which is why every pointwise splitting loses and this one does not.

Eliminating $p',q'$ in favour of the momenta $P=ap'+fq$ and $Q=bq'+ep$, the
Euler--Lagrange system is first order in $(p,P,q,Q)$ with piecewise constant matrix
\[
  A(c)=\begin{pmatrix}
    0 & 1/a & -f/a & 0\\
    g-c-e^2/b & 0 & 0 & e/b\\
    -e/b & 0 & 0 & 1/b\\
    0 & f/a & d-c-f^2/a & 0
  \end{pmatrix},
\]
so the transfer across a piece of length $\ell$ is $\exp\bigl(A(c)\ell\bigr)$. The left
conditions $p(0)=q(0)=0$ leave a two-dimensional solution space; propagating a basis
through the three pieces and imposing $p(\pi/2)=0$ and $Q(\pi/2)=0$ gives a $2\times2$
determinant $\Phi(c)$ whose zeros are exactly the eigenvalues. A sign change brackets
$c^\ast\in(0.7309,0.7310)$.

For the certificate the oscillation argument is unavailable, since for a system the count is
a Maslov index rather than a zero count, and it is not needed. Two facts compose:
$c^\ast\ge\tfrac23$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp}, and $\Phi$ has no zero on
$[\tfrac23,\tfrac{73}{100}]$. The latter is checked by covering that interval with $60$
balls and verifying in $400$-bit ball arithmetic that no enclosure of $\Phi$ contains $0$,
with $\beta$ enclosed from $u^3+3u=2$ by exact rational bisection. Hence no eigenvalue lies
at or below $\tfrac{73}{100}$.
\end{proof}

The certified $\tfrac{73}{100}$ is $99.9\%$ of $c^\ast$; the decoupled $\tfrac23$ is
$91.2\%$. Both are lower bounds, and no step in either is floating point. The covering
route is also self-contained: with the crude spectral bound $-4$ of
Theorem~\ref{thm:diag}(c), covering $[-4,\tfrac{73}{100}]$ at the fixed lengths
$(\beta,\tfrac\pi2-2\beta,\beta)$ with $3074$ boxes certifies $c_1>\tfrac{73}{100}$
directly, without the decoupled chain or the oscillation argument; the negative control
at $\tfrac{74}{100}$ is refused with the failing box at $c\in[0.7310,0.7313]$, which lies
just above $c^\ast=0.7309566\ldots$ and within $4\cdot10^{-5}$ of it: the enclosure of
$\Phi$ there is still wide enough to contain $0$, which is how a target past $c^\ast$
fails. The decoupled chain remains the hand-checkable route to
$\tfrac23$. Nothing in the
argument stops at $\tfrac{73}{100}$: certifying a target $c$ needs the enclosures to
separate from $0$ on every subinterval of $[\tfrac23,c]$, and those must shrink as $c$
approaches the root, so the number of balls grows: $60,60,800,12000$ for targets
$0.73,0.7305,0.7309,0.73095$. The last is $99.9991\%$ of $c^\ast$. We quote
$\tfrac{73}{100}$ because a reader can check it with $60$ balls; the limit is arithmetic
cost, not a gap.

\begin{remark}[{The $[\pi/2,\pi]$ eigenvalue in closed form}]\label{rem:SL}
When only the weight is raised and the mass is left at $1$ -- that is, $w=1+\lambda_J$ on
$[\pi/2,\pi-\beta]$ and $1$ on $[\pi-\beta,\pi]$ -- the transfer product collapses to a
transcendental equation for the first eigenvalue $\Lambda(\lambda_J)$ of
$-(w\eta')'=\Lambda\eta$ with $\eta(\pi/2)=0$, $\eta'(\pi)=0$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:SL}
  \sqrt{w_1}\,\cot\bigl(L_1\sqrt{\Lambda/w_1}\bigr)=\tan\bigl(L_2\sqrt\Lambda\bigr),
  \qquad w_1=1+\lambda_J,\quad L_1=\tfrac\pi2-\beta,\quad L_2=\beta .
\end{equation}
At $\lambda_J=0$ this reads $\cot(L_1\sqrt\Lambda)=\cot(\pi/2-L_2\sqrt\Lambda)$, so
$\sqrt\Lambda\,(L_1+L_2)=\pi/2$; since $L_1+L_2=\pi/2$ \emph{exactly}, $\Lambda(0)=1$
exactly. That is the marginal case $P_3=1$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:concave}, and it explains
why no gain is available on $[\pi/2,\pi]$ until the weight is raised. For $\lambda_J>0$ put
$G(w_1)=\sqrt{w_1}\cot(L_1/\sqrt{w_1})-\tan\beta$. Then $G(1)=0$, and $G$ increases: the
prefactor increases, while $L_1/\sqrt{w_1}$ decreases and $\cot$ decreases on $(0,\pi)$.
So $G>0$ for $w_1>1$. The left side of \eqref{eq:SL} decreases in $\Lambda$ and the right
side increases, so the root moves right and $\Lambda(\lambda_J)>1$ for every $\lambda_J>0$:
\[
  \begin{array}{r|cccccc}
    \lambda_J & 0 & 0.05 & 0.10 & 0.16483 & 0.25 & 0.35\\\hline
    \Lambda & 1 & 1.049467 & 1.098883 & 1.162878 & 1.246819 & 1.345183
  \end{array}
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp} runs this mechanism on both halves at once, raising the mass as
well as the weight.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Concavity on every ordered anchored cell}

The concavity results so far concern single cells: $\Sig$'s own
(Theorems~\ref{thm:concave}, \ref{thm:sharp}, \ref{thm:system}) and the eleven measured
entries of Proposition~\ref{prop:beyond} below. This subsection proves concavity for the
whole ordered anchored family at once. In the variables $p(t)=\eta(t)$,
$q(t)=\eta(t+\pi/2)$ on $[0,\pi/2]$, with $p(0)=p(\pi/2)=q(0)=0$ and $q(\pi/2)$ free, the
cell form is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cellform}
  B_{E_1,E_2}[\eta]=\int_0^{\pi/2}\bigl[2p^2-2p'^2+q^2-q'^2\bigr]
  -\int_{E_2}(p+q')^2+\int_{E_1}(q-p')^2 ,
\end{equation}
where $E_1=\{\alpha_1<0\}$ and $E_2=\{\alpha_2>0\}$.

\begin{lemma}[Monotonicity in the sign sets]\label{lem:mono}
If $E_1\subseteq E_2$ then
$B_{E_1,E_2}[\eta]=B_{E_2,E_2}[\eta]-\int_{E_2\setminus E_1}(q-p')^2\le B_{E_2,E_2}[\eta]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The two forms differ only in the set carrying the $(q-p')^2$ term.
\end{proof}

So every cell with $E_1\subseteq E_2$ and $E_2$ \emph{anchored} is dominated by a member
of the one-parameter diagonal family
$D_\tau:=B_{[0,\tau),[0,\tau)}$, and it suffices to treat that family.

\begin{theorem}[Diagonal concavity]\label{thm:diag}
$D_\tau[\eta]\le0$ for every $\tau\in[0,\pi/2]$ and every admissible $\eta$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On $[0,\tau)$ the integrand of \eqref{eq:cellform} combines, by the algebra of
Theorem~\ref{thm:system}, into $(p^2-p'^2)+2(q^2-q'^2)-2(pq)'$, so with $p(0)q(0)=0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Dtau}
  D_\tau=\int_0^\tau\bigl[(p^2-p'^2)+2(q^2-q'^2)\bigr]-2p(\tau)q(\tau)
  +\int_\tau^{\pi/2}\bigl[2(p^2-p'^2)+(q^2-q'^2)\bigr].
\end{equation}
Three overlapping ranges of $\tau$ are treated by three arguments.

\emph{Range (a): $\tau\le1/\sqrt3$.} Write \eqref{eq:Dtau} as
$B_{\mathrm{bare}}+\int_0^\tau[(p'^2-p^2)+(q^2-q'^2)]-2p(\tau)q(\tau)$ with
$B_{\mathrm{bare}}:=\int_0^{\pi/2}[2(p^2-p'^2)+(q^2-q'^2)]$. The Poincar\'e constants for
the boundary conditions are $4$ (Dirichlet--Dirichlet, for $p$) and $1$
(Dirichlet--Neumann, for $q$), so $B_{\mathrm{bare}}\le-\tfrac32\int_0^{\pi/2}p'^2$. The
added terms are bounded by $\int_0^\tau p'^2\le\int_0^{\pi/2}p'^2$, by
$\int_0^\tau q^2\le\tau^2\int_0^\tau q'^2$ (from $q(t)^2\le t\int_0^tq'^2$), and, using
Young's inequality at weight $s=1/(2\tau)$ together with $p(\tau)^2\le\tau\int_0^\tau p'^2$
and $q(\tau)^2\le\tau\int_0^\tau q'^2$,
\[
  2|p(\tau)q(\tau)|\ \le\ \frac{p(\tau)^2}{2\tau}+2\tau\,q(\tau)^2
  \ \le\ \tfrac12\int_0^{\pi/2}p'^2+2\tau^2\int_0^\tau q'^2 .
\]
Collecting, $D_\tau\le\bigl(-\tfrac32+1+\tfrac12\bigr)\int_0^{\pi/2}p'^2
+(3\tau^2-1)\int_0^\tau q'^2\le0$ for $\tau^2\le\tfrac13$.

\emph{Range (b): $\sigma:=\pi/2-\tau\le\tfrac1{18}$.} The obstruction here is that the
bound must degenerate: $D_{\pi/2}$ annihilates the mode $(p,q)=(0,\sin t)$, so no estimate
with a uniform negative constant exists near $\tau=\pi/2$, and the argument must see the
mode. Expand $q=a\varphi_1+r$ in the Dirichlet--Neumann eigenbasis
$\varphi_k=\sin((2k-1)t)$ of $[0,\pi/2]$, with $r\perp\varphi_1$ in both inner products,
and set $D:=\int q'^2-\int q^2\ge0$. From the eigenvalues $(2k-1)^2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spec}
  \int r'^2\le\tfrac98D,\qquad \|r\|_{L^2}^2\le\tfrac{D}8,\qquad
  r(t)^2\le t\!\int_0^t\!r'^2\le\tfrac{9\pi}{16}D .
\end{equation}
Since $\varphi_1''=-\varphi_1$ and $r(0)=0$, integration by parts gives
$\int_0^\tau(\varphi_1r-\varphi_1'r')=-\varphi_1'(\tau)r(\tau)=-\sin\sigma\,r(\tau)$, and
$\int_0^\tau(\varphi_1^2-\varphi_1'^2)=-\tfrac12\sin2\sigma$. Hence, using
\eqref{eq:spec} and Young's inequality $2XY\le3X^2+\tfrac13Y^2$ on the middle term,
\[
  2\!\int_0^\tau\!(q^2-q'^2)+\int_\tau^{\pi/2}\!(q^2-q'^2)
  \ \le\ -\Bigl(\tfrac78-\tfrac{3\pi}{16}\Bigr)D-a^2\sin\sigma(\cos\sigma-3\sin\sigma)
  \ \le\ -\tfrac27D-\tfrac45\sigma a^2 ,
\]
the last step using $\tfrac78-\tfrac{3\pi}{16}\ge\tfrac27$ and
$\sin\sigma(\cos\sigma-3\sin\sigma)\ge\sigma(1-3\sigma-\sigma^2)\ge\tfrac45\sigma$ for
$\sigma\le\tfrac1{18}$. For $p$, with $P^2:=\int_\tau^{\pi/2}p'^2$ and the Dirichlet
Poincar\'e constant $(2\sigma/\pi)^2\le\tfrac1{400}$ on $[\tau,\pi/2]$,
\[
  \int_0^\tau(p^2-p'^2)+2\!\int_\tau^{\pi/2}\!(p^2-p'^2)
  \ \le\ -\tfrac34\int_0^{\pi/2}p'^2-\bigl(1-\tfrac1{400}\bigr)P^2\ \le\ -\tfrac{174}{100}P^2 .
\]
The boundary term couples the pieces through $p(\tau)^2\le\sigma P^2$ and
$q(\tau)=a\cos\sigma+r(\tau)$:
\[
  2|p(\tau)||a|\cos\sigma\le\tfrac54P^2+\tfrac45\sigma\cos^2\!\sigma\,a^2,\qquad
  2|p(\tau)||r(\tau)|\le\tfrac9{25}P^2+\tfrac{25}9\,\sigma\cdot\tfrac{9\pi}{16}D ,
\]
and $\tfrac{25}9\cdot\tfrac{9\pi}{16}\,\sigma=\tfrac{25\pi}{16}\sigma\le\tfrac{25\pi}{288}
\le\tfrac27$ for $\sigma\le\tfrac1{18}$. Summing the three displays, the coefficients of
$a^2$, $D$ and $P^2$ are respectively $\tfrac45\sigma(\cos^2\sigma-1)\le0$, at most
$-\tfrac27+\tfrac{25\pi}{288}\le0$, and $-\tfrac{174}{100}+\tfrac54+\tfrac9{25}
=-\tfrac{13}{100}<0$. Hence $D_\tau\le0$.

\emph{Range (c): $\tau\in[0.55,\,1.5153]$.} The form $D_\tau$ is the $2\times2$
Sturm--Liouville system of Theorem~\ref{thm:system} with pieces of lengths
$\tau,\ \pi/2-\tau$ and coefficient rows $(1,2,-1,-2,1,1)$, $(2,1,-2,-1,0,0)$, so its
eigenvalues are the zeros of the same shooting determinant $\Phi(c;\tau)$. Two facts
combine. First, the spectrum is bounded below by $-4$: pointwise,
$2pq'\ge-(2p^2+\tfrac12q'^2)$ and $2qp'\ge-(2q^2+\tfrac12p'^2)$ absorb the cross terms of
$-D_\tau$ leaving non-negative derivative coefficients, so
$-D_\tau[\eta]\ge-\int(3p^2+4q^2)\ge-4\|\eta\|^2$. Second, $\Phi(c;\tau)\ne0$ for every
$(c,\tau)\in[-4,0]\times[0.55,1.5153]$: the rectangle is covered adaptively by $305$
boxes on which the ball-arithmetic enclosure of $\Phi$ excludes zero, with the lengths
carried as balls containing $[\tau_{\mathrm{lo}},\tau_{\mathrm{hi}}]$. Hence no eigenvalue
lies in $[-4,0]$ and none lies below $-4$, so the first eigenvalue is positive and
$D_\tau\le0$ (indeed $<0$) on this range.

The ranges $[0,1/\sqrt3]$, $[0.55,1.5153]$ and $[\pi/2-\tfrac1{18},\pi/2]$ overlap and
cover $[0,\pi/2]$.
\end{proof}

The covering was validated by negative controls: enlarged $c$-ranges crossing the
measured first eigenvalue are refused, with the failing boxes localising the eigenvalue
curve to $(\tau,c)=(0.785,0.377)$ for the mirror family below and $(1.514,0.041)$ for the
diagonal, matching the measured values.

\begin{corollary}[Ordered anchored concavity]\label{cor:anchored}
$\delta^2Q\le0$ on every cell whose sign sets satisfy
$\{\alpha_1<0\}\subseteq\{\alpha_2>0\}$ with $\{\alpha_2>0\}=[0,\tau)$ anchored. The set
$\{\alpha_1<0\}$ need not be an interval.
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[Uniform constant on the mirror family]\label{thm:mirroruniform}
For every $\tau\in[0,\pi/4]$ the mirror-anchored cell $(\tau,\pi/2-\tau)$ satisfies
\[
  \tfrac12\,\delta^2Q[\eta]\ \le\ -\tfrac3{10}\,\|\eta\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)} .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The cell form is the system of Theorem~\ref{thm:system} with lengths
$\tau,\ \pi/2-2\tau,\ \tau$; $\Sig$'s cell is $\tau=\beta$. The lower bound $-4$ on the
spectrum holds as in range (c), and the covering of
$[-4,\tfrac3{10}]\times[0,0.7854]$ by $249$ boxes shows $\Phi\ne0$ there; since
$0.7854>\pi/4$, every $\tau\in[0,\pi/4]$ is included. (For $\tau$ slightly past $\pi/4$
the middle length crosses zero and the transfer formula is an analytic continuation of no
further relevance; the enclosure covers every real $\tau$ in each ball, in particular the
range where the formula is the shooting determinant of the cell.) Hence the first
eigenvalue exceeds $\tfrac3{10}$ on the whole family. Measured, it decreases from $0.875$
at $\tau=0$ to $0.370$ at $\tau=\pi/4$, with $0.7331$ at $\Sig$'s cell.
\end{proof}


\section{Stability and uniqueness}\label{sec:stab}

Because $Q$ is exactly quadratic on the cell and $\delta Q(\Sig)=0$, the same ingredients
give more than a bound.

\begin{theorem}[Stability]\label{thm:stab}
For every ambidextrous moving sofa $T$ whose cap data $H=H_\Sig+\eta$ lies in $\mathcal D$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stab}
  |T|\ \le\ A_R^\ast-\tfrac{73}{100}\,\|\eta\|_{L^2(0,\pi)}^2 .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On the cell $\mathcal C$ the functional $Q$ is a quadratic polynomial in $H$, so
$Q(H_\Sig+\eta)=Q(\Sig)+\delta Q(\Sig)[\eta]+\tfrac12\delta^2Q[\eta]$ exactly. The first
variation vanishes by Theorem~\ref{thm:el} and $Q(\Sig)=A_R^\ast$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:QA}, while Theorem~\ref{thm:system} bounds the second variation. Combining
with $|T|\le Q(H)$ from Theorem~\ref{thm:final} gives \eqref{eq:stab}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Uniqueness]\label{cor:uniq}
$\Sig$ is the unique ambidextrous moving sofa of area $A_R^\ast$ whose cap data lies in
$\mathcal D$; every other one has strictly smaller area, by the amount \eqref{eq:stab}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Equality in \eqref{eq:stab} forces $\|\eta\|_{L^2(0,\pi/2)}=0$, and then $\eta\equiv0$ on
$[0,\pi/2]$; Theorem~\ref{thm:concave} shows $\delta^2Q<0$ strictly unless $\eta\equiv0$
throughout, since with $\eta\equiv0$ on $[0,\pi/2]$ the $E_2$ term of \eqref{eq:Q2}
contributes $-\int_0^{\pi/2-\beta}\eta'(t+\pi/2)^2dt<0$ unless $\eta$ is constant on
$[\pi/2,\pi]$, and $\eta(\pi/2)=0$ makes that constant zero.
\end{proof}

The domain $\mathcal D$ is cut out in part by (RC), and (RC) enters at exactly one
point: it forces the flux excess $E:=V-|N|$ to vanish, so that $Q$ is tight. Separation and
Reynolds give, with no curvature hypothesis,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:QplusE}
  |T|\ =\ |C_2|-2|N|\ =\ Q+2E,\qquad E\ge0 .
\end{equation}
It is natural to ask for a concave majorant of $E$, which would replace $\{E=0\}$ by
something larger. There is none.

\begin{proposition}[No concave majorant]\label{prop:nogo}
Let $\mathcal D'$ be convex with $\Sig$ in its algebraic interior, and let
$\widetilde E:\mathcal D'\to\mathbb R$ be concave with $\widetilde E\ge0$ and
$\widetilde E(\Sig)=0$. Then $\widetilde E\equiv0$, and hence $E\equiv0$ on $\mathcal D'$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $a,b\in\mathcal D'$ with $\Sig=\tfrac12(a+b)$. Concavity gives
$0=\widetilde E(\Sig)\ge\tfrac12\widetilde E(a)+\tfrac12\widetilde E(b)\ge0$, so
$\widetilde E(a)=\widetilde E(b)=0$. As $\Sig$ is interior, every point of $\mathcal D'$
arises this way.
\end{proof}

So any architecture with a concave, tight majorant is confined to the injectivity locus.
The hypothesis to weaken is concavity of $\widetilde E$: what the argument needs is that
$Q+2\widetilde E$ be concave, and the strict concavity of $Q$ leaves room.

\begin{theorem}[Excess budget]\label{thm:budget}
Let $\mathcal D'$ be convex with $\Sig\in\mathcal D'$ and $\theta\in[0,1]$. Suppose
$M<\tfrac12$ on $\mathcal D'$, that $\tfrac12\delta^2Q\le-c\|\eta\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)}$ there,
and that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:EB}
  E(H)\ \le\ \tfrac12\,\theta\,c\,\|H-H_\Sig\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)}\qquad\text{on }\mathcal D' .
\end{equation}
Then $|T|\le A_R^\ast-(1-\theta)\,c\,\|H-H_\Sig\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)}$ for every ambidextrous
moving sofa $T$ with cap data in $\mathcal D'$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Concavity of $Q$ with $\delta Q(\Sig)=0$ gives $Q(H)\le A_R^\ast-c\|\eta\|^2$. Adding
\eqref{eq:QplusE} and \eqref{eq:EB},
$|T|=Q+2E\le A_R^\ast-c\|\eta\|^2+\theta c\|\eta\|^2$.
\end{proof}

At $\theta=0$ this is Theorem~\ref{thm:stab}. For $\theta>0$ it admits $E>0$, so
Proposition~\ref{prop:nogo} is evaded, and the room available is proportional to $c$: the
constant $c^\ast=0.7309566$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:system} is what makes \eqref{eq:EB}
usable. Measured along $H_\Sig+\varepsilon\phi$ with $\phi$ the bump at $\theta_0=1$ of
half-width $0.45$, the least admissible $\theta$ is
\[
  \begin{array}{r|cccccc}
    \varepsilon & 0.0141 & 0.0200 & 0.0283 & 0.0400 & 0.0566 & 0.0800\\\hline
    \theta & 0.100 & 0.301 & 0.683 & 1.276 & 2.090 & 3.106
  \end{array}
\]
so $\theta<1$ holds out to $\varepsilon\approx0.031$, against $\varepsilon_c=0.006492$ for
(RC): the admissible radius grows by a factor of about $4.8$ in that direction. This is a
measurement, not a proof.

\begin{theorem}[Stability on the mirror class]\label{thm:mirrorstab}
Let $T$ be a connected ambidextrous moving sofa whose cap data $H$ satisfies the gauge,
the mirror condition \eqref{eq:mirror}, and (RC), and suppose that along the segment
$H_s=(1-s)H_\Sig+sH$ the set $\{\alpha_1(\cdot;H_s)<0\}$ is an interval $[0,\tau_1(s))$
with $\tau_1(s)\le\pi/4$ for every $s$. Then
\[
  |T|\ \le\ A_R^\ast-\tfrac3{10}\,\|H-H_\Sig\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)} ,
\]
and equality forces $H=H_\Sig$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The mirror condition is linear and holds at $H_\Sig$, so every $H_s$ satisfies it, and by
Lemma~\ref{lem:mirror} each cell met by the segment is mirror-anchored with parameter
$\tau_1(s)\le\pi/4$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:mirroruniform}, $g(s):=Q(H_s)$ has
$g''(s)\le-\tfrac35\|H-H_\Sig\|^2$ on each cell, $g$ is $C^1$ across cell boundaries as in
Proposition~\ref{prop:beyond}, $g(0)=A_R^\ast$ and $g'(0)=0$; integrating,
$Q(H)\le A_R^\ast-\tfrac3{10}\|H-H_\Sig\|^2$. Connectedness gives $M\le\tfrac12$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:ceiling}), and (RC) gives $V=|N|$, so
$|T|=|C_2|-2|N|=Q(H)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The arm sandwich]\label{lem:sandwich}
Suppose $0\le(H+H'')_{\mathrm{ac}}\le1$ on $(0,\pi)$, with atoms only at $\pi/2$. Then on
$(0,\pi/2)$, in the absolutely continuous sense,
\[
  \alpha_2\ \le\ \alpha_1'\ \le\ \alpha_2+1,
  \qquad
  -\alpha_1-1\ \le\ \alpha_2'\ \le\ -\alpha_1 .
\]
The upper bounds use convexity ($H+H''\ge0$), the lower bounds use (RC).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\alpha_1'=G'-F''$ and $-F''\in[F-1,\,F]$ by the two-sided bound on $F+F''$, so
$\alpha_1'\in[G'+F-1,\,G'+F]=[\alpha_2,\,\alpha_2+1]$. Likewise $\alpha_2'=F'+G''$ with
$G''\in[-G,\,1-G]$ gives $\alpha_2'\in[-\alpha_1-1,\,-\alpha_1]$.
\end{proof}

The pair $(\alpha_1,\alpha_2)$ therefore rotates: where $\alpha_2>0$ the first arm
strictly increases, and where $\alpha_1>0$ the second strictly decreases. This is the
differential form of the oscillator mechanism behind Theorem~\ref{thm:rc}, and it makes
the sign hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:mirrorstab} checkable from the arms alone. It
does not make it removable:

\begin{lemma}[Forced boundary derivatives]\label{lem:bdry}
Under (RC), $H'(0)=\tfrac12$ and $H'(\pi)=-\tfrac12$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$C_2$ is $\rho$-symmetric, so its support function satisfies $h(\theta)=h(-\theta)+\sin\theta$
on the whole circle; this determines $h$ on $[\pi,2\pi]$ from $H$ on $[0,\pi]$. On
$(\pi,2\pi)$, writing $\psi=2\pi-\theta$, one finds $h+h''=(H+H'')(\psi)$, so no condition
arises there. At the two junctions the extension has derivative jumps
\[
  h'(0^+)-h'(0^-)=2H'(0)-1,\qquad h'(\pi^+)-h'(\pi^-)=-2H'(\pi)-1 ,
\]
which are the atoms of the surface area measure at $\theta=0$ and $\theta=\pi$. Convexity
forces both to be non-negative; (RC) permits atoms only at $\pm\pi/2$, so both vanish.
\end{proof}

For $\Sig$ these read $0.4999999990$ and $-0.4999999987$ in the computed data, the
deviation being the finite-difference error. Lemma~\ref{lem:bdry} is what makes the class
of admissible caps rigid at the endpoints, and it is what selects a single member of the
following family.

\begin{remark}[The sign hypothesis is not removable]\label{rem:disc}
Consider $H_c(\theta)=1-c+c(\sin\theta+\cos\theta)$, the support data of the disc of
radius $1-c$ centred at $(c,c)$. It satisfies the gauge and the mirror condition
\eqref{eq:mirror} with $A=2c$, and $H_c+H_c''\equiv1-c$, so the absolutely continuous
part of (RC) holds for every $c\in(0,1)$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:bdry} only $c=\tfrac12$ is
admissible: $H_c'(0)=c$, and $c<\tfrac12$ makes the junction atoms negative, so the
$\rho$-extension is not convex at all, while $c>\tfrac12$ creates atoms at $0$ and $\pi$
that (RC) forbids. At $c=\tfrac12$ the body is the disc of radius $\tfrac12$ centred at
$(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, the incircle of the unit square, and every condition holds with
$H+H''\equiv\tfrac12$. Its arms are
\[
  \alpha_1\equiv\alpha_2\equiv-\tfrac12 ,
\]
so $\{\alpha_1<0\}=[0,\pi/2)$ while $\{\alpha_2>0\}=\emptyset$, and the ordering fails
identically. Moreover $|N|=0$ and $|C_2|=\pi/4$ (both the cap formula and a direct
polygon computation give $0.785398163$), so the maximal ambidextrous sofa with this cap
datum is the disc itself, and a disc of diameter $1$ moves freely through the corridor and
turns either corner. The datum is therefore realized by a connected ambidextrous moving
sofa. Hence the ordered anchored sign structure follows neither from the gauge, mirror,
convexity and (RC), nor from realizability, and the hypothesis of
Theorem~\ref{thm:mirrorstab} is permanent rather than provisional.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[A hypothesis of the niche formula, made explicit]\label{rem:seghyp}
The disc also isolates an assumption implicit in \eqref{eq:V}. The face-1 segment is
$s\in[\alpha_1^+,\sigma]$, so its contribution is $\tfrac12\bigl((\sigma-\alpha_1^+)^+
\bigr)^2$, and writing it as $\tfrac12(\sigma-\alpha_1)^2$ presupposes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:seghyp}
  \sigma\ \ge\ \alpha_1^+ \qquad\text{on }[0,\pi/2].
\end{equation}
For the disc $\alpha_1^+=0$ while $\sigma$ decreases to $-\tfrac12$, and the unmodified
expression returns $V=-0.1427$ against $|N|=0$, in apparent conflict with
Proposition~\ref{prop:reynolds}; with the segment truncated, $V=0=|N|$ and the conflict
disappears. For $\Sig$, \eqref{eq:seghyp} holds: $\sigma-\alpha_1=\cos t\,x'(t)$ by
Proposition~\ref{prop:sigx} and $x$ is strictly increasing by
Proposition~\ref{prop:mono}, while $\sigma\ge0$ throughout; the computed minimum of
$\sigma-\alpha_1^+$ is $+2.5\cdot10^{-7}$, attained as $t\to\pi/2$. All uses of
\eqref{eq:V} in this note are on data satisfying \eqref{eq:seghyp}.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[An explicit ball inside the domain]\label{prop:ball}
Let $r_0=\tfrac1{20}$. Every $\eta$ with $\eta(0)=\eta(\pi/2)=0$, satisfying the mirror
condition, and with $\max(\|\eta\|_\infty,\|\eta'\|_\infty,\|\eta''\|_\infty)\le r_0$,
gives $H=H_\Sig+\eta$ satisfying (RC), $M(H)<\tfrac12$, and the sign hypothesis of
Theorem~\ref{thm:mirrorstab} along the entire segment $[H_\Sig,H]$. In particular the
stability bound applies to every connected ambidextrous sofa with such cap data.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(RC): $\max(H_\Sig+H_\Sig'')_{\mathrm{ac}}=\tfrac34f_1\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2}\,
\cos(\tfrac\beta2+\tfrac\pi8)=0.8388253\ldots$ in closed form, and
$\|\eta+\eta''\|_\infty\le2r_0=0.1\le0.1611$. Ceiling: $M(\Sig)=0.38784\ldots$ in closed
form, and $|\delta c_y|\le\|\eta\|_\infty(\sin t+\cos t)\le\sqrt2\,r_0=0.0708$, so
$M(H)\le0.4586<\tfrac12$. Signs: the perturbations obey
$|\delta\alpha_i|\le\|\eta\|_\infty+\|\eta'\|_\infty\le2r_0=0.1$, and six margins of the
unperturbed arms are certified on grids of spacing $h=5\cdot10^{-4}$ with the Lipschitz
constant $L=2$ supplied by Lemma~\ref{lem:sandwich}: writing $S:=\sup(|\alpha_1|\vee
|\alpha_2|)$, the sandwich gives $\sup|\alpha_i'|\le S+1$, and the grid maximum $0.7506$
bootstraps to $S\le0.7506+(S+1)h/2$, hence $S\le0.7513$ and $L\le1.76\le2$. The certified
margins are: $\alpha_1\le-g$ on $[10^{-3},\beta-d]$ and $\alpha_1\ge g$ on
$[\beta+d,\pi/2]$; $\alpha_2\ge g$ on $[10^{-3},\pi/2-\beta-d]$ and $\alpha_2\le-g$ on
$[\pi/2-\beta+d,\pi/2]$; and $\alpha_2\ge0.5975$, $\alpha_1\ge0.5975$ on the windows
$[\beta-d,\beta+d]$, $[\pi/2-\beta-d,\pi/2-\beta+d]$ respectively, with $d=0.15$ and
$g=0.1224>2r_0$. Outside the windows the perturbed arms keep their signs. Inside
$\alpha_1$'s window, Lemma~\ref{lem:sandwich} gives
$\alpha_1'\ge\alpha_2\ge0.5975-0.1>0$ for the perturbed data, so $\alpha_1$ crosses zero
exactly once there; likewise $\alpha_2'\le-\alpha_1\le-(0.5975-0.1)<0$ in its window. So
$\{\alpha_1<0\}=[0,\tau_1)$ with $\tau_1<\beta+d\le\pi/4$ and
$\{\alpha_2>0\}=[0,\tau_2)$ with $\tau_2>\pi/2-\beta-d>\beta+d$, ordered and anchored.
Replacing $\eta$ by $s\eta$, $s\in[0,1]$, only shrinks the perturbation, so the same
holds along the segment.
\end{proof}

The grid evaluations use double precision; the margins exceed the evaluation error by
eleven orders of magnitude, and the Lipschitz constant is proved, not sampled. This
replaces, in the load-bearing direction, the numerical statement that $\mathcal D$ is a
$C^2$-neighbourhood: the domain \emph{contains} the explicit $C^2$-ball of radius
$\tfrac1{20}$ within the mirror class. The outer containment (that the radius along
$\sin(2k\theta)$ shrinks like $k^{-2}$) remains a measurement.

The hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:mirrorstab} are non-perturbative: no smallness of
$H-H_\Sig$ is assumed, and the class contains data as far from $\Sig$ as the mirror caps
with $\tau_1$ anywhere in $[0,\pi/4]$. The binding smallness constraint that remains in
$\mathcal D$ is (RC) alone.

The sign cell is the least structural part of $\mathcal D$. On a natural linear
subclass it collapses to a single scalar inequality.

\begin{lemma}[Mirror caps]\label{lem:mirror}
Suppose the cap is symmetric about some vertical line, equivalently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mirror}
  H(\theta)-H(\pi-\theta)=A\cos\theta,\qquad A:=H(0)-H(\pi) .
\end{equation}
Then $\alpha_2(\pi/2-t)=\alpha_1(t)$ for every $t\in[0,\pi/2]$. Consequently
$E_2=\tfrac\pi2-E_1$ as sets, and if $E_1=[0,\tau_1)$ then $E_2=[0,\tfrac\pi2-\tau_1)$, so
\[
  \tau_1+\tau_2=\tfrac\pi2 .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Condition \eqref{eq:mirror} says $h(u)-h(\sigma u)=\langle(A,0),u\rangle$ for the reflection
$\sigma(x,y)=(-x,y)$, that is, the cap coincides with its mirror image translated by
$(A,0)$; the axis is $x=A/2$. Replacing $\theta$ by $\pi/2+t$ in \eqref{eq:mirror} gives
$H(\pi/2-t)=H(\pi/2+t)+A\sin t$, and differentiating \eqref{eq:mirror} gives
$H'(\pi-\theta)=-H'(\theta)-A\sin\theta$. Hence
\[
  \alpha_2(\tfrac\pi2-t)=H(\tfrac\pi2-t)-1+H'(\pi-t)
  =\bigl[H(\tfrac\pi2+t)+A\sin t\bigr]-1+\bigl[-H'(t)-A\sin t\bigr]
  =H(\tfrac\pi2+t)-1-H'(t)=\alpha_1(t). \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

Condition \eqref{eq:mirror} is linear in $H$ once $A$ is treated as a free parameter, so the
mirror caps form a linear subspace and intersecting $\mathcal D$ with it preserves
convexity. Both $\Sig$ and Gerver's cap satisfy it, to $7\cdot10^{-16}$ in the computed
data. On that subclass the anchored sign pattern is fixed by the single number $\tau_1$,
and the order condition $\tau_1\le\tau_2$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:beyond} becomes
\[
  \tau_1\ \le\ \tfrac\pi4 ,
\]
which at $\Sig$ reads $\beta=0.289654\le0.785398$, a margin of $0.4957$. Lemma~\ref{lem:mirror}
is also what makes $L_1+L_2=\pi/2$ exact in \eqref{eq:SL}, hence $\Lambda(0)=1$ exactly:
the two lengths there are $\tau_2$ and $\tau_1$.

\begin{remark}[Why Gerver's cap is excluded]\label{rem:gerverout}
Gerver's cap satisfies \eqref{eq:mirror} and, by Theorem~\ref{thm:budget}, the excess
budget along the whole segment $[H_\Sig,H_{\mathrm G}]$ at $\theta=0.0072$; and (RC) fails
on that segment only at $s=0.398$. None of these is what excludes it. Along the segment
$M=\max_tc_y(t)$ crosses $\tfrac12$ at $s=0.406$, and at Gerver $M=0.6643$, so by
Theorem~\ref{thm:ceiling} any ambidextrous sofa with that corner path omits a vertical
strip of width $0.1646$ and a connected one lies entirely on one side of it, contradicting
that it meets both arms. So \emph{no connected ambidextrous moving sofa has Gerver's cap
data}: the exclusion is a property of the competitor class, not a limitation of the
curvature hypothesis. Weakening (RC) cannot reach Gerver, and should not.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Disconnected competitors]\label{rem:disconn}
Connectedness is used in exactly one place, Remark~\ref{rem:gerverout}, and it is
load-bearing there: when $M=\max_tc_y(t)$ exceeds $\tfrac12$ the two niches overlap, and
inclusion--exclusion gives $|S|=|C_2|-2|N|+|U\cap\rho U|>Q(H)$, so overlap \emph{increases}
area and cannot be assumed away on extremal grounds. A moving sofa is connected by
definition, so the hypothesis is part of the problem rather than an added assumption, but
that is a weak answer if the excluded competitors would win. They do not.

Rasterising the intersection directly, rather than through the cap and niche bookkeeping
that is exactly what fails when the niches overlap,
\[
  S=\bigcap_t\bigl[\mathrm{Hall}(t)\cap\rho\,\mathrm{Hall}(t)\bigr] ,
\]
$\Sig$ returns $1.64548$ against $A_R^\ast=1.6449552$. Refining the spatial grid from
$400^2$ to $2000^2$ and the rotation sampling from $120$ to $900$ angles moves the value by
at most $0.0015$, which is the error bar for everything below. Over admissible caps drawn
with (RC) holding by construction and the forced boundary data exact, $46$ samples reached
$M\ge\tfrac12$, and the largest area among them was $1.6032$, below $A_R^\ast$ by $0.042$,
some $28$ times the error bar. The overlap gain is real but the caps that produce it lose
more elsewhere.

So dropping connectedness does not appear to raise the supremum, and
Remark~\ref{rem:gerverout} is not smuggling in a restriction that does work. Rule~7: this
is \textsc{heuristic}, a rasterised measurement over $55$ caps, and it is not a proof that
no disconnected competitor beats $A_R^\ast$.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Beyond $\Sig$'s own cell]\label{prop:beyond}
$Q$ is $C^1$ and piecewise quadratic, so if every cell met by the segment
$[H_\Sig,H]$ has $\delta^2Q\le0$, then $Q$ is concave along that segment and
$Q(H)\le Q(\Sig)+\delta Q(\Sig)[H-H_\Sig]=A_R^\ast$. Convexity of the union of those cells
is not needed. In particular this holds whenever the segment meets only cells with
$\{\alpha_1<0\}\subseteq\{\alpha_2>0\}$ and $\{\alpha_2>0\}$ anchored, which is no
longer a measured condition: Corollary~\ref{cor:anchored} proves concavity on every such
cell. The eleven sample values
$-0.8751,-0.1404,-0.3602,-0.5397,-0.4497,-0.3431,-0.2285,-0.1074,-0.0457,-0.7876,-0.0421$
at $(\tau_1,\tau_2)=(0,\tfrac\pi2),(0.1,0.2),(0.3,0.5),(0.5,0.9),(0.8,1.2),(1.0,1.3),
(1.2,1.4),(1.4,1.5),(1.5,\tfrac\pi2),(0.2,1.5),(0.05,0.06)$, all negative, stand as a
numerical cross-check, with $\Sig$'s own cell giving $-0.7331$ at $m=128$ (converged
value $-0.7309566$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:system}).
\end{proposition}

Since $\Sig$ sits at $(\beta,\pi/2-\beta)$ with $\beta<\pi/2-\beta$, it lies in that
family, and the class of competitors covered is strictly larger than $\mathcal D$ and is
checkable from the sign pattern alone. (The restriction to \emph{anchored} intervals is
essential: Remark~\ref{rem:conc} exhibits $E_1=E_2=[0.4,1.2]$, unanchored, with
$\sup\tfrac12\delta^2Q/\|\eta\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)}=+0.4123$.)

The constant in \eqref{eq:stab} is $99.9\%$ of the sharp $c^\ast=0.7309566\ldots$.
What closed the earlier factor of $4.8$ was giving up the pointwise splitting in two
stages: Theorem~\ref{thm:concave} uses three separate Poincaré constants, of which $P_3=1$
is marginal and $P_2=1.5035$ nearly so; Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp} replaces all three by two
weighted eigenvalues that see the whole of each half at once; and
Theorem~\ref{thm:system} replaces those two by one system that sees both halves at once,
losing nothing.

A correction this forced. Earlier drafts quoted the sharp constant as $0.7323$, the P1
value at $m=256$. Rayleigh--Ritz gives an upper bound on an eigenvalue and that sequence
had not converged; it decreases through $0.731311,0.731247,0.731073$ at $m=512,1024,2048$
towards $c^\ast=0.7309566$. Every certificate in this note is a lower bound and every
finite-element number an upper bound, so nothing crossed and no conclusion moves; the
target was mis-stated by $0.2\%$.

\begin{proposition}[$H^1$ stability, measured]\label{prop:h1}
The second variation controls the derivative as well. Measured against assembled mass and
stiffness matrices, the largest $c_0$ with
$\tfrac12\delta^2Q[\eta]\le-c_0\|\eta\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)}-c_1\|\eta'\|^2_{L^2(0,\pi)}$ is
\[
  \begin{array}{r|cccc}
    c_1 & 0 & 0.1 & 0.3 & 0.5\\\hline
    m=64  & 0.7339 & 0.6132 & 0.3595 & 0.0796\\
    m=128 & 0.7331 & 0.6123 & 0.3583 & 0.0779\\
    m=256 & 0.7323 & 0.6114 & 0.3571 & 0.0760\\
    m=512 & 0.7313 & 0.6103 & 0.3556 & 0.0739
  \end{array}
\]
These are Rayleigh--Ritz values, hence upper bounds on the true constants, and every
column is still decreasing at $m=512$: the $c_1=0$ column is the one whose limit is known
independently, and it is descending towards the certified $c^\ast=0.7309566\ldots$, not
towards its own $m=256$ entry. So the table bounds the admissible constants from above and
does \emph{not} establish any of the inequalities. Only the $c_1=0$ case is proved, by
Theorem~\ref{thm:system}. Rule~7: the $c_1>0$ entries are \textsc{heuristic}, and the
frontier $c_0\approx0.731-1.31\,c_1$ read off them, vanishing near $c_1=0.56$, is a
measurement rather than a theorem.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[Why the earlier statement was wrong]\label{rem:h1wrong}
An earlier version of this proposition printed the $m=256$ row alone, asserted that the
entries were converged, and concluded
$\tfrac12\delta^2Q\le-0.357\|\eta\|^2-0.3\|\eta'\|^2$. The assertion was false in exactly
the way the paragraph above this proposition already describes for $0.7323$: a
Rayleigh--Ritz sequence that has not converged, read as if it had. The same mesh value
appears in both places, which is how the inconsistency survived. Reproducing a printed
number at the mesh it was computed on confirms its provenance, not its correctness.
\end{remark}

Two simplifications are worth recording. First, the $\sigma$ term has no singularity:
since $-2\int\eta\eta'\tan t=-\int(\eta^2)'\tan t=\int\eta^2\sec^2t$ when
$\eta(0)=\eta(\pi/2)=0$, and $\sec^2t-\tan^2t=1$,
\[
  -\int_0^{\pi/2}\bigl(\eta\tan t+\eta'\bigr)^2dt
  =\int_0^{\pi/2}\bigl(\eta^2-\eta'^2\bigr)dt ,
\]
checked to $10^{-12}$ on five test functions, so \eqref{eq:Q2} may be written with bounded
coefficients throughout. Second, the sharp $L^2$ constant is $0.7309566$, not the $0.7285$ of
an earlier draft; that figure came from a mass matrix that gave the endpoint hat at
$\theta=\pi$ the interior weight $2h/3$ instead of $h/3$.

\begin{remark}[The size of $\mathcal D$, and what Theorem~\ref{thm:final} is not]
\label{rem:notopt}
It is not a proof that $\Sig$ is optimal, and the reason is quantitative.

Measuring the radius of $\mathcal D$ along $H_\Sig+\varepsilon\sin(2k\theta)$ gives
$\varepsilon_k=0.0864,\ 0.0117,\ 0.0047,\ 0.0036,\ 0.0021$ at $k=2,4,6,8,10$, so
$\varepsilon_kk^2$ stays between $0.17$ and $0.35$: the radius scales like $k^{-2}$.
$\mathcal D$ therefore contains a ball in the $C^2$ norm and in no weaker one, which is
exactly what a bound on $H''$ should give. It is a genuine ball rather than a sliver,
of $60$ random eight-mode perturbations with coefficients of size $0.02/k^2$, $51$ land
but every member is a small $C^2$-perturbation of $\Sig$.
\textbf{Theorem~\ref{thm:final} is thus a $C^2$-local statement}, with explicit convex
hypotheses in place of a neighbourhood of unspecified size.

In every one of those probes the binding constraint was (b); condition (c) never became
active. That is a statement about the probe and not about the geometry: (c) is
\emph{independent} of (b). Adding $\delta\sin(\theta-\pi/2)$ on $[\pi/2,\pi]$ and $0$ on
$[0,\pi/2]$ leaves $H+H''$ identically unchanged, so (b) and the gauge hold exactly, while
the ceiling facet grows by $\delta$ and $c_y$ grows by $\delta\sin t\cos t$; at
$\delta=0.2243\ldots$ the maximum $M$ crosses $\tfrac12$. So (c) cannot be derived and must
remain a hypothesis.

$\mathcal D$ does not reach Gerver's cap, and by Remark~\ref{rem:sharp} injectivity
genuinely fails once the density exceeds $1$. The failure is sharp in the condition but
gentle in magnitude: comparing the flux $\tfrac12\int(\alpha_2^+)^2$ with the area actually
swept, the excess is $5.4\cdot10^{-7}$ at density $1.047$ and $7.4\cdot10^{-5}$ at $1.267$,
and Gerver's own excess is about $0.25\%$. Widening $\mathcal D$ therefore does not need a
weaker constant; it needs an upper bound on that excess which is \emph{concave} in $H$, so
that subtracting it preserves the concavity of $Q$. Truncating the sweep at
$\inf_{t'}s_{22}(t,t')$ fails because that infimum is concave in $H$ and
$\tfrac12\lambda^2$ then loses the convexity the Mamikon step supplies; the first-capture
decomposition is exact but its parameter domain stops being of the form
$0\le s\le\alpha_2$, so its area is no longer quadratic in $H$.
So the honest reading is that the one-corner architecture transfers to the ambidextrous
problem on an explicit convex $C^2$-neighbourhood of $\Sig$, and that enlarging that
neighbourhood is where the remaining difficulty sits.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[(RC) is sharp]\label{rem:sharp}
The constant $1$ in the forcing $R=1-(G+G'')$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:rc} is the corridor
width: it enters through $c(t)=(F-1)\mu_t+(G-1)\nu_t$, so
$\langle c(t),\nu_{t'}\rangle=n+G-1$ and $n''=-n+1-(G+G'')$. The argument therefore cannot
tolerate a density above $1$. Nor can the conclusion: perturbing $\Sig$ so that the maximum
density passes through $1$, the face-2 sweep acquires self-intersections exactly there. At
densities $0.838,0.921,0.976$ there are none; at $1.004,1.047,1.119$ there are
$160,1510,3086$ among $\binom{301}{2}$-many tested pairs, and a second mode gives $0$ at
$0.976$ against $200$ and $526$ at $1.045$ and $1.114$. So (b) is sharp as stated.
\end{remark}

\section{Formalisation}

Lemma~\ref{lem:ceiling}, Corollary~\ref{cor:sep} and the collapse of
\eqref{eq:ie} are machine-checked in Lean~4 without Mathlib as
\verb|niche_below_apex|, \verb|niche_disjoint| and
\verb|union_area_of_disjoint|. The identity underlying $\Sigma$'s middle-phase
equation is also formalised: writing $D$ for the formal derivative in the
half-angle, $D(Dv)=-(v+1)$ for every arc of constant term $-1$, which is
$4v''=-(v+(1,1))$ for the bracket of \eqref{eq:x6}
(\verb|Trig.sol6_bracket|, proved by \verb|rfl| and depending on no axioms), as is
the frame-speed computation behind Theorem~\ref{thm:a1beta}
(\verb|Trig.sol1_speed_mu|) and the covering criterion of
Theorem~\ref{thm:ceiling} (\verb|strip_covers_iff|).

Two steps of the unconditional bound are formalised, chosen as the ones where a slip
would be fatal and where no analysis is needed to state them. The four-piece
decomposition of Lemma~\ref{lem:fourpiece} is a fact about a linear order, so $\Z$ is a
faithful model: \verb|four_piece| states that a point avoids both removed open intervals
exactly when it lies in one of the four pieces, \verb|four_piece_no_cross| specialises to
the case where both cross pieces are empty, and \verb|cross_absorbed| is the containment
that makes Lemma~\ref{lem:bothpos} need no new estimate. The maximum of the profile
integral reduces to $2x^2+2(\sqrt2-x)^2=2+(2x-\sqrt2)^2$, an identity in
$\Z[\sqrt2]$, which is built explicitly rather than posited: a hypothesis $S^2=2$ over
$\Z$ has no witness and would make the statement vacuous, so \verb|Z2| carries the ring
structure and \verb|sqrt2_sq| exhibits the model, after which \verb|profile_identity| is
the identity and \verb|equality_case| locates the maximiser at $2x=\sqrt2$.

The profile integral itself is now verified, not merely its middle-range formula. The step
is \verb|integral_tent_le|: for every $x$,
$\int_0^{\sqrt2}(2-2|s-x|)^+ds\le2\sqrt2-1$, with \verb|integral_tent_at_mid| giving
equality at $x=\sqrt2/2$, so the pair says $2\sqrt2-1$ is the supremum. The Lean proof is
shorter than the case analysis of Proposition~\ref{prop:gfun} because it avoids it: the
identity $\max(0,y)=y+\max(0,-y)$ turns the truncation into a correction term,
\[
  (2-2|s-x|)^+=2-2|s-x|+2(|s-x|-1)^+ ,
\]
valid with no hypothesis on $s$, and since $|y|-(|y|-1)^+=\min(|y|,1)$ the bound becomes
\[
  \int_0^{\sqrt2}\min\bigl(|s-x|,1\bigr)ds\ \ge\ \tfrac12
  \qquad\text{for every }x\in\R ,
\]
which is \verb|half_le_integral_cap|. That has a one-line proof: split the band at its
midpoint and pair $s$ with $s+\sqrt2/2$. The triangle inequality gives
$|s-x|+|s+\sqrt2/2-x|\ge\sqrt2/2$, and $\min(a,1)+\min(b,1)\ge\min(a+b,1)$ with
$\sqrt2/2<1$ turns that into
$\min(|s-x|,1)+\min(|s+\sqrt2/2-x|,1)\ge\sqrt2/2$; integrating over $[0,\sqrt2/2]$ gives
$(\sqrt2/2)^2=\tfrac12$. Equality throughout at $x=\sqrt2/2$. The $-1$ in $2\sqrt2-1$ is
exactly twice this $\tfrac12$, which is the same $-1$ that separates the constant from
Hammersley's $2\sqrt2$.

\verb|lake build| is clean with no \verb|sorry| across the development, and
\verb|#print axioms| reports nothing beyond \verb|propext|, \verb|Quot.sound| and
\verb|Classical.choice|. The second variation is partially formalised. Both decoupling inequalities of
Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp} are verified outright, each as an exact square over the field
(\verb|decouple_E2|, \verb|decouple_E1|), and the assembly of
Proposition~\ref{prop:elem} from its analytic inputs to the constant $\tfrac1{12}$ is
verified as pure arithmetic (\verb|secondvar_assembly|); three of the five inputs are theorems: the absorption
inequality, the subset monotonicity and the tail cut are verified for continuously
differentiable data (\verb|habs_verified|, \verb|hsub_verified|, \verb|hcut_verified|),
all from one engine, $(f(b)-f(a))^2\le(b-a)\int_a^bf'^2$ (\verb|sq_sub_le|), proved by
monotonicity of $t\mapsto(t-a)\int_a^tf'^2-(f(t)-f(a))^2$, whose derivative is
$\int_a^t(f'(s)-f'(t))^2\,ds\ge0$; no Cauchy--Schwarz machinery is needed. The two Wirtinger
inequalities are also verified, and by a route worth recording: the classical
ground-state substitution divides by $\sin$, which vanishes at the Dirichlet end, and the
resulting integration by parts has a removable singularity that is expensive to
formalise. Shifting the Riccati supersolution avoids it entirely. The function
$v=k\cot(kt+c)$ with $c>0$ is regular on all of $[0,T]$ when $kT+c<\pi$ and satisfies
$v'+v^2+k^2=0$ exactly, so the pointwise square $(f'\sin-k f\cos)^2\ge0$ and the ordinary
fundamental theorem of calculus give
\[
  k^2\int_0^Tf^2\ \le\ \int_0^Tf'^2-v(T)f(T)^2+v(0)f(0)^2 ,
\]
with no limits taken anywhere (\verb|riccati_block|). At the Dirichlet end $f(0)=0$
absorbs the $v(0)$ term however large $\cot c$ is, and the loss $k\tan(c)\,f(T)^2$ at the
other end is controlled by the verified engine $f(T)^2\le T\int f'^2$. The result is
$\int f^2\le(1+\pi c)\int f'^2$ for the Dirichlet--Neumann problem and
$4\int f^2\le(1+2\pi c)\int f'^2$ for the Dirichlet--Dirichlet one
(\verb|wirtinger_DN|, \verb|wirtinger_DD|, the latter glued from two shifted blocks by
reflection), for every $c>0$; the elementary chain has slack $1/1886$, and $c=1/8000$
fits inside it (\verb|secondvar_assembly'|). The capstone
\verb|elementary_constant_verified| carries \emph{no analytic hypothesis}: for
continuously differentiable data with the boundary conditions and $\beta\le\tfrac3{10}$,
the decoupled forms dominate $\tfrac1{12}$ of the mass, machine-checked end to end.
The ball-arithmetic certificate of Theorem~\ref{thm:system} for the sharp
$\tfrac{73}{100}$ remains outside Lean, and its label remains \textsc{proved}, not
\textsc{verified}. The geometric reduction is verified, including the containment. The
placed region is defined (\verb|placedRegion|) and shown measurable; its fibres lie in
the four-piece union (\verb|fibre_subset_four_piece|); and, in the sign-restricted case
that $\Sig$ satisfies, the slicing identity is \emph{proved}: Fubini restricts the fibre
integral to the band because the region is empty outside it
(\verb|placedRegion_volume_eq|), each fibre is bounded by the two tents
(\verb|fibre_volume_le|), and the verified profile bound evaluates the result
(\verb|placedRegion_volume_le|). The containment is supplied by \verb|Containment.lean|:
\verb|AmbiSofa| records a sofa together with the two hallway placements it occupies at
angle $\pi/4$, which is what "lies in the hallway at every instant" means, so
\verb|sofa_subset_pair| is definitional; and \verb|toFibre_mem_placedRegion| carries the
intersection into \verb|placedRegion| by affine arithmetic, which is the content of
Lemma~\ref{lem:uv}. The hallways are \emph{defined} there by their half-plane
descriptions in the rotated frame rather than derived from a definition of rotation, so
that identification is recorded rather than re-derived; it is the one modelling step of
the chain, and it is not an estimate. Lemma~\ref{lem:profineq} is verified end to end: \verb|lemmaE_verified| carries no
hypotheses, and \verb|profile_bound_verified| is the lemma in its original $g$-form,
$g(m_1)+g(m_2)+2\int F\le2(2\sqrt2-1)$ for every $m_1\le m_2$. The analytic inputs are
discharged as follows: (L1) by \verb|L1_triangle| (extend the domain, split at the
midpoint, two ramp integrals); (L2) by pointwise domination; (L3) and (L4) by
\verb|L3_full| and \verb|L4_full|, which rest on the splitting $C=A-K$ of
\verb|Excess.lean|, the four ramp integrals \verb|ramp_up_in| through
\verb|ramp_down_le|, the zone analysis \verb|Kloss_le|, the closed forms
\verb|Cfun_right| and \verb|Cfun_far| beyond the band, and the reflection
\verb|Cfun_symm|, which delivers the left side for free. No covering, no interval
arithmetic and no numerics of any kind appear in the verified chain. Trigonometric quantities are carried as formal symbols with
the sign hypotheses $\sin t\ge0$, $\cos t\ge0$, which is all
Lemma~\ref{lem:ceiling} uses.
